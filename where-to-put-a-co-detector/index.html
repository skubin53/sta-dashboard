Where Should a Carbon Monoxide Detector Go?

Where the alarm goes, where it does not, what the test button really checks, and what to do tonight.

By Shannon Nicole, founder of Switch to America. Seven years helping women switch to a toxin-free home, and a health coach for over 20 years. Published October 24, 2026.

Where should a carbon monoxide detector go?

Quick answer: on every level of your home, and outside the rooms where people sleep.

Carbon monoxide is often written CO. The CPSC puts the rule in one line: "Install battery-operated CO alarms or CO alarms with battery backup on every level of the home and outside sleeping areas."

The CDC says the same about bedrooms. It says to put battery or battery back-up detectors "near every sleeping area in your home."

So for most homes, the first spot is the hallway outside the bedrooms. Then add one on every other floor, the basement too.

The CPSC adds three more things:

  • A battery inside. "Hard wired or plug-in CO alarms should have battery backup." That way it still works if the power goes out.
  • Linked alarms are best. "Interconnected CO alarms are best; when one sounds, they all sound."
  • The maker's manual. "CO alarms should be installed according to the manufacturer's instructions."

Sources: CPSC, Carbon Monoxide Questions and Answers (the page shows no date); CDC, Carbon Monoxide Poisoning Basics, January 12, 2026.

Can I plug my carbon monoxide detector in by the stove?

Quick answer: no. The CPSC says not in the kitchen.

So many of us pick the outlet by the stove. That is where we picture a leak starting. But the CPSC says: "CPSC does not recommend installing CO alarms in kitchens or above fuel-burning appliances."

It also says to "Avoid locations that are near heating vents or that can be covered by furniture or draperies."

How far from the furnace or gas stove should it be?

The CPSC page gives no distance in feet.

One fire department does. The City of Madison, Wisconsin fire department says to place CO alarms "At least 15 feet from gas-burning appliances (not any closer)."

That is one city's advice, not a federal rule. Your alarm's manual comes first.

Does it go high or low on the wall?

Either one. The CPSC says "CO alarms may be installed into a plug-in receptacle or high on the wall." A receptacle is a wall outlet.

The Madison fire department says why height is not a big deal: "The weight of CO is very close to that of normal atmosphere, so the mounting height does not matter."

Sources: CPSC, Carbon Monoxide Questions and Answers; City of Madison Fire Department, Where To Place Your Carbon Monoxide Alarms.

Does the test button mean my carbon monoxide detector works?

Quick answer: it means the circuits work. It does not check the sensor.

Most of us think the beep means we are safe. The CPSC says: "Using a test button tests whether the circuitry is operating correctly, not the accuracy of the sensor."

So a beep tells you the alarm can make noise. It does not tell you the sensor is still accurate.

That is why age matters. The CPSC says: "Alarms have a recommended replacement age, which can be obtained from the product literature or from the manufacturer."

The CDC gives a number. It says: "Replace your CO detector following the manufacturer's instructions or every 5 years."

Look in the papers that came with your alarm, or ask the maker. Then write the replace-by date on a strip of tape on the back.

When should I check the battery?

The CDC gives an easy reminder: "Check CO detector batteries when you change the time on your clocks each spring and fall." So when you set your clocks back this fall, check the battery too.

Keep using the test button, too, as often as your alarm's manual says. The CPSC says to follow the maker's instructions for testing. The button still tells you the alarm can sound.

Sources: CPSC, Carbon Monoxide Questions and Answers; CDC, Carbon Monoxide Poisoning Basics, January 12, 2026.

What should I do if my carbon monoxide alarm goes off?

Quick answer: get outside first. Then call for help.

The CPSC says: "Never ignore an alarming CO alarm!" Then:

  1. "Immediately move outside to fresh air."
  2. "Call your emergency services, fire department, or 911."

Do not wait to smell something. The CDC calls carbon monoxide "an odorless, colorless gas that kills without warning."

The CPSC says the first signs of low to moderate CO poisoning "are similar to the flu (but without the fever)."

Sources: CPSC, Carbon Monoxide Questions and Answers; CDC, Carbon Monoxide Poisoning Basics, January 12, 2026.

What if my plug-in alarm is a KH158?

Quick answer: stop using it, and put up a new one the same day.

On August 20, 2026, the CPSC warned owners of one plug-in gas and carbon monoxide detector, model KH158: "Stop using and dispose of these products immediately."

The CPSC said it knew of 91 reports of these detectors failing to alarm during gas or carbon monoxide leaks. It said the maker "has not agreed to recall these detectors."

I show how to spot one, and the 18 brand names it was sold under, in Are plug-in carbon monoxide detectors reliable?

If yours is not a KH158, that does not clear it. Every alarm still needs a battery, the right spot, and a new one when it reaches its replacement age.

Source: CPSC, warning on the KH158 plug-in gas and carbon monoxide detector, August 20, 2026.

What can I do tonight?

Quick answer: move the alarm out of the kitchen and into the hall outside the bedrooms.

  1. Unplug the alarm by the stove. If it is a KH158, stop using it and get a new one today.
  2. Put the alarm in the hall outside the bedrooms. Use an outlet there, or put it high on that wall.
  3. Check for a battery. The CPSC says a plug-in should have battery backup. If yours has none, get one that runs on a battery or has a battery backup.
  4. Add one on every other level. The basement counts.
  5. Keep it in the open. Not by a heating vent. Not behind curtains or a couch.
  6. Find its replacement age. Check the papers or ask the maker. Write the date on tape on the back.
  7. Book a yearly furnace check. The CPSC says to "Have the heating system professionally inspected and serviced annually to ensure proper operation." It says an alarm "is no substitute for proper use and upkeep of appliances that can produce CO."

The swap, in plain words: a battery or battery backup alarm in the hallway outside the bedrooms, instead of a plug-in by the stove.
