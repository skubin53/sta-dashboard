Is It Safe to Microwave Food in a Ziploc Bag?

What the bag maker says, what a 2023 study found when plastic was heated in a microwave, what that study did not test, and the easy habit that sidesteps it.

By Shannon Nicole, founder of Switch to America. Seven years helping women switch to a toxin-free home, and a health coach for over 20 years. Published October 15, 2026.

Ziploc's maker, SC Johnson, says its bags can go in the microwave, but only to defrost or reheat food. Still, in a 2023 University of Nebraska-Lincoln study, microwaving made plastic shed more tiny bits than storing it in the fridge or at room temperature. A polyethylene food pouch shed more than polypropylene containers. The simple fix is to move your food to a glass bowl or a plate before you heat it.

Heat was when the plastic shed the most. So the food comes out of the bag before it goes in the microwave.

Can you put a Ziploc bag in the microwave?

Quick answer: the company says yes, but only to defrost or reheat, with the zipper open at least one inch.

SC Johnson makes Ziploc. Its FAQ says the bags "can be used in the microwave, but only to defrost or reheat food." It says to take care with foods high in sugar or fat, because they heat up very quickly. And it says to "leave the zipper of the bag open at least one inch to vent any steam or heat."

The same FAQ says its bags made with polyethylene should not be used to cook food in boiling water. It lists their softening point as 230°F. It also says the bags are BPA free, and that its products are "extensively evaluated for toxicity and safety."

Notice what the FAQ covers: steam, sugar, fat, softening, BPA and dioxin. It does not mention microplastics.

Source: SC Johnson, Ziploc Frequently Asked Questions, U.S. page, as saved by the Internet Archive on July 28, 2026.

Does microwaving plastic put microplastics in your food?

Quick answer: in a 2023 lab study that used liquids in place of food, yes. The microwave released the most tiny plastic bits of any way the plastic was used.

Microplastics are tiny bits of plastic. Nanoplastics are even smaller, under one thousandth of a millimeter across.

Scientists at the University of Nebraska-Lincoln tested two plastic food containers made of polypropylene and a reusable food pouch made of polyethylene. They filled them with water or with a weak acid. The acid (3% acetic acid, the acid in vinegar) stands in for foods like dairy, fruits and vegetables. Then they heated them at full power for three minutes in a 1,000 watt microwave. Here is what they found, in plain words:

  • The microwave released the most. The study says microwave heating "caused the highest release" of microplastics and nanoplastics, compared with the fridge or room temperature.
  • The counts were huge. In 3 minutes, some containers released as many as 4.22 million microplastics and 2.11 billion nanoplastics from one square centimeter of plastic. That is a patch smaller than a postage stamp.
  • Storage without heat was not zero. Keeping the containers in the fridge or at room temperature for over six months also released millions to billions of particles.
  • The pouch shed more. The polyethylene food pouch released more particles than the polypropylene containers. And SC Johnson's own FAQ talks about Ziploc bags "made with polyethylene."

Sources: Hussain and others, Assessing the Release of Microplastics and Nanoplastics from Plastic Containers and Reusable Food Pouches, Environmental Science and Technology, 2023; University of Nebraska-Lincoln, news release, July 21, 2023.

What did the study not show?

Quick answer: it did not test Ziploc bags, real meals, or harm in people.

  • It did not test Ziploc bags. It tested two containers and one reusable pouch.
  • It did not use real leftovers. It used water and a weak acid in place of food.
  • It did not measure harm in people. In a lab dish, the team put the released particles on human kidney cells. At the highest amount, only 23% of the cells were still alive after two days. The cells got more particles than one container released. That was meant to match what could build up over days or from several sources. That is a cell test, not a test in people.

The university's own news release says the health effects of eating these particles "remain unclear."

So this study does not prove your lunch will make you sick. It does show that heat makes plastic shed more. And heat is the one step you can skip for free.

Is it only microplastics, or do chemicals come out too?

Quick answer: chemicals can come out too, and heat can speed that up.

Plastic is not only plastic. Makers add other chemicals to it. A 2011 study in the journal Environmental Health Perspectives explains why. These additives are not chemically part of the plastic itself, so they can leach out.

The team tested hundreds of store-bought plastic products, some after microwaving. Almost all of them released chemicals with estrogen-like activity in a lab cell test. That included products sold as BPA free. The authors wrote that stress like microwaving "often increases the leaching" of those chemicals.

Plastic bags were part of the test. The team bought 43 of them, along with the other products, from 2005 to 2008. Even with no heat, 98% of those bags showed this activity.

One thing to know about that study. Four of its five authors worked for, or founded, two companies, CertiChem and PlastiPure. The paper says two of them own stock. The authors also wrote that they were free to design and publish the work without a controlling sponsor.

Source: Yang and others, Most Plastic Products Release Estrogenic Chemicals, Environmental Health Perspectives, 2011, including its conflict of interest statement.

What should you heat leftovers in instead?

Quick answer: glass or a plate. Take the food out of the bag first.

  1. Keep a glass bowl next to the microwave. Then the easy choice is the clean choice.
  2. Take the food out of the bag before you heat it. Pour soup into the bowl. Slide leftovers onto a plate.
  3. Cover it with a plate, not plastic. A plate on top keeps the splatter in.
  4. Thaw frozen soup in the fridge first. Then pour it into a bowl to heat.
  5. Go one step further when you are ready. Store leftovers in glass, too. In the Nebraska study, storage in the fridge or at room temperature still released particles over six months, just less than the microwave.

Common questions about microwaving plastic bags

Does leaving the Ziploc bag open make it safe to microwave?

SC Johnson says to leave the zipper open at least one inch to vent steam or heat. Its FAQ does not say that venting changes what comes off the plastic. The 2023 Nebraska study did not test vented bags. The cleaner habit is to move the food to glass or a plate before you heat it.

Does keeping food in plastic in the fridge release microplastics?

In the 2023 Nebraska study, yes, but less than the microwave. Plastic kept in the fridge or at room temperature for over six months also released millions to billions of tiny particles. The microwave released the most.

Are "BPA free" plastic bags safe to heat?

"BPA free" only tells you about one chemical. SC Johnson says its Ziploc bags are BPA free. But a 2011 study found that almost all the plastic products it tested, including ones sold as BPA free, released chemicals with estrogen-like activity in a lab cell test. Four of its five authors worked for or founded two companies, and two of them own stock.

What is the best way to reheat leftovers in the microwave?

Take the food out of the plastic first. Put it in a glass bowl or on a plate, cover it with another plate, and heat it there. Keep a glass bowl next to your microwave so it is the easy choice every time.

Sources for these answers: SC Johnson, Ziploc FAQ (archived July 28, 2026); Hussain and others, Environmental Science and Technology (2023); Yang and others, Environmental Health Perspectives (2011).

What can I do this week?

Put one glass bowl next to your microwave today. The next time you reach for a bag of leftovers, pour it into the bowl first. It takes a few seconds, and it skips the step where the plastic shed the most.
