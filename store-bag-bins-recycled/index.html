Do the Plastic Bag Bins at the Store Actually Recycle the Bags?

What California said happens to the bags in the bin by the front door, what its survey found, what the tiny print on the bag means, and what to carry instead.

By Shannon Nicole, founder of Switch to America. Seven years helping women switch to a toxin-free home, and a health coach for over 20 years. Published November 19, 2026.

In California, the state says mostly no. In an October 2025 complaint, it alleged that "the vast majority" of bags dropped in store bins "are not recycled, in California or anywhere else." The bag makers settled in January 2026 for $3.35 million and did not admit wrongdoing.

A bin by the front door is a promise. It is not proof that your bags get recycled.

What did California say happens to the bags in the store bin?

Quick answer: the state says most of them end up in landfills, get burned, or get shipped to other countries.

In October 2025, California sued three bag makers: Novolex, Inteplast and Mettler. The state's complaint says many stores keep bins out front for used bags. It says signs on or near the bins tell you that you can recycle your bags by dropping them in.

Then it says this: "the vast majority of the plastic carryout bags that consumers deposit in these store drop-off bins for recycling are not recycled, in California or anywhere else." It says the bags "end up in landfills or incinerators or are shipped to other countries."

These are the state's allegations. They are not a court finding. No judge ruled on them, because the companies settled.

Source: The People of the State of California v. Novolex Holdings, LLC, et al., complaint, San Francisco Superior Court, October 2025, paragraphs 58 and 59.

What did the state find when it checked recycling sites?

Quick answer: of 69 sites it surveyed, only two said they took plastic bags, and neither could confirm the bags were recycled.

The Attorney General's office announced the settlement on January 16, 2026. It said the investigation "included a statewide survey to verify whether waste processing and recycling facilities accept plastic bags for recycling."

Its words: "Out of the 69 facilities surveyed, only two claimed to accept plastic bags, but could not confirm the bags were, in fact, recycled."

That survey was about recycling sites, not the bins at the store. But it is where store bin bags would have to go to become something new.

The state also said that under subpoena, the bag makers it looked into could not show how many bags got recycled at their own plants. They also could not show what share of the bags they sold to California stores got recycled.

Source: California Attorney General, Attorney General Bonta Announces $3.35 Million Settlement with Plastic Bag Manufacturers, January 16, 2026, read November 19, 2026.

What does the tiny print on the bag mean?

Quick answer: it is a warning that a recycling program may not exist where you live.

The state's complaint shows a Novolex bag sold in California. In bold capital letters it says, "PLEASE RETURN TO A PARTICIPATING STORE FOR RECYCLING." Below that, in tiny print, it says, "Programs may not exist in your area."

There is a national guide for the word "recyclable." It is the FTC's Green Guides. The FTC polices false ads. Here is what the guide says:

  • The plain word is for most people. A company can just say "recyclable" when recycling is open to a "substantial majority" of people where it is sold. That "means at least 60 percent."
  • A soft warning is for a little less. When recycling reaches "slightly less than a substantial majority," a company may say, "Recycling facilities for this product [package] may not exist in your area."
  • Only a few people? Say it louder. "If recycling facilities are available only to a few consumers, marketers should use stronger clarifications."

Look at how close the bag's small line is to that soft warning. The guide meant that line for places where a little less than 60 percent of people can recycle. The state's complaint quotes this same guide. And it alleges that in California, most bags are not recycled at all. That is the state's claim, not a court finding.

Sources: California complaint, October 2025, paragraphs 35 and 47; FTC Green Guides, 16 CFR 260.12, Recyclable claims, read November 19, 2026.

What does this mean if I live outside California?

Quick answer: the case does not tell you what happens to the bin at your store.

Here is the other side, straight.

  • Nobody admitted anything. The court papers say the deal does not "constitute an admission of wrongdoing or liability."
  • Some bags do get recycled, the company says. The complaint notes that Novolex says its plant in North Vernon, Indiana, has recycled store drop-off bags into new bags since at least 2020.
  • This case is about California's law. It is not a finding about every bin in the country.

The complaint does go further. It alleges there is "no robust market" for used plastic bags "anywhere in the United States." That is the state's claim, not a court finding.

So if you live somewhere else, the honest answer is this: no one has shown you that your bin works, and California says the bins there mostly did not.

Is it worth taking my bags back to the store?

Quick answer: the better trip is the one where no new bags come home.

You saved every bag. You carried the whole bundle back because the bag told you to. You wanted that trip to count. That is a good habit. The state says the bags did not keep up their end.

So here is what I do instead, in plain words:

  1. Keep two cloth bags on the hook by the back door. Put them back in the car when you unload.
  2. Wash them like a dish towel when they get grubby.
  3. Use up the plastic bags you already have. Line a small trash can with them until they tear.
  4. Keep them out of the blue bin at home. The complaint says most California sorting plants tell people not to put bags in home recycling bins.
