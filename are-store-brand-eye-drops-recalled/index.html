Are Store Brand Eye Drops Recalled?

Which store brand eye drops were pulled in 2026, who really made them, what the FDA found at the plant, and what to do instead.

By Shannon Nicole, founder of Switch to America. Seven years helping women switch to a toxin-free home, and a health coach for over 20 years. Published October 8, 2026.

Yes. In March 2026, a California company called K.C. Pharmaceuticals recalled 3,111,072 bottles of eye drops, FDA recall records show. Most of the labels named are store brands, like Walgreens, CVS Health, Kroger and Rite Aid. The reason was "Lack of Assurance of Sterility," and in August 2026, Walgreens Lubricant Eye Drops from a second maker were recalled for the same reason.

A store brand tells you who sells the eye drops. It does not tell you who made them.

Which store brand eye drops were recalled in 2026?

Quick answer: eight kinds from one maker in March, and Walgreens Lubricant Eye Drops from another maker in August.

FDA recall records show K.C. Pharmaceuticals, Inc., of Pomona, California, starting eight recalls at once on March 3, 2026. The FDA's records give the same reason for all eight: "Lack of Assurance of Sterility." Each one went out nationwide. Added together, it is 3,111,072 bottles. (The FDA's August 2026 warning letter gives an earlier start date for this recall: February 18, 2026.)

Was there a second store brand eye drop recall in 2026?

Yes. On August 20, 2026, Sterling Pharmaceutical Services of Dupo, Illinois, recalled Walgreens Lubricant Eye Drops. The FDA has described Sterling as a company that makes sterile eye drugs for other companies. That was 301,114 single bottles and 199,600 twin packs. The FDA lists the same reason: "Lack of Assurance of Sterility."

These batches carry expiration dates from September 2026 to July 2027. So some of them are not past their date yet.

Who actually makes store brand eye drops?

Quick answer: often a company whose name you have never seen.

A store brand can be made by another company for the store. On these recalls, the FDA's records list the store as the one that distributed the drops. For example, "Distributed by Walgreen Co." The company that filled the bottles is a separate firm.

K.C. Pharmaceuticals filled bottles sold as Walgreens, CVS Health, Kroger, Rite Aid, Publix, Meijer, H-E-B and more. That is why one problem at one company can show up under many store names at the same time.

What did the FDA find at K.C. Pharmaceuticals?

Quick answer: problems keeping germs out, written up twice, three years apart.

On August 3, 2023, the FDA sent the company a warning letter. It said the company's "aseptic processing operation is inadequately designed to prevent contamination of your ophthalmic drug products." Aseptic processing means filling bottles in a germ-free way. Ophthalmic means for the eye.

The FDA inspected again from January 20 to February 13, 2026. On August 12, 2026, it sent a second warning letter. Here is what the FDA wrote, in plain words:

  • It kept going after failed tests. The company kept making and releasing sterile drugs after test runs of its filling line failed on June 21, September 4 and November 4, 2024.
  • Workers leaned into the clean area. Operators put their upper bodies inside the barrier meant to keep the filling area clean.
  • The old problems came back. The FDA said the company's fixes after the 2023 letter were "neither effective nor durable." It wrote that the company's "failure to correct these issues led to unacceptable drug product being distributed to the U.S. market."
  • Production paused. The FDA noted the company's promise to pause making its over-the-counter drug products at that plant.

These are the FDA's findings. A warning letter gives the company a chance to answer, and this one asked for a written reply within 15 working days.

What is a "media fill"?

The FDA's letter calls those failed test runs "media fills." In a media fill, the line fills bottles with a liquid that germs grow in, in place of the real drug. Then the bottles are stored for at least two weeks and checked for germs. The FDA explains this in its guidance on sterile drugs made by aseptic processing.

Sources: FDA warning letters to K.C. Pharmaceuticals, August 3, 2023 and August 12, 2026; FDA, Sterile Drug Products Produced by Aseptic Processing.

Does "Lack of Assurance of Sterility" mean there were germs in my drops?

Quick answer: no. It means the company could not show the drops were sterile.

That is not the same as finding germs in a bottle. The August 2026 letter says the company started the recall "due to failing media fills and a lack of sterility assurance."

Here is the other side, straight. The FDA put every 2026 recall on this page in Class II. That is the middle of its three recall classes. The FDA uses Class II when a product "may cause temporary or medically reversible adverse health consequences or where the probability of serious adverse health consequences is remote."

So why does it still sit wrong with me? Because the FDA had put this company's germ-control problems in writing in 2023. Then it found them again.

Source: FDA, Recalls Background and Definitions.

Why do eye drops have to be so clean?

Quick answer: because a drop goes right onto your eye, past some of your body's defenses.

In its 2023 eye drop warning, the FDA put it this way: "drugs applied to the eyes bypass some of the body's natural defenses."

The same year, the CDC tied an outbreak of a rare, drug-resistant germ to an imported brand of artificial tears, EzriCare. Its maker, Global Pharma of Chennai, India, recalled it. As of May 15, 2023, the CDC counted 81 patients in 18 states. It reported 14 with vision loss, 4 who had an eyeball removed, and 4 deaths within 30 days of the lab sample being taken.

That was not a store brand. But it shows why clean making is the whole product when it comes to eye drops.

Sources: FDA, FDA warns consumers not to purchase or use certain eye drops from several major brands, October 27, 2023; CDC, Outbreak of Extensively Drug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa Associated with Artificial Tears (archived page, update as of May 15, 2023).

Were store brand eye drops recalled before 2026?

Quick answer: yes, more than once in 2023.

  • June 2023. K.C. Pharmaceuticals recalled two eye drops, 738,998 bottles in all. One was Dry Eye Relief sold as Walmart's Equate. The FDA lists the reason as "good manufacturing deficiencies related to a lack of documentation of the fill line."
  • October 2023. The FDA warned people not to buy or use 26 eye drop products sold under CVS Health, Leader, Rugby, Rite Aid, Target Up & Up and Velocity Pharma. It later added Walmart's Equate Hydration PF. The FDA said its investigators found "insanitary conditions" at the plant and bacteria in samples from "critical drug production areas." The maker, Kilitch Healthcare India Limited, recalled the products in November 2023.

To be fair, in October 2023 the FDA also said it had "not received any adverse event reports of eye infection associated with these products at this time."

Sources: FDA Enforcement Report records D-0897-2023 and D-0898-2023; FDA, eye drop warning and updates, October 27 to November 15, 2023.

Are name brand eye drops safer than store brands?

Quick answer: not because of the name alone.

Name brands had sterility recalls in 2026, too. FDA records show Rohto Cooling Eye Drops, made in Vietnam, recalled in July 2026. They show Clear Eyes Redness Relief recalled in September 2026. Both list the same reason: "Lack of Assurance of Sterility."

So the real question is not store brand or name brand. It is who made the drops, and who will answer when something goes wrong.

What should I do instead?

Quick answer: keep using drops if you need them. Just know who made them.

  1. Stop using a recalled bottle. If you know a bottle you own was recalled, stop using it and throw it away. That was the FDA's advice for the 2023 recall list.
  2. Do not treat "not on a list" as a clean bill of health. A recall covers only the batches named in it. It does not tell you how any other bottle was made.
  3. Choose a maker you can name. Buy eye drops from a company that makes them itself and puts its own name on the bottle. Then you know who made them, and who to call.
  4. Make the swap easy. Set the store brand bottle aside tonight. Put the one you can trace in its spot in your purse.
  5. Watch your eyes. If your eyes hurt, turn red, have a discharge, or your vision gets blurry after using drops, see a doctor right away.
