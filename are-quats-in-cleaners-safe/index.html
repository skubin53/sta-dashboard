Are Quats in Cleaners Safe?

What quaternary ammonium compounds are, where they hide at home, what the research really says, and the simple swap.

By Shannon Nicole, founder of Switch to America. Seven years helping women switch to a toxin-free home, and a health coach for over 20 years.

Quats, short for quaternary ammonium compounds, are the most common germ killers in disinfecting wipes, and a close cousin does the softening in fabric softener and dryer sheets. The germ-killing kind are registered as pesticides, and studies of people who clean for work have linked them to asthma, though the research is mixed. For everyday cleaning you do not need them, because the CDC says cleaning with soap or detergent removes most harmful germs from surfaces.

What are quats?

Quats are a big chemical family, not one chemical. California's Department of Toxic Substances Control describes them as a class of hundreds of different chemicals and chemical mixtures. Researchers at Indiana University put it more simply: quats are chemicals used as disinfectants in cleaning and other consumer products.

When the California agency screened cleaning and personal care products, it found 263 different quats used across 78 product categories. That spread is why it helps to know the names, which are listed below.

The EPA sorts quats into four groups for testing. Two of those groups are the DDAC group (didecyl dimethyl ammonium chloride) and the ADBAC group (alkyl dimethyl benzyl ammonium chloride). ADBAC is sometimes called benzalkonium chloride, or BAC.

Sources: California Department of Toxic Substances Control, Background Document on Quaternary Ammonium Compounds (December 2024); Zheng, Filippelli and Salamova, Environmental Science and Technology Letters (2020); US EPA, PRN 88-2: Clustering of Quaternary Ammonium Compounds; National Pesticide Information Center, Oregon State University, Quats (ADBAC and DDAC) Fact Sheet.

Where do quats hide at home?

At home, look in four places: disinfecting wipes, disinfecting sprays, fabric softener and dryer sheets, and hair conditioner. The California agency lists surface disinfectants in sprays and wipes, and antistatic agents in hair conditioners and fabric softeners, among the ways quats are used in consumer products.

Are quats in disinfecting wipes and sprays?

Yes. A California Department of Public Health fact sheet says the most common chemicals in disinfecting wipes are quats. The National Pesticide Information Center at Oregon State University says quats come in ready-to-use sprays, aerosols, wipes and other forms.

Here is the part that surprises people. Bleach free does not mean quat free. A national-brand disinfecting wipe sold as bleach free lists two quats on its own ingredient label: alkyl C12-14 dimethylethylbenzyl ammonium chloride and alkyl C12-18 dimethylbenzyl ammonium chloride.

For a closer look at two familiar sprays, see Is Lysol safe to inhale? and Is Clorox spray toxic?

Are quats in fabric softener and dryer sheets?

Yes. The softening ingredient is often a type of quat called an esterquat. A peer-reviewed review in the Journal of Oleo Science calls esterquats a new generation of fabric softening agents, and says quats with ester groups replaced the older softening chemicals in fabric softeners during the 1990s. The Indiana researchers also note that DDAC quats are used in fabric softeners.

You can see it on the labels. A national-brand liquid fabric softener lists diethylester dimethyl ammonium chloride as its softener active, and a national-brand dryer sheet lists dialkylester dimethyl ammonium methosulfate as its cationic softener. Both are esterquats. There is more on this in Are dryer sheets bad for you?

Are quats in hair conditioner?

Some are. The California agency lists quats as antistatic agents in hair conditioners, and the Indiana researchers note that one group, called ATMACs, is used in cosmetics and hair conditioners. This page sticks to cleaning and laundry.

Sources: California Department of Public Health, Disinfectants and Work-Related Asthma: Information for Employers (2017); National Pesticide Information Center, Quats Fact Sheet; Mishra and Tyagi, Ester Quats: The Novel Class of Cationic Fabric Softeners, Journal of Oleo Science (2007); Zheng, Webster and Salamova, Environmental Science and Technology (2021); manufacturer ingredient lists linked above.

How do I spot quats on a label?

Look for these names. They come from the government fact sheets and manufacturer labels on this page.

  • Benzalkonium chloride, or BAC
  • Alkyl dimethyl benzyl ammonium chloride (ADBAC)
  • Didecyl dimethyl ammonium chloride (DDAC)
  • Benzyl-C10-16-alkyldimethyl chlorides
  • Alkyl C12-14 dimethylethylbenzyl ammonium chloride
  • Diethylester dimethyl ammonium chloride
  • Dialkylester dimethyl ammonium methosulfate

A quick tip from this list: almost every name has the word ammonium or benzalkonium in it. The state health fact sheet adds that quats also go by similar sounding names, so if a long chemical name looks like these, give it a second look.

Why are quats registered as pesticides?

Because they are made to kill germs. The National Pesticide Information Center says quats are used in antimicrobial products and are considered pesticides, and that more than 2,000 pesticide products contain quats. The EPA defines antimicrobial pesticides as products that destroy or suppress the growth of germs such as bacteria, viruses or fungi on nonliving objects and surfaces.

Which law covers a product depends on where you use it. The EPA says germ killers used on surfaces fall under the federal pesticide law, called FIFRA, while those used on people or animals fall under the Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act. That is why the California agency notes that many quat cleaning products, including disinfecting wipes made for surfaces rather than skin, are registered pesticide products. The EPA's 2006 review of ADBAC lists it as registered for use in homes, among other settings, and its registered uses include indoor hard surfaces such as walls, floors, tables and toilets, plus laundry and carpets.

The Indiana researchers also report that the EPA's List N, its list of disinfectants for the COVID virus, has more than 200 products containing quats as active ingredients.

Sources: National Pesticide Information Center, Quats Fact Sheet; US EPA, What are Antimicrobial Pesticides? and Pesticide Registration Manual, Chapter 4; US EPA, Reregistration Eligibility Decision for ADBAC (2006); California Department of Toxic Substances Control, QACs Background Document; Zheng, Webster and Salamova, Environmental Science and Technology (2021).

Are quats linked to asthma?

In several studies of people who clean for a living, yes, though not every study agrees.

The California Department of Public Health names quats (benzalkonium chlorides) among cleaning ingredients known to cause asthma and tells employers to avoid them. The same fact sheet says most disinfectant wipes contain asthmagens, meaning chemicals that can cause asthma even in people who have never had it. The sheet was written for workplaces, and the state's work-related asthma program has found more than 250 work-related asthma cases linked to disinfectant exposure. That count covers all disinfectants, not quats alone.

The Association of Occupational and Environmental Clinics (AOEC), a nonprofit network of clinics, lists two specific quats, benzalkonium chloride and DDAC, as asthmagens that met its criteria for sensitizer-induced asthma. AOEC notes that its list is not an official government document.

In a peer-reviewed study of 543 health care workers, 89 percent of them women, quat exposure was linked to 7.5 times the odds of reported doctor-diagnosed asthma and 3.2 times the odds of nose symptoms at work. The highest risk came from mixing and diluting disinfectant concentrate by hand.

Is the research settled?

No, and it is fair to say so. A study of 4,102 US nurses who already had asthma, average age 58, found that quat exposure was not linked to poorer asthma control, even though several other disinfectants were. A larger study of 73,262 US female nurses, average age 54.7 when it began, found that high-level exposure to several disinfectants, quats among them, was associated with a 25 to 36 percent higher risk of COPD, a chronic lung disease. For quats, the figure was 33 percent. But nurses exposed only to quats showed no link to COPD, and the authors say more research is needed to show cause and effect.

The National Pesticide Information Center gives the same balanced picture: several studies found asthma was more common in workers who used quat cleaning products, while the nurses' study did not find harder-to-control asthma. And the Indiana researchers note that human studies of quats are limited and mostly about workplace exposure. So most of what we know comes from nurses and cleaners, not from a woman wiping her kitchen counter.

Sources: California Department of Public Health, Disinfectants and Work-Related Asthma (2017, filed in the CDC Stacks NIOSH collection); AOEC, Exposure Code Lookup and Description of the AOEC Exposure Code System; Gonzalez et al., Clinical and Experimental Allergy (2014); Dumas et al., European Respiratory Journal (2017); Dumas et al., JAMA Network Open (2019); National Pesticide Information Center, Quats Fact Sheet; Zheng, Webster and Salamova, Environmental Science and Technology (2021).

Do quats get into the air and dust at home?

Yes, in the studies that have looked. In a 2024 study, UMass Lowell and NIOSH researchers measured the air home care aides breathed while they cleaned a mock home bathroom with a ready-to-use quat spray. Quats turned up in all 20 air samples taken while they used that spray. The National Pesticide Information Center adds that germ-killing products like these are not typically meant to be sprayed into the air.

Dust holds on to them too. Indiana researchers tested vacuum dust from 40 Indiana homes in 2020, and each of the 19 quats they looked for turned up in more than 90 percent of the samples. Dust from 21 homes collected in 2018 and 2019, before the pandemic, had quats in more than 95 percent of samples, and median levels rose 62 percent in the pandemic samples. Homes that disinfected 1 to 5 times a week had about twice the quat level in their dust compared with homes that disinfected less than once a week or not at all.

The mix of quats in the dust was similar to the mix in the disinfecting products those homes used, which the researchers say suggests those products could be a significant source. They also note the samples were small and came from a limited area.

Are quats in people's blood?

In one Indiana study, mostly yes. Researchers tested 222 blood samples, 111 from 2019 and 111 from 2020, and found 15 of the 18 quats they looked for. In the 2020 samples, two benzalkonium-type quats showed up in 97 percent and 95 percent of samples. Even before the pandemic, in 2019, the most common quats were in 88 to 94 percent of samples. The donors averaged 57 years old and were half women.

Two honest caveats. The study did not record whether donors used disinfectants, so it cannot tie blood levels to cleaning habits. And finding a chemical in blood tells you exposure happened, not that it caused harm.

Sources: Quinn, Lindberg et al., Annals of Work Exposures and Health (2024); National Pesticide Information Center, Quats Fact Sheet; Zheng, Filippelli and Salamova, Environmental Science and Technology Letters (2020); Zheng, Webster and Salamova, Environmental Science and Technology (2021).

So, are quats in cleaners safe?

Here is the plain answer. Quats are in more than 2,000 registered pesticide products, and the research on everyday home use is limited. But they are pesticides built to kill germs, they have been linked to asthma in people who use them at work, they show up in home dust, and one study found the most common ones in most blood samples tested. Since most days you do not need them, the calm move is simply to use them less.

Clean is not the same as disinfected, and on most days clean is all your home needs.

What should I use instead of quat cleaners?

The swap is simple, and it lines up with what the CDC already recommends.

What cleans well without quats?

Plain soap and water. The CDC says household cleaners with soap or detergent remove germs and dirt, that cleaning alone removes most harmful viruses and bacteria from surfaces, and that in most situations cleaning regularly is enough to prevent the spread of germs. In its advice for workplaces, the California health department adds that disinfectant wipes are often used where water and a cloth would do, like a coffee spill, and that regular cleaning with microfiber is usually enough.

When should I disinfect?

When it matters. The CDC says to disinfect when someone in the home is sick or has a weakened immune system. If a surface is visibly dirty, the EPA says to wash it with soap and water first. The same thinking applies at the bathroom sink, covered in Do you need antibacterial soap?

How do I breathe in less while I clean?

Let fresh air in. The CDC advises good ventilation when you use products indoors, such as a fan or open windows and doors, and says not to mix products. The California health department also says to bring in fresh air, especially while cleaning. And spray onto the cloth, not into the room. The 2024 UMass Lowell and NIOSH study says spraying cleaning and disinfecting products, conventional and green, should be avoided, and points to earlier research showing that holding the nozzle about 1 centimeter (under half an inch) from the cloth or paper towel can reduce what you breathe in.

What should I choose for laundry and surfaces?

Choose fragrance-free and quat-free products for laundry and for wiping down surfaces, with a short ingredient list you can actually read, and American made where you can. For laundry, that can be as simple as skipping the liquid softener and the dryer sheet. Before you buy, check the label against the names above.

Common questions about quats

Are quats in cleaners safe?

Quats are registered as pesticides, and studies of people who clean for work have linked them to asthma, though the evidence is mixed and research on everyday home use is limited. Because the CDC says regular cleaning with soap or detergent is enough in most situations, most homes can skip them day to day and save disinfectant for when someone is sick.

What are quats and where are they found?

Quats, short for quaternary ammonium compounds, are a family of hundreds of chemicals, and many are used to kill germs in disinfecting wipes and sprays. A related kind called esterquats does the softening in fabric softener and dryer sheets, and some quats are used in hair conditioner. California's Department of Toxic Substances Control found 263 different quats used across 78 product categories in cleaning and personal care products.

How can I tell if a product has quats?

Read the ingredient list for names like benzalkonium chloride, alkyl dimethyl benzyl ammonium chloride, or didecyl dimethyl ammonium chloride. Fabric softeners and dryer sheets can list softeners such as diethylester dimethyl ammonium chloride or dialkylester dimethyl ammonium methosulfate. A disinfecting wipe sold as bleach free can still contain quats.

Are quats linked to asthma?

In some studies, yes. The California Department of Public Health lists benzalkonium chlorides, a common quat, among cleaning ingredients known to cause asthma, and a study of 543 health care workers linked quat exposure to a higher risk of reported doctor-diagnosed asthma. The evidence is mixed, though: a study of 4,102 US nurses who already had asthma found quats were not linked to poorer asthma control. Most of the research is on people exposed at work.

Do I need to disinfect my home every day?

No. The CDC says that in most situations, cleaning regularly is enough to prevent the spread of germs, and that household cleaners with soap or detergent remove germs and dirt. The CDC advises disinfecting when someone in the home is sick or has a weakened immune system.

What can I do this week?

Start small. Tonight, look under the sink and read the labels on your wipes and sprays for the names above. This week, wipe your counters with soap, water and a microfiber cloth, and keep the disinfectant for when someone is sick. On your next laundry day, skip the softener and the dryer sheet and see if you even miss them. Open a window while you clean.
