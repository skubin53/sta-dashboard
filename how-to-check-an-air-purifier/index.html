How Do I Know If an Air Purifier Really Works?

What to check on the box besides "True HEPA": the room size, the CADR number, and whether it gives off ozone. Plus the two steps the EPA puts ahead of any filter.

By Shannon Nicole, founder of Switch to America. Seven years helping women switch to a toxin-free home, and a health coach for over 20 years. Published October 25, 2026.

To know if an air purifier can really do the job, check two things the EPA points to: a CADR (clean air delivery rate) big enough for your room, and no ozone. "True HEPA" on the box is the company's own claim, and you cannot test it at home. The EPA also says the best ways to clean indoor air come first: take out what puts things into the air, and let in clean outdoor air.

"True HEPA" tells you about the filter. The CADR tells you if the machine is big enough for your room.

Does an air purifier really work?

Quick answer: it can pull particles out of the air. The EPA calls it a helper, not the first step.

The EPA says portable air cleaners "can reduce indoor air pollution; however, they cannot remove all pollutants from the air."

It says filtering "can be an effective supplement" to two bigger steps. One is to take out the sources. The other is to bring in clean outdoor air.

Can a purifier help your health? The EPA's answer is "Possibly." It says studies with portable HEPA air cleaners found small gains in heart and lung health. It also says those gains are "typically small and not always noticeable" to the person.

Source: US EPA, Guide to Air Cleaners in the Home, last updated October 1, 2026.

What does "True HEPA" on the box really tell me?

Quick answer: it is the company's claim about its own filter. You have no way to test it at your kitchen table.

The EPA says a HEPA filter "can theoretically remove at least 99.97%" of airborne particles 0.3 microns in size. That is what the word HEPA on a box is supposed to mean.

You cannot see a filter's grade by looking at it. So "True HEPA" is a promise you take on trust.

Even California's air board warns, in a section on furnace filters, that high-efficiency filters "are sometimes mistakenly called HEPA or True HEPA."

What happened with the Levoit "True HEPA" lawsuit?

Shoppers sued Vesync (US) Corp., the company behind Levoit. The official settlement site says the lawsuit alleges "false or misleading" "True HEPA," "HEPA" and "H13" claims. They were about Levoit Core and EverestAir purifiers and filters sold in the US from August 29, 2019 to August 4, 2023.

Vesync "unequivocally denies all allegations." The court has not decided who is right. This is a proposed settlement, not a verdict. Claims close November 3, 2026.

So when you shop for the next one, lean on more than one line on the box.

Sources: US EPA, What is a HEPA filter?; California Air Resources Board, Air Cleaning Devices for the Home, fact sheet dated November 6, 2020; official notice, Sen v. Vesync (US) Corp. settlement.

What should I check on the box besides "True HEPA"?

Quick answer: three things. The CADR, the largest room size it is made for, and whether it makes ozone.

What is CADR on an air purifier?

CADR stands for clean air delivery rate. The EPA says to pick a portable air cleaner with a CADR "that is large enough for the size of the room or area in which you will use it."

"The higher the CADR, the more particles the air cleaner can filter and the larger the area it can serve," the EPA says.

Some boxes show three CADR numbers: tobacco smoke, dust and pollen. For the smallest particles, the EPA says to look for a high CADR for tobacco smoke.

One catch. The EPA says the CADR rating "is for particles only." There is no widely used rating for gases or smells.

How do I know if an air purifier is big enough for my room?

  1. Measure the room. Multiply the length by the width. That is your square feet.
  2. Find the numbers on the box. Look for the CADR and the largest room size. Compare them to your square feet.
  3. Use the EPA chart below. Say your living room is 15 feet by 20 feet. That is 300 square feet. On the chart, it needs a CADR of at least 195.
  4. Go bigger for big spaces. With an open floor plan, the EPA says to count the whole open space. With ceilings above 8 feet, it says to pick a unit sized for a larger area. If the space is bigger than any one unit can serve, you could use more than one.

AHAM, a trade group for appliance makers, keeps an online directory of room air cleaners. It says each model's performance there was checked at a third-party lab. That checks the CADR number. It does not prove a purifier is safe.

How do I know if an air purifier gives off ozone?

Quick answer: skip anything sold to make ozone, and check California's list of certified air cleaners before you buy.

The EPA says to "avoid portable air cleaners and furnace/HVAC filters that intentionally produce ozone." It adds, "Ozone is a lung irritant."

When you breathe it in, the EPA says, ozone can damage the lungs. Even fairly low amounts can cause chest pain, coughing, shortness of breath and throat irritation.

The EPA also says no agency of the federal government has approved ozone machines for use in rooms where people are. And California's air board says it plainly: "Ozone generators do not clean the air."

What words on the box can mean ozone?

California's air board says ozone machines have been sold as making "activated oxygen," "superoxygen" or "mountain-fresh air." The EPA names "energized oxygen" and "pure air." If a box says the machine gives off something like this, put it back.

Other kinds can give off ozone too. The EPA says some units with ionizers, electrostatic precipitators, plasma, or UV lights without adequate lamp coatings "may have the potential to emit ozone."

Your nose is not a test. The EPA says a "lack of odor does not guarantee that levels are safe."

What does "CARB certified" mean on an air purifier?

Since 2010, every portable indoor air cleaner sold in California must be certified by CARB, the state's air board. All of them are tested for electrical safety. Electronic ones are also tested for ozone and must stay at or under 0.050 parts per million.

CARB calls filter-only units "Mechanical." It says they "do not generate ozone or ions."

Here is what the list does not tell you. CARB says its certification "does not reflect air cleaner effectiveness or 'health safety.'" It says it "does not imply that a specific air cleaner is 'safe to use.'" And the EPA says it "does not certify or recommend specific brands or models."

So the CARB list helps with ozone. The CADR helps with size. Neither one proves a purifier is safe.

Does ENERGY STAR mean an air purifier cleans well?

No. The EPA says ENERGY STAR on an air cleaner means it meets energy efficiency guidelines. It says it "does not refer to its air-cleaning effectiveness in any way."

Sources: US EPA, Guide to Air Cleaners in the Home; US EPA, Ozone Generators that are Sold as Air Cleaners, last updated February 19, 2026; California Air Resources Board, Air Cleaning Devices for the Home; California Air Resources Board, List of CARB-Certified Air Cleaning Devices.

How do I get more from the purifier I already own?

Quick answer: run it on high in the room you use most, and keep the filter fresh.

  • Run it higher and longer. The EPA says the CADR is "typically measured at the air cleaner's highest speed." On a lower speed, it filters less air. So on low, it usually does less than the number on the box. Running it longer filters more air.
  • Put it where you sit. The EPA says to put it in the room you spend the most time in. Keep it away from curtains and anything that blocks the airflow.
  • Change the filter. "All filters need regular replacement," the EPA says. "If a filter is dirty and overloaded, it won't work well."

What should I do instead of trusting the box?

Quick answer: take out what puts things into the air, then open a window. Let the purifier be the helper.

The EPA says, "The most effective ways to improve your indoor air are to reduce or remove the sources of pollutants and to ventilate with clean outdoor air."

Here are plain swaps that follow that order:

  • Swap a scented plug-in for an empty outlet. Unplug it tonight. Here is what a plug-in sends into your room.
  • Swap the room spray for an open window. Letting in clean outdoor air is the EPA's second step.
  • Swap an ozone or "activated oxygen" machine for a filter-only purifier. Pick one with a CADR that fits your room's square feet.
  • For smells and fumes, pick carbon. Most filters are made for either particles or gases, the EPA says. For gases, it says to choose an activated carbon filter, and "the thicker the better." It says research has not yet shown that plasma, photocatalytic oxidation or UV light remove gases well in home units.

Common questions about air purifiers

Does "True HEPA" mean an air purifier really works?

Not by itself. "True HEPA" is the company's own claim about its filter, and you cannot test it at home. The EPA says a HEPA filter can in theory remove at least 99.97% of particles 0.3 microns in size. To know if the purifier fits your room, check the CADR and the room size on the box.

What CADR do I need for my room?

Multiply the room's length by its width to get square feet. On the EPA's chart, a 300 square foot room needs a CADR of at least 195, and a 400 square foot room needs at least 260. The chart assumes an 8 foot ceiling, so go bigger for higher ceilings or an open floor plan.

Do ionizer air purifiers give off ozone?

Some can. The EPA says ionizers, electrostatic precipitators, plasma air cleaners and UV lights without adequate lamp coatings may have the potential to give off ozone. It calls ozone a lung irritant. California tests electronic air cleaners sold there for ozone, but it says its certification does not mean a unit is "safe to use."

Did a court find that Levoit filters were not HEPA?

No. Shoppers sued over the "True HEPA," "HEPA" and "H13" claims on Levoit Core and EverestAir purifiers and filters. Vesync, the company behind Levoit, denies all of the allegations, and the court has not decided who is right. It is a proposed settlement, not a verdict. Claims close November 3, 2026.

Sources for these answers: EPA, What is a HEPA filter?; EPA, Guide to Air Cleaners in the Home; CARB, List of CARB-Certified Air Cleaning Devices; official settlement notice.

What can I do this week?

Tonight, measure the room where your purifier sits. Multiply the length by the width. Then find the CADR on the box or the maker's page, and check it against the EPA chart above.

If it is too small, move it to a smaller room. Run it on high when you can. Then unplug one scented thing in that same room and open the window for a few minutes.
