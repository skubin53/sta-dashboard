Are Plug-In Air Fresheners Safe?

What researchers found coming out of one, what the label cannot tell you, and how to get a fresh house without it.

What did researchers find coming out of a plug-in?

Quick answer: more than 20 chemicals into the air, and none of them named on the box.

In 2008, Anne Steinemann, then a professor at the University of Washington, tested six top-selling scented products. Three were air fresheners: a solid disk, a spray and a plug-in oil. Each one sat in a closed space at room temperature while the air around it was tested.

The plug-in gave off more than 20 volatile organic compounds. Seven of them are regulated as toxic or hazardous under federal law. Across all six products she found nearly 100. They included acetone, limonene, acetaldehyde, chloromethane and 1,4-dioxane.

Five of the six gave off at least one chemical the EPA classes as a cancer-causing hazardous air pollutant, she said.

Source: University of Washington, Toxic chemicals found in common laundry products, air fresheners (July 24, 2008). The study was published in Environmental Impact Assessment Review.

What the study does not tell you

The release says it plainly: "This study does not address links between exposure to chemicals and health effects." It tested one product of each kind, and it did not name the brands. So it tells you what came out. It does not tell you what that did to anyone.

Why can't I tell what is in it from the label?

Quick answer: fragrance is a blend the label does not have to spell out.

The plug-in in that study listed no ingredients at all. Its safety data sheet, the paper written for people who handle chemicals at work, called the contents a "mixture of perfume oils." At the time, the researcher noted that no law required any product to list the chemicals in its fragrance.

Some big brands now post more fragrance detail on their websites. On the package in your hand, fragrance is still usually one word. That is why reading harder is not the answer. Choosing a company you trust is.

How many people say air fresheners bother them?

Quick answer: about one in five adults in a national survey.

In a 2016 survey of 1,136 American adults, 20.4 percent said they had health problems when they were around air fresheners or deodorizers. The most common were breathing problems at 9.5 percent and migraine headaches at 7.2 percent. Another 4.7 percent reported asthma attacks.

Across all scented products, 34.7 percent reported health problems. These are people's own reports, not lab tests, so they show how common the complaint is, not what caused it.

Source: Steinemann, Fragranced consumer products: exposures and effects from emissions, Air Quality, Atmosphere and Health (2016), and the full text.

What does the EPA say about air fresheners and indoor air?

Quick answer: they are on its list of household sources of VOCs, and fresh air is its first step.

The EPA lists air fresheners among the household products that give off VOCs. It says levels of several of these chemicals average 2 to 5 times higher indoors than outdoors. Its first step to cut exposure is simple: increase ventilation when you use products that give them off.

Source: US EPA, Volatile Organic Compounds' Impact on Indoor Air Quality.

How do I make my house smell fresh without one?

Quick answer: remove the smell instead of covering it.

Find the source. It is usually the trash, the drain, damp towels, the dog's bed or the fridge. Fix that one thing and the room stops needing a scent.

Let air move. Open two windows across from each other for ten minutes. Run the bathroom and kitchen fans. The researcher behind the 2008 study said she would recommend ventilation instead of air fresheners.

Soak it up. A box of baking soda in the fridge or by the litter box takes a smell away instead of adding a new one on top.

Then look at what you clean with. A strong clean smell fills the same room. I go deeper on sprays in Is Febreze safe to breathe? and room by room in Are air fresheners toxic?
