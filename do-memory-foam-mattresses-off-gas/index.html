Do Memory Foam Mattresses Off-Gas?

What is off-gassing?

Off-gassing is a plain word for chemicals leaving a solid thing as a gas. The EPA says VOCs are given off as gases from certain solids or liquids, and it lists building materials and furnishings among the sources.

New things do it most. The Minnesota Department of Health says household furnishings tend to off-gas more VOCs when they are new, and that chemicals off-gas more in heat and humidity. Consumer Reports says a new mattress may give off a chemical odor that could linger for a few days.

Here is the bigger picture. The EPA says many VOCs are consistently higher indoors than outdoors, up to ten times higher. That figure is about VOCs in general, from every source in a home, not about mattresses. For another common source of scent in the air at home, see Are air fresheners toxic?

Does the smell mean it is harmful?

Not by itself. The Minnesota Department of Health says VOCs may or may not be able to be smelled. It says smelling is not a good indicator of health risk. And the EPA says that, at present, not much is known about what health effects occur from the levels of organics usually found in homes. So treat the smell as a cue to open a window, not a reason to panic.

Sources: US EPA, Volatile Organic Compounds' Impact on Indoor Air Quality; Minnesota Department of Health, Volatile Organic Compounds in Your Home; Consumer Reports, VOCs and Other Toxic Chemicals in Mattresses: What to Know (updated November 2025).

What did researchers measure in memory foam mattresses?

The most useful study we found on memory foam mattresses is a 2022 paper in the journal Chemosphere. Researchers set up two new memory foam mattresses in a simulated consumer use setting and tracked the air for 32 days. They sampled the air for a range of VOCs. The chemicals tied to the mattresses were 2-propanol, acetone, chloromethane and toluene, and they also tracked total VOCs.

Levels in the air peaked during the first day after the mattresses were set up, then kept falling over the next 31 days.

Averaged over the 32 days, the researchers' model put total VOCs at about 20 and 33 micrograms per cubic meter for the two mattresses. Averaged over a full year, the modeled figures were 2.7 and 4.2 micrograms per cubic meter.

The authors say each chemical they measured and modeled was well below available health-based benchmarks. Total VOCs were within available indoor air quality recommendations. They say this suggests the mattress models they tested are unlikely to pose a health risk to consumers.

What are the limits of that study?

Three honest caveats. Only two mattresses were tested, and the abstract does not name the brands. The one-year numbers come from a model, not a full year of testing. And all five authors list Cardno ChemRisk as their affiliation. The full paper is behind a paywall, so we could not check who paid for the work, and this page quotes only the published abstract.

Source: Beckett, Miller, Unice, Russman and Pierce, Evaluation of volatile organic compound (VOC) emissions from memory foam mattresses and potential implications for consumer health risk, Chemosphere, vol. 303, article 134945 (2022), DOI 10.1016/j.chemosphere.2022.134945.

Does body heat make it worse?

In lab tests, yes. Researchers at the Technion in Israel tested pieces of eight polyurethane mattresses in lab chambers, changing the temperature, humidity and carbon dioxide to match the air right around a sleeping person. Under those sleeping conditions, VOC emissions rose significantly, and heat seemed to be the biggest driver, more than humidity or carbon dioxide.

One caveat: the mattresses in that study were children's mattresses, not adult ones, so we use it only for this one point about heat. The American Chemical Society's press release on the study says it in plain words: the mattresses released more VOCs when temperature was elevated to simulate body heat.

Health agencies say the same about VOCs in general. The Minnesota Department of Health says chemicals off-gas more in high temperatures and humidity. The EPA's indoor air guide, The Inside Story, says high temperature and humidity can raise the levels of some pollutants. So keep the room cool while a new mattress airs out. The Minnesota Department of Health suggests keeping temperature and humidity as low as is comfortable.

Sources: Oz et al., Volatile Organic Compound Emissions from Polyurethane Mattresses under Variable Environmental Conditions, Environmental Science and Technology 53(15):9171-9180 (2019), DOI 10.1021/acs.est.9b01557; American Chemical Society, Mattresses could emit higher levels of VOCs during sleep (press release, via ScienceDaily, July 2019); Minnesota Department of Health, Volatile Organic Compounds in Your Home; US EPA and US CPSC, The Inside Story: A Guide to Indoor Air Quality.

How long does the new mattress smell last?

The highest levels come early. In the 2022 memory foam study, levels peaked on day one. The researchers' model found emissions of each chemical then fell in two steps. First came a fast drop: emissions fell by half in about 4 or 12 hours. Then came a slow fade: emissions fell by half about every 24 days.

In plain words: a fast drop at first, then a much slower fade through the rest of the 32-day study. Consumer Reports says the odor could linger for a few days.

You will see exact airing times on store and brand websites. We did not find a study or health agency that backs a set number of hours or days. The honest answer is that the first days matter most, and fresh air helps the whole way through.

How do I air out a new mattress?

Keep it simple. The health agencies and Consumer Reports all point to the same fix: fresh air.

  1. Unpack it in a room where you can open a window. Consumer Reports says these compounds will dissipate if you crack open a window and let the mattress air out before you sleep on it.
  2. Move the air. The EPA's first tip for cutting VOC exposure is to increase ventilation when using products that give off VOCs. Its indoor air guide says opening windows and doors, or running a window or attic fan when the weather permits, raises the amount of outdoor air coming in.
  3. Give it time before the first night if you can. In the memory foam study, levels were highest on the first day, so if you have a spare bed, let the new one air out before you sleep on it.
  4. Keep the fresh air coming for the first few weeks. The Minnesota Department of Health says more fresh air in your home helps lower the amount of VOCs indoors, and the slow fade in the study went on for weeks, not hours.
  5. Next time, ask about a floor model. When you buy new items, the Minnesota Department of Health suggests looking for floor models that have been allowed to off-gas in the store.

Sources: Consumer Reports, VOCs and Other Toxic Chemicals in Mattresses: What to Know; US EPA, Volatile Organic Compounds' Impact on Indoor Air Quality; US EPA and US CPSC, The Inside Story; Minnesota Department of Health, Volatile Organic Compounds in Your Home; Beckett et al., Chemosphere (2022).

What should I look for next time?

Two labels to know are CertiPUR-US and GREENGUARD Gold. Both are useful, and each one covers less than you might think.

What does CertiPUR-US mean?

CertiPUR-US is a program of the nonprofit Alliance for Flexible Polyurethane Foam. Its guidelines say certified foam is made without formaldehyde, ozone depleters, mercury, lead and other heavy metals. It is also made without the phthalates regulated by the US Consumer Product Safety Commission. And it has low VOC emissions, marketed as less than 0.5 parts per million. Phthalates are one of the chemical families covered in Endocrine disruptors in your home.

The real limit is set a different way, in milligrams per cubic meter of air in a lab chamber. Total VOCs must be under 0.5. Formaldehyde must be under 0.015. Benzene and toluene must each be under 0.5. The guidelines say these limits work out lower than 0.5 parts per million.

Three things the label does not tell you:

  • It is tested at room temperature, not body heat. The foam sample sits in the test chamber for 72 hours at about 23 degrees Celsius (about 73 degrees Fahrenheit) before it is measured.
  • It covers the foam only. The program says it does not certify furniture, product lines or companies, only flexible polyurethane foam. Consumer Reports adds that it does not cover any other materials in the mattress.
  • It is not a promise of no flame retardants. The guidelines ban a named list of flame retardants. It includes PBDEs, HBCD, PBBs and the Tris flame retardants TDCPP, TCEP, TEPA and TDBPP. The same guidelines list conventional flame retardant foams as a foam family that can be certified. So certified foam can still contain flame retardants the program does not ban. The program's FAQ does not say certified foam has no flame retardants at all. It points readers to that banned list.

What does GREENGUARD Gold mean?

GREENGUARD Gold is the stricter tier of a certification run by UL Solutions. UL says Gold sets even lower emission limits. It limits emissions of more than 360 VOCs and total chemical emissions. Products must also meet California's chamber-testing method, known as California Section 01350.

In a UL criteria table for furniture and mattresses, GREENGUARD Gold allows up to 220 micrograms per cubic meter of total VOCs, compared with 500 for the standard tier. For formaldehyde, the Gold limit is 9 micrograms per cubic meter, compared with 61.3 for the standard tier. That table is dated 2013, and we could not open the current standard, so check the product's own listing for today's limits.

Consumer Reports puts it well: a GREENGUARD label does not guarantee no VOCs, only that a mattress has low chemical emissions.

What about natural latex or wool?

Here is the honest answer. We did not find a study that tested natural latex or wool mattresses side by side with memory foam. No source we opened said whether memory foam gives off more or less than other foams. So we will not claim one is cleaner than another.

What you can do is ask better questions. Ask what the whole mattress is made of, not just the foam, because CertiPUR-US covers only the foam. Ask whether the finished mattress carries GREENGUARD Gold, which speaks to the mattress's emissions. Choose American made where you can. And whatever you pick, give it fresh air when it arrives.

What can I do this week?

Start small. If a new mattress is on the way, unpack it in a room with a window you can open. Give it that first day before you sleep on it. If yours is already on the bed, crack the window for the next few weeks and run a fan when the weather allows. Next time you shop, ask what the whole mattress is made of, and read the label for what it really covers.
