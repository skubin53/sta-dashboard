Is There a US Legal Limit for Pesticide on Strawberries?

Who sets the limit, what the number is for fludioxonil, and what that number does not tell you about the box in your fridge.

Who sets the pesticide limit on strawberries in the US?

Quick answer: the EPA. It sets the limits pesticide by pesticide and food by food. Some pesticides are exempt.

The EPA says it "regulates the amount of each pesticide that may remain in and on foods." It calls those limits "tolerances."

In the EPA's words, a tolerance, "or maximum residue limit," is "the amount of pesticide residue allowed to remain in or on each treated food commodity."

The EPA says the tolerance applies to food grown here and to food brought in from other countries.

Who brings the proof? The EPA says pesticide companies "must submit a wide variety of scientific studies" before it sets a tolerance.

Source: EPA, Setting Tolerances for Pesticide Residues in Foods, last updated June 25, 2026.

What does the limit actually do?

Quick answer: it is the trigger for action. Food found over it can be taken off the market.

The EPA says: "The tolerance is the residue level that triggers enforcement actions. That is, if residues are found above that level, the commodity will be subject to seizure by the government."

So the limit is a line in the sand. It does not tell you what is on any one box.

What is the US limit for fludioxonil on strawberries?

Quick answer: 3.0 parts per million.

Fludioxonil is a fungicide. Its limits are in a federal rule, 40 CFR 180.516. The table lists "Berry, low growing, subgroup 13-07G, except cranberry" at 3.0 parts per million.

Where are strawberries in that? A second rule, 40 CFR 180.41, lists "Crop Subgroup 13-07G. Low growing berry subgroup." Strawberry is the main crop named for that group.

The federal rules site showed this text as up to date on November 12, 2026. Limits change by new rules, so the date matters.

Sources: 40 CFR 180.516, Fludioxonil; tolerances for residues; 40 CFR 180.41, Crop group tables; all limits are listed in 40 CFR part 180. eCFR, title 40, up to date as of November 12, 2026.

How does this fit the Driscoll's lawsuit?

Quick answer: the case is about what shoppers were told when they paid. It is not a ruling on any limit.

Six shoppers sued Driscoll's in federal court in Northern California on June 26, 2026. Their complaint lists 12 pesticides it says lab testing found on the berries. An online report wrote up that testing in May 2026. It calls eight of them PFAS.

For fludioxonil, the complaint lists 60 parts per billion. That number comes from the complaint, not from a court or an agency.

The complaint also points to limits set in other countries. It says those foreign limits are "not alleged herein as independent legal duties" in the US.

What it does say: the clamshell carries the slogan "Only the Finest Berries," and the shoppers say they would not have bought the berries, or would have paid less, if they had been told. These are allegations. No court has ruled.

Is a berry under the limit a clean berry?

Quick answer: no one can tell you that from a limit.

A limit is set for one pesticide at a time. The complaint lists 12 from a single report. And a limit does not tell you what is on the box in your hand.

That is why I would rather you know the person who grew your fruit.

What can I do instead?

Quick answer: find a strawberry farm near you and ask the farmer what goes on the plants.

  1. Find a farm close to home. Look for a pick-your-own strawberry farm or a farm stand within a short drive.
  2. Call before the season starts. Ask what goes on the plants, and when. A farmer can answer that.
  3. Go pick your own. Bring a basket. Now you know a person, not a slogan.

I will not promise a farm berry is perfect. I promise you can ask the person who grew it.
