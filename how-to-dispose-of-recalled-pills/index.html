How Do I Get Rid of a Recalled Bottle of Pills?

Your lot number matches. Now what? Who to call first, where the FDA says unused pills should go, why plain acetaminophen does not go down the toilet, and what to buy next time.

By Shannon Nicole, founder of Switch to America. Seven years helping women switch to a toxin-free home, and a health coach for over 20 years. Published November 2, 2026.

Set the bottle aside, do not take the pills, and contact the seller first. Then, the FDA says the best way to get rid of most unused medicines is a drug take-back program, like a drop-off box or a prepaid mail-back envelope. For a recalled bottle of plain acetaminophen, the order is simple: a take-back box first, the home trash second, and the toilet never.

What do I do first when my lot number matches?

Quick answer: do not take it, set it aside, and contact the seller. For the Amazon acetaminophen recall, that means Amazon customer service.

The bottle in the news this fall is Amazon's own acetaminophen. On September 18, 2026, Aurobindo Pharma USA recalled Amazon Basic Care Extra Strength 500 mg caplets. The federal recall record gives the reason as "Discoloration: consumer complaints received for discolored tablets."

Fox Business listed seven lots in three bottle sizes on October 14, 2026, citing the FDA. That is more than 112,000 bottles, by its count.

  • 200 count: ACD125040A
  • 500 count: ACD124036A, ACD124030A, ACD124042A, ACD124048B and ACD125010A
  • 1,000 count: ACD124048A

Match every letter and number, right to the end. ACD124048A and ACD124048B are two lots, in two sizes.

If yours matches, do not take it. CNN reported that customers "are asked to contact Amazon customer service for a remedy." Put the bottle up high, away from your other medicines, until you hear back.

If any pill in any acetaminophen bottle looks spotted or off color, do not take that one either. Fox Business reported that the FDA's information "does not identify the cause of the discoloration." It also reported that the agency did not describe any specific health risks or say whether any injuries had been reported.

Before the pills leave your house, snap a photo of the lot number and EXP date. Then you still have them after the bottle is gone.

Where does the FDA say unused pills should go?

Quick answer: to a drug take-back program. That can be a drop-off box or a prepaid mail-back envelope.

The FDA puts it plainly: "The best way to dispose of most types of expired, unwanted or unused medicines is through a drug take-back program." It says this goes for prescription and over-the-counter medicines.

The FDA lists two ways to do it:

  • Drop them off at a drug take-back location. "Some pharmacies offer on-site medicine drop-off boxes or kiosks," the FDA says.
  • Mail them in a prepaid drug mail-back envelope. The FDA says these are sold at some pharmacies and online, and "Some pharmacies offer them at no cost."

The FDA also says that all medicines dropped off at take-back locations "will be destroyed."

How do I find a drop-off box near me?

The FDA says take-back locations may be at pharmacies in stores, hospitals and clinics, and at law enforcement offices. You can search for one with the DEA's Year-Round Drop-Off Locations tool. Or ask your pharmacist if they take pills back or offer mail-back envelopes.

The DEA also holds National Prescription Drug Take Back Days. On those days, temporary drop-off sites open in communities across the country, the FDA says. Local police may hold their own take-back events, too.

Sources: FDA, Where and How to Dispose of Unused Medicines, content current as of April 16, 2025; FDA, Drug Disposal: Drug Take-Back Options, content current as of October 31, 2024; DEA Diversion Control Division, Year-Round Drop-Off Locations search.

Can I throw recalled pills in the trash?

Quick answer: yes, if you cannot get to a take-back option. The FDA gives clear steps.

The FDA says that when a take-back option is not available, "almost all medicines, except those on the FDA flush list, can be thrown into your household trash." Plain acetaminophen is not on the flush list. For a recalled bottle, do this after you have contacted the seller.

Here are the FDA's steps, in plain words:

  1. Take the pills out of the bottle.
  2. Do not crush them. The FDA says "do not crush tablets or capsules."
  3. Mix them with something unpleasant, like used coffee grounds, dirt or cat litter.
  4. Put the mix in something you can close, like a zipper bag or an empty can.
  5. Throw it in your trash at home.
  6. Scratch out your personal information on the empty bottle. Then throw the bottle away or recycle it.

Why the coffee grounds? The FDA says the mix makes the pills "unrecognizable to someone who might intentionally go through the trash looking for drugs."

Sources: FDA, Where and How to Dispose of Unused Medicines, content current as of April 16, 2025; FDA, Drug Disposal: Dispose "Non-Flush List" Medicine in Trash, content current as of October 31, 2024.

Can I flush recalled acetaminophen down the toilet?

Quick answer: no. Plain acetaminophen is not on the FDA flush list.

The FDA says, "Remember, don't flush your medicine unless it is on the flush list." That means not down the toilet and not down the sink.

The flush list is short. It is for medicines "sought-after for their misuse and/or abuse potential" that "can result in death from one dose if inappropriately taken." Most of them are opioids. The word acetaminophen is not on the list.

What about a parent's prescription pain pills?

Some prescription pain pills mix acetaminophen with an opioid, like hydrocodone. The flush list covers any drug that contains the word "hydrocodone" or "oxycodone," along with other opioids. So those pills are on the list, because of the opioid.

Even then, the FDA says to use a take-back option first and to flush only if one is not readily available. If a doctor or pharmacist gave you disposal steps for a medicine, the FDA says to follow those.

Sources: FDA, Drug Disposal: FDA's Flush List for Certain Medicines, content current as of October 31, 2024; FDA, label for hydrocodone bitartrate and acetaminophen tablets.

What should I buy instead next time?

Quick answer: buy straight from the company that made it, with its own name on the front.

The recalled bottle says Amazon on the front. CNN reported the pills "are made by Aurobindo Pharma but sold under Amazon's private label." So the name on the bottle told you who sold it, not who made it.

Next time, buy straight from the manufacturer. Pick a company whose own name is on the front. Pick one that answers the phone when something looks wrong.

Buy a bottle size you will use up. If it is ever pulled, there are fewer pills to get rid of.

Buying straight from the maker will not make any pill perfect. But you will know who made it and who to call.

Should I crush the pills before I throw them out?

No. The FDA's trash steps say "do not crush tablets or capsules." Take the pills out of the bottle, mix them with used coffee grounds, dirt or cat litter, and seal the mix in a bag or can.
