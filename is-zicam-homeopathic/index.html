Is Zicam Homeopathic?

What the word on the box means, whether the FDA ever approved the nasal swabs, and what to reach for instead when a cold starts.

By Shannon Nicole, founder of Switch to America. Seven years helping women switch to a toxin-free home, and a health coach for over 20 years. Published October 30, 2026.

On a cold remedy box, "homeopathic" does not mean natural, gentle or FDA approved.

Is Zicam homeopathic?

Quick answer: yes. The nasal swab is, and Zicam says all of its Cold Remedy products are.

The swab carton says "ZINC-FREE HOMEOPATHIC" in capital letters. It is on the label that Church & Dwight, the company behind Zicam, filed with the FDA. That label is posted on DailyMed, the drug label site run by the National Library of Medicine.

The federal recall record for the August 2026 swab recall describes the same box. It calls it "Zinc-Free Homeopathic."

Zicam says it, too. Its website says, "All of our Cold Remedy products are homeopathic." The title of its home page calls them "Homeopathic Cold Remedies."

Not every Zicam product is homeopathic. In the FDA's drug listing data, the RapidMelts, lozenges and fruit drops are listed as "unapproved homeopathic." Two Zicam nasal sprays for a stuffy nose are not. They use a decongestant drug called oxymetazoline.

What is in the Zicam swab?

The label lists three active ingredients: Galphimia glauca, Luffa operculata and Sabadilla. Each one is marked "4x."

That mark is a homeopathic dilution. The FDA says homeopathic products often list ingredients "in terms of dilution, such as 1X, 6X, 2C."

The front of the carton says "SHORTENS COLDS." It calls the swab "THE ONLY COLD SHORTENING NASAL SWAB." Those are the company's own words.

Sources: DailyMed, Zicam Cold Remedy nasal swab label, updated June 1, 2026; federal recall record D-0790-2026; Zicam, About Homeopathy and home page; FDA drug listing data for Zicam, via openFDA; FDA, Homeopathic Products.

What does "homeopathic" mean?

Quick answer: it is an old healing theory from the late 1700s. It rests on the idea that like cures like, and on very heavy dilution.

The FDA calls homeopathy "an alternative medical practice that was developed in the late 1700s."

It rests on two ideas, the FDA says. The first is "like-cures-like." A substance that causes symptoms in a healthy person is diluted and used to treat similar symptoms in a sick person. The second is that the more a substance is diluted, the stronger it is supposed to be. The FDA says this is known as the "law of infinitesimals."

Zicam's own website explains homeopathy the same way, with "like cures like."

Source: FDA, Homeopathic Products, content current as of January 17, 2025.

Has the FDA ever approved Zicam nasal swabs?

Quick answer: no. Today's swab is listed as "unapproved homeopathic." In 2009, the FDA said the old zinc swabs could not be sold without its approval.

The FDA's page on homeopathic products is blunt: "There are no FDA-approved products labeled as homeopathic."

It goes on: "Homeopathic products marketed in the U.S. have not been reviewed by the FDA for safety and effectiveness to diagnose, treat, cure or prevent any diseases or conditions."

DailyMed lists the Zicam swab's marketing status as "unapproved homeopathic." Right under it, the site adds this note: "This homeopathic product has not been evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration for safety or efficacy. FDA is not aware of scientific evidence to support homeopathy as effective."

Zicam's website says homeopathic medicines "are regulated by the FDA." Regulated is not the same as approved.

What did the FDA say about Zicam swabs in 2009?

On June 16, 2009, the FDA warned people to stop using and throw out three Zicam nasal products that contained zinc. Two of them were swabs. The FDA said it had "more than 130 reports" of lost sense of smell linked to use of the three products. Zicam's own site says a clinical link to lost smell "was NOT established," and that it recalled those products "out of an abundance of caution."

The FDA also sent the maker, Matrixx Initiatives, a warning letter. The FDA said the letter advised the company "that these products cannot be marketed without FDA approval."

NPR reported that same day that "Zicam is considered a homeopathic remedy."

Today's swab is sold as zinc-free, so it is not the 2009 formula. But it is still a homeopathic swab, and it is still listed as unapproved.

Sources: FDA, Homeopathic Products; DailyMed listing; Zicam, About Homeopathy; FDA consumer update, Warnings on Three Zicam Intranasal Zinc Products, June 16, 2009, as archived by the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit; NPR, FDA: Avoid Zicam Nasal Gels, June 16, 2009.

What does the FTC say about homeopathic cold claims?

Quick answer: the FTC says they need the same proof as claims for any other drug. It also says most over-the-counter homeopathic drugs have no valid modern studies showing they work.

The FTC watches health ads. In 2016 it put out a policy on homeopathic drug claims. It was announced November 15, 2016, and printed in the Federal Register on December 13, 2016.

It says: "Efficacy and safety claims for homeopathic drugs are held to the same standards as similar claims for non-homeopathic drugs." For health claims, the FTC says it "has generally required" "competent and reliable scientific evidence."

The FTC also wrote that "for the vast majority of OTC homeopathic drugs," there are "no valid studies using current scientific methods showing the product's efficacy."

What if a company does not have that proof? The FTC says an ad might avoid misleading people by saying "there is no scientific evidence that the product works." It would also need to say the claims "are based only on theories of homeopathy from the 1700s that are not accepted by most modern medical experts."

This is a general policy. It does not name Zicam.

Does the FDA watch homeopathic products used in the nose more closely?

In general, yes. On December 6, 2022, the FDA issued guidance on where it will focus its enforcement for homeopathic products. One group on its list is products used some way other than by mouth or on the skin, such as products "taken nasally." Others include products with "reports of injury" and products with "significant quality issues."

This is a general policy, too. It does not name Zicam.

Sources: FTC, Enforcement Policy Statement on Marketing Claims for OTC Homeopathic Drugs, Federal Register, December 13, 2016 (FR Doc. 2016-29770); FDA, Homeopathic Products.

Does "homeopathic" mean natural or gentle?

Quick answer: no. It names a theory. It is not a promise about what is in the box.

The FDA says homeopathic products "are often marketed as natural, safe and effective alternatives" to other medicines. But it says they "have not been reviewed by the FDA for safety and effectiveness."

The FDA also says, as a general point, "Improper manufacturing of homeopathic products also can increase the potential for contamination." That is a general statement about homeopathic products. It is not the FDA's finding about either Zicam recall.

Why were Zicam swabs recalled?

Quick answer: fungus in 2025, and germ test results outside the limit in 2026. That is two swab recalls in 14 months.

On June 6, 2025, Church & Dwight began a Class I recall of 3,374,918 cartons of Zicam Cold Remedy Nasal Swabs. The federal record gives the reason as "Fungal contamination of nasal swabs." Class I is the most serious kind.

On August 10, 2026, it began a Class II recall of 318,600 cartons in three lots: UN52882, UN53431 and UN60411. The record says an "FDA inspection of the contract manufacturer noted out of limit results for microbiological testing."

Sources: federal recall record D-0504-2025; federal recall record D-0790-2026.

What can I use instead when a cold starts?

Quick answer: a hot mug of lemon, fresh ginger and honey from a farm near you.

When the scratch starts, put the kettle on. Slice half a lemon and a thumb of fresh ginger into a mug. Pour the hot water over and stir in a spoon of honey.

Buy the honey from a local farm. Ask the farmer where the hives sit. A farmer who is proud of it will tell you.

Will a mug stop a cold? I am not going to promise that. But nothing in it goes up your nose, and you know the farmer behind the honey.

Do one thing tonight. Take any Zicam swabs out of your medicine cabinet and put them in the trash.
