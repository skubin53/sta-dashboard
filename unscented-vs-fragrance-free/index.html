Does "Unscented" Mean Fragrance Free?

What each word on the front does and does not promise, what the FDA says about hidden fragrance, what a study of "fragrance free" moisturizers found, and what to do instead.

By Shannon Nicole, founder of Switch to America. Seven years helping women switch to a toxin-free home, and a health coach for over 20 years. Published October 28, 2026.

"Unscented" is a promise about smell. It is not a promise about what went into the bottle.

What is the difference between unscented and fragrance free?

Quick answer: "unscented" is about how a product smells. "Fragrance free" sounds like a promise about what was put in it.

A product can smell like nothing and still have fragrance in it. That is the gap. And as you will see, a "fragrance free" product can still hold ingredients tied to fragrance allergy.

What does "unscented" mean?

Quick answer: no noticeable smell. It does not mean no fragrance.

The FDA says it plainly on its page about fragrance: "Even some products labeled 'unscented' may contain fragrance ingredients. This is because the manufacturer may add just enough fragrance to mask the unpleasant smell of other ingredients, without giving the product a noticeable scent."

So a little fragrance can be used to cover up a smell. The product ends up smelling like nothing. And the front can still say unscented.

Does that cover deodorant?

Yes. The FDA lists deodorants as cosmetics. It says "deodorants that are also antiperspirants" count as both a cosmetic and a drug. Either way, the stick on your dresser falls under the FDA's cosmetic rules.

Sources: FDA, Fragrances in Cosmetics, content current as of February 28, 2022; FDA, Is It a Cosmetic, a Drug, or Both? (Or Is It Soap?), content current as of September 11, 2024.

Does "fragrance free" mean there is no fragrance?

Quick answer: it sounds that way. But one study found many "fragrance free" moisturizers still had ingredients tied to fragrance allergy.

In 2017, a team led by Northwestern University dermatologists published a study in JAMA Dermatology. They looked at the top 100 best-selling whole-body moisturizers at three big online stores: Amazon, Target and Walmart. That came to 174 different products.

Here is what they found, in their words: "For products with a claim of 'fragrance free,' 18 (45%) had at least 1 fragrance cross-reactor or botanical ingredient."

In plain words, nearly half of the moisturizers that said "fragrance free" still had at least one ingredient tied to fragrance allergy. It was either a plant ingredient that a skin allergy database links to fragrance, or an ingredient that can set off the same allergy fragrance does.

The same study found that "fragrance mix" was the most common allergen on its list. It was in 87 of the 174 products.

Two fair notes. This was a moisturizer study, not a deodorant study. And the study's disclosure says two of its authors own a stake in an online resource that recommends products to shoppers.

Source: Xu and colleagues, "Consumer Preferences, Product Characteristics, and Potentially Allergenic Ingredients in Best-selling Moisturizers," JAMA Dermatology, November 1, 2017, volume 153, issue 11, pages 1099 to 1105.

Why can one word, "Fragrance," hide so much?

Quick answer: U.S. rules let a company list its whole scent mix as one word.

The FDA says that "under U.S. regulations, fragrance and flavor ingredients can be listed simply as 'Fragrance' or 'Flavor.'"

Here is why, in the FDA's own telling. The ingredient list law cannot be used to force a company to tell "trade secrets." And fragrance formulas are "complex mixtures of many different natural and synthetic chemical ingredients." So the whole mix can sit behind one word.

Who checks fragrance before it is sold?

Not the FDA. For fragrance ingredients in cosmetics, the FDA says, "The law does not require FDA approval before they go on the market." The company is responsible for its own product and its own label.

Allergy warnings work differently than on food, too. The FDA says it "does not have the same legal authority to require allergen labeling for cosmetics as for food."

Source: FDA, Fragrances in Cosmetics, sections on labeling of fragrance ingredients, safety requirements, and fragrance allergies and sensitivities.

What did the "unscented" deodorant lawsuit say?

Quick answer: a shopper alleged that a stick sold as Unscented had a scent ingredient in it.

On August 12, 2026, a New York shopper filed a class action against Procter & Gamble in federal court. Her complaint alleges that Native Unscented Deodorant contains "the fragrance ingredient Zingiber officinale (ginger) root extract."

The complaint calls ginger root extract "an aromatic essential oil" with "a warm, spicy, and fresh scent." It also lays out a price gap. By its numbers, the Unscented stick cost $5.28 an ounce. A scented Native stick cost $4.35 an ounce.

These are her allegations, not court findings. Notice that the ingredient in dispute is a plant extract. Plant ingredients linked to fragrance were part of what the 2017 study counted in "fragrance free" moisturizers, too.

Source: Class action complaint, Chiaravalloti v. The Procter & Gamble Company, US District Court, Southern District of New York, No. 1:26-cv-06902, filed August 12, 2026.

What should I use instead?

Quick answer: a cleaner product from a company that stands behind the word on the front.

  • Start with one product. Pick the one on your skin every single day, like the stick on your dresser. You do not have to redo your whole bathroom shelf tonight.
  • Choose a company that stands behind its word. If the front says unscented or fragrance free, nothing should be added for scent. Not even a little, just to cover a smell.
  • Ask the company straight out. "Did you add any fragrance or scent ingredient, even just to cover a smell?" The FDA says shoppers with questions may contact the maker directly. A company that stands behind its word will give you a plain answer.

Common questions about unscented and fragrance free

Does "unscented" mean fragrance free?

No. The FDA says some products labeled "unscented" may contain fragrance ingredients. A maker may add just enough fragrance to mask the smell of other ingredients. The product then has no noticeable scent, but fragrance is still in it.

Does "fragrance free" mean nothing linked to fragrance is inside?

Not always. A 2017 study in JAMA Dermatology looked at best-selling moisturizers. Of the ones that claimed "fragrance free," 45% still had at least one fragrance-linked plant ingredient, or an ingredient that can set off the same allergy as fragrance. It was a moisturizer study, not a deodorant study.

What does "Fragrance" mean on an ingredient list?

It can stand for a whole mix. The FDA says that under U.S. rules, fragrance ingredients can be listed simply as "Fragrance." It says fragrance formulas are complex mixtures of many natural and synthetic chemicals, and the labeling law cannot be used to force a company to tell trade secrets.

Does the FDA approve fragrance ingredients before they are sold?

No. The FDA says the law does not require FDA approval before fragrance ingredients in cosmetics go on the market. It also says it does not have the same legal power to require allergy labels on cosmetics that it has for food.

Sources for these answers: FDA, Fragrances in Cosmetics; Xu and colleagues, JAMA Dermatology (2017).

What can I do this week?

Pick the one product you use every day that says "unscented" or "fragrance free." Ask the company if anything was added for scent, even to cover a smell. If you cannot get a straight answer, that tells you something.
