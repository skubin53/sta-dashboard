Is Your Shampoo Toxic?

The honest answer, the cancer concern that actually has a name, and the cleaner swap.

By Shannon Nicole, founder of Switch to America. Seven years helping women switch to a toxin-free home, and a health coach for over 20 years.

Here is the honest answer. Sulfates like SLS are strong detergents, not a poison on their own. But the most common one, sodium laureth sulfate, is made by a process that leaves behind a contaminant called 1,4-dioxane. The EPA lists 1,4-dioxane as likely to cause cancer in people, and California has kept it on its cancer list since 1988. That is the part the label never tells you, and it is the part worth knowing.

So do sulfates cause cancer?

Not the sulfate itself, and that is exactly why the internet scare backfires. Sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS) is a detergent. It is harsh, but it is not a carcinogen, and anyone can look that up in a minute and then wave off everything else you say. The real story is one step over. To make sulfates gentler and sudsier, manufacturers run them through a process called ethoxylation. That process creates 1,4-dioxane as a byproduct, and it rides along into the finished shampoo. 1,4-dioxane is the one the cancer agencies actually name.

The cancer concern in your shampoo is real. It has a name, and it is never on the label: 1,4-dioxane.

Sources: U.S. EPA IRIS assessment characterizing 1,4-dioxane as "likely to be carcinogenic to humans," EPA final risk evaluation; California OEHHA Proposition 65 listing for cancer, first listed 1988, OEHHA chemical page.

Then why is it not on the ingredient list?

Because a contaminant is not an added ingredient, and labeling law only requires the ingredients a company puts in on purpose. 1,4-dioxane is a leftover from how the product is made, so it can be present and never printed anywhere on the bottle. It does not have to be there. Companies can remove it cheaply with a step called vacuum stripping, and the cleanest brands do. But nothing in federal law forces them to, so plenty of everyday shampoos still carry a trace of it and simply do not mention it. That is the honest reason to care about how a product is made, not only what the label says.

The sulfate that is just plain harsh

Set the cancer question aside and SLS still earns its bad name. It is so reliably irritating to skin that dermatologists use it as the standard irritant control in patch testing, the known benchmark they measure other reactions against. In routine patch-test data, roughly one in five people show an irritant reaction to it. On dry, color-treated, or sensitive scalps it strips color faster and leaves hair dry and squeaky. If that is you, a gentler sulfate-free formula genuinely helps.

Source: Forkel et al., "Strengths and Limitations of Sodium Lauryl Sulfate as an Irritant Control in Patch Testing," Contact Dermatitis (2026). Read the study.

The other thing the label hides

Fragrance. On a shampoo label that single word can legally stand in for dozens of undisclosed ingredients, the same loophole that runs through air fresheners and cleaners, and it is a common trigger for an itchy scalp or a headache in the shower. Chasing "sulfate-free" while ignoring a long mystery-fragrance list only fixes half the problem. A short, readable ingredient list matters more than any one buzzword.

What to look for in a shampoo

  • Sulfate-free, and free of ethoxylated ingredients. That means no "laureth" and no "PEG" on the label. Skipping the ethoxylated ingredients is what keeps 1,4-dioxane out in the first place.
  • Fragrance-free or lightly, naturally scented. This closes the undisclosed-ingredient loophole, which is the honest win for most people.
  • A short label you can read, and American made. Fewer synthetic extras, a clear supply chain, and concentrated formats that come out cheaper per wash than the salon names.

Where to start

You do not need to throw out the bottle in your shower tonight. When it runs low, choose a fragrance-free, sulfate-free, American made shampoo that skips the ethoxylated ingredients, and give it two or three washes to settle. It is a small, once-a-month swap that quietly closes one of the doors most people never knew was open.
