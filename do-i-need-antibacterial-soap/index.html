Do You Need Antibacterial Soap?

The honest answer for everyday home use, what happened to triclosan, and the simpler hand soap swap.

By Shannon Nicole, founder of Switch to America. Seven years helping over 100 women switch to safer, American made home and personal care products.

For everyday use at home, you do not need antibacterial soap. Plain soap and water, used properly, cleans your hands just as well for healthy people, and the "antibacterial" version mostly added chemicals without adding proven protection. Here is the honest version, and the simpler swap.

Do you need antibacterial soap at home?

No, not for everyday home use. For years the "antibacterial" label was a marketing upgrade you paid for, but the protection did not hold up. In September 2016 a federal final rule removed 19 antibacterial active ingredients, including triclosan and triclocarban, from over-the-counter consumer hand soaps and body washes, because the companies selling them could not show they were any safer or more effective at preventing illness than plain soap and water. Companies were given a year to reformulate, and the rule covered consumer washes, not the products used in hospitals and other health care settings.

Source: "FDA's final rule on antibacterial soaps bans 19 ingredients," Food Safety News, September 2016. Read the reporting.

The "antibacterial" label added chemicals without adding proven everyday protection. Plain soap was doing the job the whole time.

What happened to triclosan?

Triclosan was the ingredient behind most "antibacterial" hand soaps, and it was the headline of that 2016 rule. Beyond the fact that it did not beat plain soap for everyday use, it had raised two other flags: animal studies pointed to possible hormone effects, and researchers worried that heavy household use could contribute to bacterial resistance. It also turned up widely in the environment and in people. None of that is a reason to panic about a bar you used last year, but it is why the everyday "antibacterial" claim quietly disappeared from most soap aisles.

How should you actually wash your hands?

The boring answer is the effective one: plain soap, clean running water, and about twenty seconds of scrubbing, front and back, between the fingers and under the nails. The mechanical act of lathering and rinsing is what lifts germs and grime off your skin and sends them down the drain. You do not need a special active ingredient to make that work.

What hand soap should I use instead?

  • Plain soap, not "antibacterial." You are not giving anything up. The everyday protection is the same, minus the extra chemistry.
  • Fragrance-free. "Fragrance" is one word that can legally stand in for dozens of undisclosed ingredients, on something you use many times a day. Skipping it removes that question entirely.
  • A short label you can read, and American made. A domestic supply chain, a clear ingredient list, and concentrated or refillable formats that usually come out cheaper per wash than the antibacterial drugstore names.

Where to start

You do not need to throw anything out in a hurry. Next time you restock, reach for a plain, fragrance-free, American made hand soap instead of the "antibacterial" one. It is one of the easiest swaps in the house, and it costs less, not more.
