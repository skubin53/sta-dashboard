Who Decides If a Cleaner Can Say "Non-Toxic"?

Is "non-toxic" a regulated word? What the federal guidance says, what it does not do, and how one cleaner lost the word.

By Shannon Nicole, founder of Switch to America. Seven years helping women switch to a toxin-free home, and a health coach for over 20 years. Published November 17, 2026.

The word "non-toxic" on a cleaner is not a government stamp of approval. For an everyday cleaner, the FTC's Green Guides say the company should have "competent and reliable scientific evidence" that it is non-toxic for people and the environment. But those guides are the FTC's views. They "do not operate to bind the FTC or the public." The word can be challenged after the bottle is sold, by the FTC or by a shopper in court. That is how a lawsuit took "Non-Toxic" off Krud Kutter.

On an everyday cleaner, "non-toxic" is the company's own promise. The rules ask for proof, but the fight over that proof starts after the bottle is on your shelf.

Is "non-toxic" a regulated word?

Quick answer: there is federal guidance on it, from the FTC. But it is guidance, not a sign-off.

The FTC's Green Guides have a section just for this word. It is 16 CFR 260.10, "Non-toxic claims." It says: "It is deceptive to misrepresent, directly or by implication, that a product, package, or service is non-toxic."

It also says what the word tells you. A non-toxic claim "likely conveys that a product, package, or service is non-toxic both for humans and for the environment generally."

So the company "should have competent and reliable scientific evidence" for it, or should "clearly and prominently qualify" the claim.

What does the FTC say about a cleaner that calls itself non-toxic?

The guide uses a cleaner as its own example. A cleaning product sold as "essentially non-toxic" and "practically non-toxic" likely tells you it "does not pose any risk to humans or the environment, including household pets."

And if that cleaner is safe for people but toxic to the environment, the FTC says the claim would be deceptive.

Source: 16 CFR 260.10, eCFR, Title 16 up to date as of November 13, 2026.

Who checks the word before it goes on the bottle?

Quick answer: the Green Guides are not a pre-check. The FTC can act after, and it has to prove its case.

The first section of the Green Guides, 16 CFR 260.1, says what kind of rule this is. The guides "set forth the Federal Trade Commission's current views about environmental claims."

It goes on: "They do not confer any rights on any person and do not operate to bind the FTC or the public."

The FTC "can take action under the FTC Act" when a claim does not line up with the guides. But in that case, the FTC "must prove that the challenged act or practice is unfair or deceptive."

Source: 16 CFR 260.1, eCFR, Title 16 up to date as of November 13, 2026.

Is the rule different for a disinfectant?

Quick answer: yes. A germ-killing disinfectant is held to a stricter rule.

The EPA counts disinfectants as antimicrobial pesticides, and it says general use disinfectants are the main kind used in homes. Its label rule for pesticides, 40 CFR 156.10, lists claims that count as misbranding.

Those include "safe," "nonpoisonous," "harmless" or "nontoxic to humans and pets," even "with or without such a qualifying phrase as 'when used as directed.'"

Sources: EPA, What are Antimicrobial Pesticides?; 40 CFR 156.10(a)(5)(ix), eCFR, Title 40 up to date as of November 13, 2026.

So how did "Non-Toxic" come off Krud Kutter?

Quick answer: a shopper's lawsuit, not the FTC. Rust-Oleum settled and admitted no wrongdoing.

On May 13, 2020, a California shopper sued Rust-Oleum over Krud Kutter. His complaint alleged that cleaners sold as "Non-Toxic" and "Earth Friendly" could harm people, animals and the environment. That is an allegation. Rust-Oleum "denies all of the allegations made in the Action," its settlement agreement says.

In court, Rust-Oleum said its front label read "Caution: Eye and Skin Irritant" right next to "Non-Toxic." US Magistrate Judge Laurel Beeler would not end the case early. Her January 26, 2024 order found "genuine disputes of fact remain on all issues."

Then Rust-Oleum settled. On October 2, 2025, the judge gave final approval. Rust-Oleum agreed "to permanently remove the 'Non-Toxic' representation from all Krud Kutter product labels and marketing." The order also says the deal "is not an admission of liability" by Rust-Oleum.

Common questions about "non-toxic" on cleaners

Is "non-toxic" a regulated word on cleaning products?

There is federal guidance, but it is not a stamp of approval. The FTC's Green Guides say a non-toxic claim likely means the product is non-toxic for people and for the environment, and that the company should have "competent and reliable scientific evidence" for it. The guides are the FTC's views and do not bind the public. The FTC can take action, but it must prove a claim is unfair or deceptive.

Can a disinfectant say "non-toxic" on the bottle?

A disinfectant is held to a stricter rule. The EPA counts household disinfectants as antimicrobial pesticides. Its label rule, 40 CFR 156.10, lists claims such as "nontoxic to humans and pets" as misbranding, even with the words "when used as directed."

Did the FTC say the Krud Kutter label was deceptive?

No. A shopper sued Rust-Oleum in 2020. Rust-Oleum denied the claims and settled. On October 2, 2025, a federal judge approved the deal, and Rust-Oleum agreed to remove "Non-Toxic" from all Krud Kutter labels and marketing. The order says the deal is not an admission of liability.

If a bottle says "non-toxic," does that mean it is safe?

No. It means the company is making that claim. Rust-Oleum told the court its Krud Kutter label said "Caution: Eye and Skin Irritant" right next to "Non-Toxic." Ask who stands behind the word before you trust it.
