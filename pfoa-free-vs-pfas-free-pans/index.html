Does "PFOA Free" on a Pan Mean It Has No PFAS?

What the words on a pan box do and do not promise, what California and Minnesota law say, and what to cook in instead.

"PFOA free" means one PFAS was left out. It does not mean the pan has no PFAS.

What is the difference between PFOA and PFAS?

Quick answer: PFAS is a big family of chemicals. PFOA is one member of that family.

PFAS is short for per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances. They are made by people, and they last a long time.

The EPA puts it plainly: "There are thousands of PFAS chemicals, and they are found in many different consumer, commercial, and industrial products."

PFOA is one of them. So when a box says "PFOA free," it is talking about one chemical out of thousands.

The slick coating on many nonstick pans is a different one, called PTFE. A California Senate committee report on the state's cookware law calls PTFE "a polymer form of PFAS."

Sources: US EPA, PFAS Explained, last updated September 9, 2026; California Senate Committee on Environmental Quality, analysis of AB 1200, hearing date July 7, 2021.

Why do so many pans say "PFOA free"?

Quick answer: eight big chemical makers told the EPA they met a goal to phase out PFOA by 2015. So it is an easy thing to promise.

In 2006, the EPA asked eight major companies to "work toward eliminating PFOA from emissions and product content no later than 2015."

The EPA says "All participating companies state that they met the PFOA Stewardship Program goals." It says most of them "stopped manufacture and import of long-chain PFAS, and then transitioned to alternative chemicals."

The EPA also says PFOA "may be produced, imported, and used by companies not participating" in the program.

So "PFOA free" often names a chemical that big makers already said they phased out. It tells you nothing about the chemicals in the coating now.

California lawmakers were told about this. A 2021 Senate committee report on the cookware law cites a report on pans that noted some labels say "PFOA free," "despite the fact that the cookware may contain PTFE or other PFAS chemicals."

Sources: US EPA, Fact Sheet: 2010/2015 PFOA Stewardship Program; California Senate Committee on Environmental Quality, analysis of AB 1200, 2021.

Can a pan box in California say "PFOA free"?

Quick answer: only if no PFAS at all was added to the pan on purpose.

California's cookware law is called AB 1200. Since January 1, 2024, this rule has applied to the pan's package: a maker "shall not make a claim that the cookware is free of any specific chemical if the chemical belongs to a chemical group or class identified on the designated list, unless no individual chemical from that chemical group or class is intentionally added to the cookware." The same rule has covered the pan's website since January 1, 2023.

In plain words, a box cannot brag about leaving out one member of a listed family if other members of that family were added on purpose.

Is PFAS on California's list?

Yes. The "designated list" is kept by California's Department of Toxic Substances Control. The state's Attorney General says it "includes many PFAS compounds."

Scientists at that department went further in a 2021 journal article. They wrote that "the entire class is on the DTSC's list of Candidate Chemicals." DTSC is that department, and its Candidate Chemicals list is the list the law points to.

Put together, a pan box in California can say "PFOA free" only if no PFAS was added to the pan on purpose.

The Attorney General warned makers in 2023 that his office may bring a case under this law, "seeking civil penalties, restitution, and injunctive relief."

One honest limit: this is a rule about what a box or website may claim for pans sold in California. It is not a safety test, and it does not prove any pan is safe.

Sources: California Attorney General, Enforcement Advisory on AB 1200, October 17, 2023; California Health and Safety Code section 109013; California Senate Committee on Environmental Quality, analysis of AB 1200, 2021; Bălan and others, California Department of Toxic Substances Control, "Regulating PFAS as a Chemical Class under the California Safer Consumer Products Program", Environmental Health Perspectives, February 17, 2021.

Which states ban PFAS in pans?

Quick answer: it depends on the state. Minnesota is one example, and its ban started January 1, 2025.

Minnesota law says "Beginning January 1, 2025, a person may not sell, offer for sale, or distribute for sale in this state the following products if the product contains intentionally added PFAS." Cookware is third on that list.

The law defines PFAS as "a class of fluorinated organic chemicals containing at least one fully fluorinated carbon atom." That is the whole family, not just PFOA.

The Minnesota Pollution Control Agency runs the law. It says it treats cookware as pots, pans and the other listed items that "include a component that has a nonstick coating containing intentionally added PFAS."

Other states have their own laws. Minnesota's Department of Health puts it this way: "Products that may contain them will vary by state due to differing state laws."

A ban in one state is not proof that any pan is safe. It only tells you what may be sold in that state.

Sources: Minnesota Statutes, section 116.943, subdivisions 1 and 5; Minnesota Pollution Control Agency, 2025 PFAS prohibitions for retailers and manufacturers, July 25, 2024; Minnesota Department of Health, PFAS and Products.

What does the Gotham Steel lawsuit say about "PFOA Free"?

Quick answer: the suit lists "PFOA Free" among the words it says were used to sell the pans. The company denies it, and no court has ruled.

Two buyers filed a class action in California on September 8, 2025. They sued E. Mishan & Sons, Inc. over Gotham Steel, Granite Stone and Bell & Howell cookware with stainless steel parts.

The suit lists words it says were used to sell those pans, like "Toxin Free" and "PFOA Free."

The suit alleges this was "despite the undisclosed presence of chromium, iron, nickel, lead, cadmium, PFOA, PFAS, PTFE, or PFOS."

Those are claims, not findings. The suit does not claim the pans hurt anyone. The court notice says "Defendant denies the allegations" and "The Court did not rule in favor of either side." Both sides agreed to settle. The final approval hearing is set for November 2, 2026.

What should I cook in instead?

Quick answer: a pan with no coating at all, like a plain cast iron skillet made in America.

A coated pan needs words on the box to explain the coating. A pan with no coating does not.

Plain cast iron is iron all the way through. It has no nonstick layer to wear thin.

Start with your egg pan. It is the one you reach for every morning.

Heat it slowly.Add a little oil or butter before the eggs. Wash it in hot water, dry it on the burner, and wipe on a thin coat of oil.

The eggs may stick at first. The more you cook in it, the slicker it gets.

Before you buy any new pan, ask one question: what is the cooking surface made of? If the answer is a nonstick coating, "PFOA free" is not the whole answer.
