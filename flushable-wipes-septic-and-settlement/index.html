Can You Flush "Flushable" Wipes With a Septic Tank?

What the EPA says your tank can take, what the FTC alleged about one wipe maker, and where the flushable wipes settlement stands as of October 2026.

By Shannon Nicole, founder of Switch to America. Seven years helping women switch to a toxin-free home, and a health coach for over 20 years. Published October 18, 2026.

Your septic tank is made for two things: human waste and toilet paper. A wipe is neither, no matter what the front of the pack says.

Can flushable wipes go in a septic tank?

Quick answer: no. The EPA says to flush only human waste and toilet paper.

The EPA has a plain page called "How to Care for Your Septic System." It says: "An easy rule of thumb is not to flush anything besides human waste and toilet paper."

It asks, "What can I flush down the toilet?" Its answer is one line: "Only flush human waste and toilet paper down the toilet."

Then it lists what never to flush. Non-flushable wipes are on that list, along with paper towels, dental floss and cat litter. The EPA says those items "could clog your septic system and cause a failure."

That list names non-flushable wipes. It does not say anything about wipes sold as flushable. But the rule names only two things you can flush, and a wipe is not one of them.

A septic tank is not a city sewer. It is a tank on your own land. The EPA puts it this way: "everything that goes down your drain ends up in your septic system."

Source: US EPA, How to Care for Your Septic System, last updated August 17, 2026.

Who said flushable wipes were safe for septic tanks?

Quick answer: some wipe packs did, according to the FTC and to buyers who sued. In 2015 the FTC alleged one maker could not back that up.

In May 2015, the Federal Trade Commission charged Nice-Pak, a wipe maker. The FTC alleged the company misled buyers by claiming one kind of its wipes "are safe for septic systems" and "break apart shortly after being flushed." It also alleged the company's tests did not match real home plumbing or septic conditions.

Nice-Pak made store brand wipes. The FTC said Costco, CVS, Target and BJ's Wholesale Club sold that kind of wipe under their own labels.

Jessica Rich, then the director of the FTC's Bureau of Consumer Protection, said it plainly: "If you claim a product is flushable, it needs to flush in the real world, without clogging household plumbing or sewer and septic systems."

Nice-Pak settled. There was no trial. The FTC made the order final on November 2, 2015. It says the company may not call its wipes flushable, or safe for sewer or septic systems, unless it can back that up. Its tests must match the real conditions where the wipes end up.

Sources: FTC, Wet Wipe Manufacturer Agrees To Substantiate "Flushability" Advertising Claims, May 18, 2015; FTC, FTC Approves Final Order, November 2, 2015.

What was the flushable wipes lawsuit about?

Quick answer: buyers said Kimberly-Clark's flushable claims were false. The company denies it, and no court has ruled on who is right.

The case is Kurtz v. Kimberly-Clark, in federal court in New York. It was filed in February 2014. The settlement covers flushable wipes from five Kimberly-Clark brands, including Cottonelle and Scott, bought from February 21, 2008 to May 19, 2022.

The buyers alleged it was false or misleading for the packs to say the wipes were "flushable," would "break up after flushing," and were safe for sewer and septic systems. The settlement's notice to buyers says the buyers claim the wipes "damaged or clogged plumbing pipes, septic systems, and sewage lines and pumps."

Here is the other side, straight. Kimberly-Clark denies all of it. It says its labels were truthful, and that the wipes "did not cause property damage in well-maintained plumbing systems." The same notice says, "The Court has not determined the merits of these arguments."

A settlement is not a finding. In this case, no court has ruled that the wipes clogged anything.

Source: Settlement Agreement and Long Form Notice, Kurtz v. Kimberly-Clark Corp., No. 1:14-cv-01142 (E.D.N.Y.), Document 432-1, filed April 5, 2022.

Did the flushable wipes lawsuit ever pay out?

Quick answer: not yet. A judge approved the settlement again in March 2026, and an objector appealed in April 2026.

How much did buyers actually claim?

The headline number is up to $20 million. The judge's March 2026 order tells the rest.

When the buyers' lawyers asked for final approval in 2022, "the total value of the compensation to the Class was $993,958.70." The order goes on: "The unpaid funds, or roughly $19 million, was to revert to Kimberly-Clark." Revert means go back to the company.

The same order approves $3,169,335.02 in fees for the buyers' lawyers. That money is paid separately from the buyers' money.

Each home could claim $1.10 a package with proof of purchase, up to $50.60. Without proof, it was 70 cents a package, up to $7.00.

When will buyers get paid?

The deal says approved claims are paid within 60 days after it becomes final. It is not final while an appeal is still open. Mr. Frank's appeal asks the court to throw out the approval. So no claim is paid while the appeal goes on. If the approval holds up, claims are paid after the appeal is over.

The time to file a claim is over, so there is nothing new to file.

Sources: Memorandum and Order, Kurtz v. Kimberly-Clark Corp., Document 501, March 12, 2026; Settlement Agreement, Document 432-1, sections 1.13, 2.4 and 2.9; Opening Brief of Appellant Theodore H. Frank, Second Circuit Nos. 26-946 and 26-947, July 17, 2026.

Does the settlement pay for a septic or plumbing repair?

Quick answer: no. It pays a small amount per package, not repair bills.

In the March 2026 order, the judge called this "a price-premium claim for an inexpensive consumer product, i.e., flushable wipes." In plain words, the money is about the extra price people paid, not about fixing pipes.

The deal also asks something of buyers. Once it is final, buyers in the class give up claims that "arise from or relate to the claims and allegations" in the lawsuits. The deal leaves out only claims for personal injury.

Mr. Frank's brief says this means buyers give up property damage claims too, like plumbing bills. That is his argument on appeal, not a ruling. If you have a repair bill you tie to wipes, talk with a lawyer in your state.

Sources: Memorandum and Order, Document 501, March 12, 2026; Settlement Agreement, Document 432-1, sections 1.24 and 7.1; Opening Brief of Appellant Theodore H. Frank, July 17, 2026.

What should I do instead?

Quick answer: keep the wipes if you like them. Just stop flushing them.

  • Put a small bin with a lid beside the toilet. Used wipes go there, not down the drain. It costs almost nothing and starts tonight.
  • Let toilet paper be the only paper that goes down. That is the EPA's rule for a septic tank.
  • Want a wet clean? Run plain toilet paper under warm water first. It is still toilet paper.
  • Get your tank checked. The EPA says the average household septic system "should be inspected at least every three years by a septic service professional." It says household tanks are typically pumped every three to five years.

What can I do this week?

Put the lidded bin by the toilet today. Then find the date of your last septic pumping. If it has been more than three years since anyone looked at your tank, book a check.
