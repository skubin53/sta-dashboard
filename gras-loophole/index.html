What Is the GRAS Loophole?

How a new food ingredient can reach your table without the FDA ever looking at it, what changed in 2025 and 2026, and what you can do at home.

By Shannon Nicole, founder of Switch to America. Seven years helping women switch to a toxin-free home, and a health coach for over 20 years. Published October 5, 2026.

The GRAS loophole is a rule that lets a company decide on its own that a new food ingredient is "generally recognized as safe," and then sell it without the FDA ever reviewing it. The company does not even have to tell the FDA. In 2013, Pew estimated that companies had put about 1,000 chemicals into food this way, and in August 2026 the FDA proposed making companies report them.

Under today's GRAS rules, the company selling a new food ingredient can decide it is safe, and it does not have to tell the FDA.

What does GRAS mean?

Quick answer: GRAS stands for "generally recognized as safe." It is a gap in the 1958 food additive law.

In 1958, Congress passed the Food Additives Amendment. It said a new food additive needs FDA review and approval before it is sold.

But the law left a door open. If qualified experts "generally recognize" a use of a substance as safe, that use does not count as a food additive. So it skips the review.

Pew calls it an exemption that was intended for common food ingredients. The federal GRAS rule (21 CFR 182.1) gives salt, pepper, vinegar and baking powder as its examples.

Sources: FDA, proposed rule on Substances Generally Recognized as Safe, Federal Register, August 11, 2026, which lays out the 1958 law; Pew Charitable Trusts, November 2013; 21 CFR 182.1.

How does an additive get into food without FDA review?

Quick answer: the company makes the safety call, and telling the FDA is optional.

  1. A company wants to use a new ingredient, or an old one in a new way.
  2. It has the safety data looked at. Pew found that firms usually use "their own employees, consultants or experts that they select and pay."
  3. It decides the use is GRAS.
  4. It may send the FDA a notice. It does not have to. The rule, 21 CFR 170.205, says a company "may notify FDA."
  5. The food goes on sale.

Even when a company does send a notice, the FDA does not approve the ingredient. The best answer a company can get is a "no questions" letter. That means the FDA does not question the company's reasons.

Why do people call it a loophole?

In 1997, the FDA proposed a voluntary notice program. In 2016, it made that program final. Voluntary means a company may tell the FDA, but it is not required to.

Government auditors flagged this years ago. In a 2010 report, the GAO said the FDA does not have information on other GRAS decisions "because companies are not required to inform FDA of them."

How many additives never got an FDA review?

Quick answer: no one knows for sure, and the FDA says so itself.

In 2013, Pew estimated that companies had decided about 1,000 chemicals were GRAS and used them without telling the FDA. Pew also found the FDA had not reviewed the safety of about 3,000 of the 10,000 additives allowed in food.

In its August 2026 proposal, the FDA wrote that "an unknown number of substances" are entering the market this way. It also said it cannot verify the outside estimates.

Some companies do choose to tell the FDA. More than 1,200 GRAS notices have been filed since 1998. The FDA says 617 of them came from foreign firms. Those are only the ones companies chose to send.

Is the FDA closing the GRAS loophole?

Quick answer: it has proposed to, but it is not final yet.

On March 10, 2025, HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. directed the FDA to explore a new rule. The goal was to end the path where companies use GRAS without telling the FDA why.

On August 11, 2026, the FDA published a proposed rule in the Federal Register. It would require companies to send a GRAS notice that explains their safety call. There would be some exceptions. The FDA says it would turn the voluntary program into a mandatory one.

It is still a proposal. Public comments are open until December 9, 2026, under docket number FDA-2025-N-3262. Until a final rule takes effect, telling the FDA stays voluntary. Nothing in your pantry changes on its own.

Source: FDA, Substances Generally Recognized as Safe, proposed rule, Federal Register, August 11, 2026. It describes the March 10, 2025 direction from the HHS Secretary.

How can I eat around the GRAS loophole?

Quick answer: cook more from whole foods, and buy fewer, simpler packaged foods.

You do not need to learn a list of chemical names. Most additives go into made foods, the kind that come in a box or a bag.

Cook from whole foods more often: meat, eggs, butter, fruit, vegetables, beans and rice. A whole food is one ingredient, so there is less room for extras.

When you do buy packaged food, buy fewer and simpler ones. Choose from an American made company you can trust.
