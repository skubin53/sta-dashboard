Does the FDA Check Panty Liners Before They Are Sold?

What the FDA requires before a liner is sold, what its rule lets pads skip, and who, if anyone, is testing liners for forever chemicals.

By Shannon Nicole, founder of Switch to America. Seven years helping women switch to a toxin-free home, and a health coach for over 20 years. Published November 3, 2026.

Not in the way you might think. The FDA regulates panty liners as medical devices, but its rule says a liner made of common pad materials does not need the filing the FDA reviews before some devices are sold. And the FDA's plan to have makers check all period products for contaminants is still only a draft, so a liner on the shelf is not proof that anyone tested it for PFAS.

Registered with the FDA is not the same as tested for forever chemicals.

Does the FDA check panty liners before they are sold?

Quick answer: the FDA sets rules that makers must follow. But a liner made of common pad materials does not have to go through the FDA's premarket filing.

To the FDA, a liner is a pad. Its page on pads says, "A panty liner, or liner, is a thinner version of this kind of menstrual pad." It also says, "Menstrual pads are regulated by the FDA." Its main page on period products says they are regulated "as medical devices."

The FDA says that before pads "can be legally sold in the United States," makers must do these things:

  • "Register and list the product with the FDA."
  • Follow FDA rules for "good manufacturing practices." These cover how the factory makes the product.
  • Report "device malfunctions and adverse events." That means problems with the product, and bad reactions.
  • Meet "FDA labeling requirements for medical devices."

Look at what is not on that list. None of those steps, as the FDA lists them, is a lab test for forever chemicals.

What does the federal rule say about liners?

Two federal rules cover pads. One is 21 CFR 884.5435, for unscented pads. The other is 21 CFR 884.5425, for scented ones. Both put pads "made of common cellulosic and synthetic material with an established safety profile" in Class I. Cellulosic means made from plant fiber. That wording is how the rule sorts pads. It does not mean a lab tested them for PFAS.

For those pads, both rules say the same thing. They are "exempt from the premarket notification procedures." A premarket notification, also called a 510(k), is a filing a maker sends the FDA before it sells some devices. Exempt means the maker does not have to send one.

There are limits, set out in 21 CFR 884.9. A maker must still file if its pad is meant for a different use, or works in a different basic way, than pads already sold.

Which period products does the FDA review before they are sold?

On its main page about period products, the FDA names tampons. It says, "Certain menstrual products, like tampons, are subject to FDA review." It does not name pads or liners in that line.

Sources: FDA, Menstrual Pads and Liners, content current as of July 6, 2026; FDA, Menstrual Product Options, Facts, and Safe Use, updated July 6, 2026; 21 CFR 884.5435; 21 CFR 884.5425; 21 CFR 884.9.

Is anyone testing panty liners for forever chemicals?

Quick answer: the FDA has a plan that would ask makers to check all period products for contaminants. It is still a draft, and it does not name PFAS.

In late 2025, the FDA put out a draft called "Menstrual Products - Performance Testing and Labeling Recommendations." Its notice in the Federal Register, dated November 17, 2025, says the advice applies "to tampons, pads, and menstrual cups."

The notice says the draft covers two things that matter for a liner:

  • "disclosing ingredients (including fragrances/deodorants) on all menstrual product outer package labels"
  • "evaluating contaminants for all menstrual products"

But the same notice says, "This draft guidance is not final nor is it for implementation at this time." It also says the guidance "is not binding on FDA or the public."

On November 3, 2026, the FDA's page for it still said "Draft" and "Not for implementation." The draft does name a few contaminants, like dioxin and pesticide residues. But in all 37 of its pages, it never uses the word PFAS.

Sources: Federal Register notice, FR Doc. 2025-19948, November 17, 2025, Docket FDA-2025-D-5107; FDA, guidance page and draft text, October 2025; FDA, Menstrual Product Options, Facts, and Safe Use, updated July 6, 2026.

What does California's law do?

California has its own law, AB 2515. Since January 1, 2025, it bans selling period products with PFAS "that a manufacturer has intentionally added." Starting January 1, 2027, it also counts PFAS found in a product at or above a limit. The state's toxics department is to set that limit.

So until 2027, the ban turns on what a company added on purpose.

What does the Carefree lawsuit claim?

On October 9, 2026, hundreds of Los Angeles County residents sued the companies behind Carefree liners, Manufacturing Dive reported. They claim a 2024 independent lab test found PFOA in one or more of the liner's five layers. They also claim the liners were made with it on purpose. These are claims, not court findings.

Essity now owns Carefree in North America. An Essity spokesperson said, "We do not intentionally add PFAS chemicals to our products." The spokesperson also said, "Carefree stands behind its products," and that Essity plans to "vigorously respond."

What does the FDA say about wearing a liner every day?

Quick answer: change it often, and stop if it bothers your skin.

The FDA gives a few use tips for pads. A liner is a pad, so they apply.

  • "Change menstrual pads every few hours, even if your flow is light."
  • "Wearing a pad too long may irritate the skin."
  • "Some women may have a reaction to scents, deodorants or other materials added to pads."
  • If you have "itching, rash, pain, swelling or discomfort," the FDA says to stop using the pad and contact your healthcare provider.

These are tips for use. They do not tell you what is inside a liner.

Source: FDA, Menstrual Pads and Liners, content current as of July 6, 2026.

What should I wear instead?

Quick answer: a liner with no PFAS in any layer, from a company that tests for it and will say so in writing.

  1. Ask before you buy. Ask the company two things. Do you test every layer for PFAS? Will you say so in writing? If the answer is no, skip it.
  2. Skip added scent. Pick a liner with no added scent or deodorant. The FDA says some women react to them.
  3. Change it often. Every few hours, even on light days, as the FDA says.
  4. Speak up about a reaction. If a liner gives you a rash or itching, stop using it and call your doctor. You can also tell the FDA through MedWatch, its safety reporting program. The FDA says you can report a problem with any menstrual product there.

One more thing. Do not take "regulated by the FDA" as proof that a liner was tested for PFAS. Ask the company.

Common questions about the FDA and panty liners

Does the FDA test panty liners before they are sold?

The FDA requires makers to register, follow its factory rules, report problems and meet its labeling rules. But the federal rule says a liner made of common pad materials is exempt from premarket notification, the filing a maker sends the FDA before it sells some devices. The FDA's example of a period product it reviews before sale is tampons. It does not name pads.

Is the FDA testing pads for contaminants?

The FDA has a draft plan that includes "evaluating contaminants for all menstrual products," and that covers pads. It is advice for the companies that make them. But the FDA says the draft "is not final nor is it for implementation at this time." The draft also does not use the word PFAS.

Are panty liners regulated by the FDA?

Yes. The FDA says a panty liner is a thinner version of a menstrual pad, and that menstrual pads are regulated by the FDA as medical devices. But being regulated is not the same as being tested for PFAS.

Does "regulated by the FDA" mean a liner has no PFAS?

No. None of the steps the FDA lists before a pad is sold is a test for PFAS, and its plan for contaminant checks is still a draft. A new lawsuit in California claims a lab found PFOA in Carefree liners. Essity, which owns Carefree in North America, says it does not add PFAS on purpose. These are claims, not court findings.

Sources for these answers: FDA, Menstrual Pads and Liners; FDA, Menstrual Product Options, Facts, and Safe Use; 21 CFR 884.5435; Federal Register, November 17, 2025; Manufacturing Dive, October 2026.
