Why this guide exists

My doctor gave me a supplement list. I followed it. Six months later I was taking everything on that list and still not feeling the way I expected to feel.

She never asked me what I was cleaning my house with. What was sitting in my laundry room. What I was spraying in the bathroom every morning before anyone else was awake.

I started going through EPA documentation, peer-reviewed environmental health studies, and indoor air quality research. The information is not hidden. It is just not part of clinical training. Nobody connects what you're breathing in your home to what's happening in your body — because they're trained to address one and not the other.

The gap is real. I found it the hard way. This checklist is what I put together so you don't have to spend a year getting there.

The 5 product categories to look at first

These are the categories that show up most consistently in indoor air quality and toxicology research. Most people have at least two of them under their roof right now.

  1. 1
    Laundry Detergent Sodium lauryl sulfate, synthetic fragrance, and optical brighteners appear in products marketed as gentle, plant-based, and hypoallergenic. The FDA has not defined "plant-based." That word means nothing enforceable on a detergent label.
  2. 2
    All-Purpose and Bathroom Cleaners These are documented VOC sources. Ammonia, 2-butoxyethanol, and bleach are the most common flagged compounds. The EPA has identified these categories as primary contributors to indoor VOC exposure in residential environments.
  3. 3
    Laundry Fragrance Boosters "Fragrance" on any label is a proprietary formula. Thousands of individual compounds can legally hide under that single word. Many of them are documented endocrine disruptors in peer-reviewed literature.
  4. 4
    Air Fresheners and Plug-Ins This is the highest VOC-emitting category in residential use. Several major brands carry compounds listed under California Proposition 65. If you have a plug-in running in a small room, it is likely the largest source of airborne compounds in your home.
  5. 5
    Dryer Sheets The heat from a dryer activates the coating on the sheet. Quat compounds and synthetic fragrance are the primary concerns. The activation step is what separates dryer sheets from other laundry products in terms of inhalation exposure.

Reading the label

Before you start pulling products, here is what to understand about the language on the packaging.

  • "Fragrance" is a proprietary formula. No brand is required to disclose what is in it.
  • "Plant-based" has no regulatory definition. Any company can use it for any product.
  • "Hypoallergenic" has no legal standard. It does not mean tested, safer, or approved.
  • "Natural" is the same situation. Not defined. Not regulated.
  • If you cannot find a product's ingredients in the EPA Safer Choice database or cross-reference against the Prop 65 list, that is worth a second look before it goes back under your sink.
The EPA Safer Choice database is public and free. The Prop 65 list is public and free. The data exists. Most people just don't know to look for it.

The 20-minute audit

You do not need to go through every cabinet at once. Start here.

  1. Laundry Room Pull out your detergent, fabric softener, and dryer sheets. Read the ingredient list on each one. Write down anything you don't recognize.
  2. Under the Kitchen Sink All-purpose spray, dish soap, any cleaners stored here. Same process — read the label, note what you can't identify.
  3. Bathroom Toilet bowl cleaner, tub and tile spray, air freshener, any plug-in unit. If you have a plug-in, pull it out and read the refill label.
  4. Look up what you found Search the product name plus "Prop 65" or "EPA Safer Choice." The data is public. This step takes less time than people expect.
