Shannon Nicole — Eczema Home Audit Guide

The home ingredient audit your pediatrician couldn't give you.

Start with the laundry room. It is almost always there.

Why this guide exists

My daughter's eczema had a pattern. Cream from the pediatrician. Flare subsides. Three weeks later, it was back. We repeated that cycle more times than I want to count.

The turning point was not a new doctor. It was a question I asked myself while standing in the laundry room: what is actually in the detergent I just bought?

The label said "gentle." It said "sensitive skin." It had a lavender plant on the front. None of that means anything. The words gentle, plant-based, and sensitive skin have no regulatory definition under FDA or EPA guidelines. A product can carry all three claims and still contain compounds documented in the dermatology literature as skin sensitizers.

What I found when I actually read the ingredient lists — and cross-referenced them against EPA hazard data, peer-reviewed dermatology research, and the California Proposition 65 list — changed what I keep in my home.

This guide is that research, organized so it takes you 20 minutes instead of months.

The laundry room audit (start here)

Clothing is the largest surface area in direct contact with skin, all day, every day. Residues from detergents and fabric treatments do not fully rinse out. This is where I found the most significant exposures in our home.

  • Synthetic Fragrance

    The word "fragrance" on an ingredient label is a legally protected proprietary formula. A single fragrance listing can contain dozens to thousands of individual chemical compounds. Manufacturers are not required by FDA to disclose what those compounds are. Major brands marketed specifically for sensitive skin — including well-known natural lines — frequently include fragrance blends. Some compounds commonly found in fragrance formulations appear on documented sensitizer lists in dermatology literature and EU regulatory reviews.

  • Sodium Lauryl Sulfate and Sodium Laureth Sulfate (SLS / SLES)

    These surfactants appear in both conventional and plant-derived detergents. SLS in particular has a documented role in disrupting the skin's barrier function in peer-reviewed literature. Barrier disruption is one of the mechanisms associated with eczema flare cycles. The presence of these ingredients in "natural" formulations is common.

  • Optical Brighteners

    Optical brighteners work by coating fabric fibers with compounds that absorb UV light and re-emit it as visible blue-white light — making whites appear whiter. They are designed to remain on fabric after washing. That means they are in direct, continuous contact with skin throughout the day. Multiple optical brightener compounds appear in sensitizer databases used by regulatory bodies.

  • Fabric Softeners and Dryer Sheets

    Fabric softeners carry among the highest fragrance loads of any laundry product. Many also contain quaternary ammonium compounds (quats), which coat fabric fibers and remain after drying. The heat cycle of a dryer activates and volatilizes compounds in both liquid softeners and dryer sheets, increasing inhalation and skin exposure. These are the products I removed first.

  • Enzyme Compounds (Proteases, Amylases, Lipases)

    Enzymes are added to detergents to break down protein and starch stains. They are effective. They are also biologically active compounds. In individuals with sensitive immune responses, repeated skin exposure to protease-class enzymes has been associated with immune sensitization in occupational and consumer health literature. They appear in both conventional and plant-based formulations marketed as "powerful clean."

What "gentle," "plant-based," and "hypoallergenic" actually mean

Before I started reading ingredient lists, I made purchasing decisions based on front-of-label language. Here is what I found when I looked into what those words actually require:

  • Plant-based has no definition under FDA or EPA regulation. A product can contain synthetic petrochemical compounds and still carry this claim.
  • Hypoallergenic has no legal standard in the United States. The FDA proposed a rule in the 1970s to define the term; it was never finalized. Any manufacturer can use it.
  • Gentle has no regulatory definition. It is a marketing descriptor.
  • Dermatologist recommended typically means one or more dermatologists participated in some form of consultation or endorsement arrangement. It does not indicate a clinical study of the specific product on eczema-prone skin.
  • Fragrance-free is more meaningful than "unscented" — unscented products can contain masking fragrances. Fragrance-free is worth seeking, but still requires label verification because formulations change.
  • "Fragrance" on the ingredient list remains a proprietary formula regardless of how the front label reads. A product can say "pure" and list fragrance as an ingredient.

The standard I used: If I could not find the ingredient on the EPA Safer Choice list or confirm it was absent from the California Prop 65 list, it did not stay in my laundry room.

Beyond the laundry room

The laundry room is where I started because it produced the most immediate change. But it is not the only room that matters for skin and respiratory exposure.

2–5x

EPA documentation shows indoor air can be 2 to 5 times more polluted than outdoor air in a closed home. Source: EPA Indoor Air Quality documentation

Children have a higher respiration rate than adults. They spend more time closer to the floor — where heavier volatile compounds settle. They also have a higher ratio of skin surface area to body mass, which means proportionally greater dermal exposure to the same environment.

After the laundry room, I looked at:

  • Bathroom cleaners — toilet bowl cleaners, tub and tile sprays, and disinfectants often contain compounds documented under ATSDR (Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry) guidelines. Spray application increases inhalation exposure.
  • Kitchen sprays and surface cleaners — countertops where food is prepared and where hands rest throughout the day. Residue from cleaning sprays remains after the surface appears dry.
  • Air fresheners and plug-in diffusers — continuous fragrance delivery systems that operate on cycles or constantly. Many air freshener formulations contain VOCs (volatile organic compounds) documented by EPA as contributing to indoor air quality degradation. "Natural" and "essential oil" diffusers are not exempt from this.

The 20-minute audit process

You do not need a chemistry degree. You need two free public resources and the willingness to read labels. Here is the exact process I use:

  1. Start in the laundry room. Pull out every product — detergent, softener, dryer sheets, stain remover. Read every ingredient label, not just the front.
  2. Check for the five categories listed in this guide: synthetic fragrance, SLS/SLES, optical brighteners, softener/sheet compounds, and enzyme blends.
  3. Open the EPA Safer Choice database (epa.gov/saferchoice). It is free, public, and searchable by product name. Products listed have met documented ingredient safety standards.
  4. Cross-reference against the California Proposition 65 list (oehha.ca.gov/proposition-65/proposition-65-list). This is the most current publicly maintained list of chemicals known to the state of California to cause cancer, birth defects, or other reproductive harm.
  5. Move to the bathroom. Apply the same process to cleaning products, then air fresheners.
  6. Move to the kitchen. Surface cleaners, dish soap, any sprays used on food-contact surfaces.
  7. Document what you find. I kept a simple list. What I replaced first made the biggest difference fastest.

No app required. No paid database. EPA Safer Choice and California Prop 65 are both free, updated regularly, and as authoritative as publicly available information gets.
