What the research shows

This section is educational. It summarizes published science and government documentation. It is not medical advice and does not constitute a diagnosis of any kind.

Children breathe proportionally faster than adults. Because of their smaller body mass relative to lung surface area, children take in more air per unit of body weight. This means higher exposure to whatever is airborne in their environment. EPA

Children spend more time indoors than any other demographic. The EPA has documented that Americans spend approximately 90 percent of their time indoors. For children, that figure is typically higher due to school, sleep, and home time.

Indoor air is frequently more polluted than outdoor air. EPA documentation indicates that indoor air can be two to five times more concentrated with certain pollutants than outdoor air in a home with limited ventilation. The primary contributors in residential settings are products — not structural materials alone. EPA

The leading indoor VOC sources in residential homes are consumer products. Cleaning products, air fresheners, and fragranced laundry products are consistently identified as primary indoor VOC contributors. These are products most families use daily without knowing what is in them. EPA ATSDR

Peer-reviewed environmental health literature has examined associations between VOC exposure and neurological symptoms in children. This includes attention, cognition, and headache-type symptoms. This is published science, not fringe positions. The research base is growing.

The 5 product categories to audit

  • Air Fresheners and Plug-Ins This is consistently the highest VOC-emitting category in most residential homes. Common VOCs include formaldehyde and acetaldehyde. The EPA flags plug-in air fresheners as a significant indoor air quality contributor. These products operate continuously and are often placed in rooms where children sleep.
  • Bathroom and Multi-Surface Cleaners Ammonia and chlorine compounds in enclosed spaces create elevated exposure. Bathrooms and kitchens are typically the lowest-ventilated rooms in a home. Cleaning frequency is high and ventilation during use is rarely adequate to fully clear the air before the space is occupied again.
  • Laundry Fragrance — Detergents, Boosters, Dryer Sheets The word "fragrance" on a product label is a proprietary formula. Manufacturers are not required to disclose what chemical compounds make up that fragrance blend. Laundry products coat clothing and bedding. That coating has continuous skin contact and off-gases into the air of whatever room that clothing or bedding is in — including the bedroom where a child sleeps.
  • Scented Candles Paraffin releases VOCs when burned. Children's bedrooms are one of the most common locations for candles in residential use. A candle burned in a bedroom before sleep is releasing compounds into the air of the room the child will be breathing in for eight or more hours.
  • Dish Soap and Kitchen Cleaners High frequency of daily use makes this category significant. Look specifically for ammonia, chlorine, and synthetic fragrance on the label. These products are used multiple times per day in a room that is often the most active room in the home.

The timing test

Before you change anything, spend a week tracking when the headaches happen. Timing patterns tell you more than bloodwork for environmental factors, because the exposure source is tied to a location and a time of day.

  • Are headaches worse on cleaning days?
  • Are headaches worse in specific rooms?
  • Are headaches better outdoors?
  • Are headaches better when your child is away from home — at school, at a grandparent's house, on vacation?
  • Do headaches tend to appear at a consistent time of day?
  • Did the pattern start or worsen after you changed a product — new detergent, new candle, a plug-in you added?

You can observe these patterns. A physician in a clinic cannot. That is not a flaw in the medical system — it is simply a difference in what each person can see. You live there. You have information no test can surface.

The 20-minute audit

Work room by room. Start in the bedroom — it is where your child spends the most hours and where product contact is continuous through sleep. Then the bathroom. Then the kitchen.

  • Step 1 Bedroom first. Laundry detergent, dryer sheets, fabric softener, any candles, air fresheners, or plug-ins. Read every label. Look for the word "fragrance" and for chlorine, ammonia, formaldehyde, or acetaldehyde.
  • Step 2 Bathroom second. All cleaning products, toilet bowl cleaners, tile spray, air fresheners. Note anything with ammonia, chlorine, or fragrance in the ingredient list.
  • Step 3 Kitchen third. Dish soap, countertop spray, multi-surface cleaner. Frequency of use is the key factor here — daily exposure adds up faster than a product you use once a week.
  • Step 4 Cross-reference for free. Look up any flagged product on the EPA Safer Choice database (epa.gov/saferchoice) and check the California Prop 65 list (p65warnings.ca.gov). Both are free, publicly available, and maintained by government agencies. No account required.
