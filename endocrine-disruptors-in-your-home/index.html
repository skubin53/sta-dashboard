Endocrine Disruptors in Your Home

What they are, where they hide, what the science really says, and the simple swaps that lower your everyday exposure.

By Shannon Nicole, founder of Switch to America. Seven years helping women switch to a toxin-free home, and a health coach for over 20 years.

Endocrine disruptors are everyday chemicals that scientists study for one reason: they may mimic or interfere with your body's hormones. They are not exotic. They turn up in scented products, in some plastics, and in the lining of food cans, and national testing keeps finding them in most people who get tested. The honest part is that this is a lower-your-exposure story, not a panic story, and most of the swaps are simple and cost nothing extra.

What is an endocrine disruptor?

The National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences, the federal institute that studies this, puts it plainly. Endocrine disruptors are "natural or man-made chemicals that may mimic or interfere with the body's hormones, known as the endocrine system." Your hormones are the chemical messengers that quietly run your energy, your sleep, your metabolism, and more, so a chemical that behaves like a hormone or gets in the way of one is worth understanding.

NIEHS says they are found in many everyday products, including some cosmetics, food and beverage packaging, toys, carpet, and pesticides, and it names BPA and phthalates as examples. In other words, the sources are ordinary things already in most homes.

Source: National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences (NIEHS), Endocrine Disruptors.

If they are everywhere, are they in me?

Most likely, yes, and that is not a reason to be frightened. When the government tests Americans through national biomonitoring, it keeps finding these chemicals in the majority of people. Phthalate breakdown products show up in most of the population tested. The CDC found bisphenol A (BPA) in 92.6 percent of the 2,517 people age six and older it tested in its 2003 to 2004 national survey, which the researchers described as widespread exposure.

Sources: Calafat et al., CDC, "Exposure of the U.S. Population to Bisphenol A," Environmental Health Perspectives (2008); NHANES phthalate biomonitoring, Journal of Exposure Science and Environmental Epidemiology.

Here is the honest counterweight, and it matters. Finding a chemical in your body is not the same as being harmed by it. The CDC states directly that "the measurement of an environmental chemical in a person's blood or urine does not mean, by itself, that the chemical causes disease." Detection tells you exposure happened. It does not tell you it made you sick.

Source: CDC, National Exposure Report, Interpretation of the Data.

This is a reduce-your-exposure story, not a fear story. You cannot avoid every chemical, and you do not have to.

The three that show up most at home

Phthalates, hiding in the word "fragrance"

Phthalates are a group of chemicals that researchers describe as "known or suspected human reproductive and endocrine-disrupting toxicants," meaning the science studies how they may interfere with hormone signals. The part most people never hear: they often ride along inside the single word "fragrance." One peer-reviewed study of women's product use put it flatly, that "phthalates are seldom listed on product labels because current United States regulations do not require listing individual fragrance components." So a scented lotion, cleaner, or air freshener can carry them without ever naming them. When a label will not tell you what is in it, the simplest answer is to choose products that will.

Sources: PLoS ONE, phthalate biomonitoring in U.S. women; Journal of Exposure Science and Environmental Epidemiology, phthalates and personal care products.

BPA and the plastics that replaced it

Bisphenol A (BPA) is a studied endocrine disruptor, part of the group researched for acting as a hormone mimic. The National Toxicology Program notes it is used in hard polycarbonate plastics and in the epoxy resins that "coat some metal food cans, bottle tops, and water supply pipes," so most everyday contact comes through food and drink. And the chemicals sold as safer swaps are turning up too: in the 2013 to 2014 national survey, BPA was detected in 95.7 percent of samples, its replacement BPS in 89.4 percent, and BPF in 66.5 percent. Chasing a "BPA-free" label often just changes which bisphenol you carry, so the more reliable move is to lean away from hard plastic and canned food and never heat food in plastic.

Sources: National Toxicology Program (NIEHS/NIH), Bisphenol A (BPA); Lehmler et al., ACS Omega, NHANES 2013-2014.

Certain pesticides

Some pesticides are studied as possible endocrine disruptors too. The EPA runs a screening program that checks pesticide chemicals for potential effects on the body's three hormone pathways, the estrogen, androgen, and thyroid systems, and it names DDT, an old pesticide, as one example of an endocrine-disrupting chemical. For a modern one, atrazine is among the most widely used weed killers on US farms, and the fact that the government caps it in drinking water at no more than 0.003 milligrams per liter tells you it is a chemical worth keeping out of your everyday life where you can. A federal health agency suggests simple steps like staying away from fields that were just sprayed and testing your water if you are on a private well.

Sources: US EPA, Endocrine Disruptor Screening Program and Overview of Endocrine Disruption; ATSDR, ToxFAQs: Atrazine.

What the science does and does not say

It would be easy to turn this into a scare, so here is the balanced version. NIEHS is open that "scientists are not certain how some endocrine disruptors work, and study continues." Because hormones act in extremely small amounts, whether an exposure matters can depend on the dose and the timing, which is exactly why the science is careful rather than loud. And as the CDC reminds us, finding a chemical in the body is not proof of harm.

What the experts agree on is the practical part. NIEHS says these chemicals "cannot be completely avoided or removed; however, you can make informed choices to reduce exposure and risk of any potential health effects." That is the whole point of this page. Not fear, just fewer of the ones that are easy to skip.

Source: NIEHS, Endocrine Disruptors and Your Health (fact sheet).

How to lower your exposure without going overboard

You do not need to redo your house in a weekend. The endocrine experts and federal health agencies point to the same short, sensible list, and most of it is free.

Where it hidesThe simple swap
Scented products (the word "fragrance")Choose fragrance-free, or scented only with essential oils
Hard plastic and canned foodLean fresh, cut back on canned, store in glass or stainless
Heating food in plasticNever microwave or store hot food and drinks in plastic
Tap waterUse a water filter
Household dustClean floors regularly, wash hands before meals

None of that is expensive, and a lot of it saves money. Fragrance-free, American made staples with a short, readable ingredient list do the same jobs as the scented name brands, and a set of glass containers or a good water filter pays for itself over time. Safer does not have to mean pricier, and here it usually is not.

Source: Endocrine Society, Endocrine-Disrupting Chemicals (patient library).

Where to start

Pick one. The easiest first move for most women is going fragrance-free, because scent is in so many products at once, from laundry to lotion to the spray under the sink. After that, move hot food out of plastic and into glass. You do not have to be perfect, and you do not have to do it all today. One steady swap at a time is what actually sticks, and it adds up to a home with a lot less of this in it.
