Does My Lash Serum Have a Prostaglandin in It?

The names to look for besides the one in LiLash, what Europe's safety panel said about them in 2026, and a plain swap for your lashes.

By Shannon Nicole, founder of Switch to America. 7 years helping women switch to a toxin-free home, and a health coach for over 20 years. Published October 27, 2026.

Look at your serum's ingredient list for a long word with "prost" inside it, like isopropyl cloprostenate, ethyl tafluprostamide, norbimatoprost, dehydrolatanoprost or bimatoprost. Those are prostaglandin analogs. That is the same drug family as some prescription eye drops, and they can grow lashes. In February 2026, Europe's consumer safety panel said none of the three it checked "can be considered safe" in serums meant to grow lashes and brows.

Europe's safety panel looked at three lash-growing prostaglandins in 2026 and said none of them can be considered safe in a lash or brow serum.

How do I know if my lash serum has a prostaglandin in it?

Quick answer: find the ingredient list and look for a long word with "prost" inside it.

The ingredient list is on the box, on the tube, or on the brand's website.

Every full chemical name in this guide has the letters "prost" inside it. Europe's 2022 list was built partly the same way. Names in this family were found in Europe's cosmetic ingredient database by searching for "prost."

Look all the way to the end of the list. In a 2026 lawsuit, Sandra Meyers claims the maker of LiLash put its prostaglandin analog, dehydrolatanoprost, "second-to-last" on the list, "without any indication that it is a PGA." PGA is short for prostaglandin analog. That is her claim, not a court finding. To be fair, as of October 27, 2026, LiLash's own product page does call dehydrolatanoprost "a prostaglandin analog."

Still, do not wait to see the word "prostaglandin." It may not be on the box. Look for the name instead.

What did Europe's safety panel say about these in 2026?

Quick answer: that none of the three it checked "can be considered safe" in serums meant to grow lashes and brows.

The panel is the European Commission's Scientific Committee on Consumer Safety, or SCCS. On February 2, 2026, it adopted a final opinion on isopropyl cloprostenate, methylamido dihydro noralfaprostal and DDDE.

The panel was asked if they are safe in tiny amounts, from 0.005% to 0.03% of the serum. It looked at the data sent in to support that. It wrote that "none of them can be considered safe for use in cosmetic products intended for promoting the growth of eyelashes and eyebrows."

One reason it gave was "the potent pharmacological activity of the PGAs, even at low concentrations." In plain words, they act like drugs, even in tiny amounts.

The panel also wrote that leaflets for prostaglandin eye drop medicines list possible side effects such as red eyes, stinging, dry eye, eye pain, darker eyelids, blurry vision, and even a permanent change in the color of the eye. A lash serum is not put in the eye. But it is used right next to it, and the panel said a spill into the eye "cannot be excluded."

It added that most of these eye side effects would not show up in the standard eye and skin irritation tests done on cosmetics.

In 2022, the same panel had been softer. It was "not able to conclude on the safety" of isopropyl cloprostenate and ethyl tafluprostamide. It noted concerns about their use in cosmetics, "in particular those that are intended for use in the proximity of the eye."

One honest limit. An SCCS opinion is expert advice to the European Commission. It is not a law, in Europe or in the US.

Europe is working on a ban. In September 2026, the European Commission sent a draft rule to the Council of the European Union. It would add prostaglandins and their analogs to Europe's list of banned cosmetic ingredients. As of October 27, 2026, there was no sign it had been adopted.

Sources: SCCS, final opinion SCCS/1680/25, adopted February 2, 2026, published February 10, 2026, Conclusion and section 8; SCCS, opinion SCCS/1635/21, adopted February 3, 2022, Conclusion; European Commission, draft regulation amending Regulation (EC) No 1223/2009, sent to the Council September 21, 2026, recitals 21 to 25, and its annex, Annex II entry "Prostaglandines and their analogues."

How common are prostaglandins in lash serums?

Quick answer: in one 2026 study, about 3 in 10.

French researchers looked at 79 cosmetic products that promised longer, thicker or fuller lashes. They wrote that "About 30% of them contain a prostaglandin analogue."

They found six different ones, including bimatoprost and norbimatoprost. The most common were ethyl tafluprostamide and isopropyl cloprostenate. The rest of the serums leaned on plain things like castor oil.

Bimatoprost is the drug in Latisse, a prescription lash product. The Latisse label says Latisse "is a prostaglandin analog." It warns that darker color in the iris, the colored part of the eye, "is likely to be permanent."

Sources: Couteau and Coiffard, Annales Pharmaceutiques Françaises, 2026, abstract; Latisse (bimatoprost) prescribing label, DailyMed, National Library of Medicine, revised July 2024.

What if I do not find any of these names?

Quick answer: that does not prove your serum is safe. It only means you did not find this one family.

These lists come from one European panel and one study. They are not every lash-growing ingredient that exists.

Write to the company and ask: "Does this serum contain a prostaglandin analog, under any name?" Save the answer.

What does the LiLash lawsuit say?

Quick answer: that LiLash was sold as a beauty serum "with no warning of serious side effects or risks."

On August 31, 2026, Sandra Meyers sued Cosmetic Alchemy, the company behind LiLash, in federal court in California. Her complaint says, "Dehydrolatanoprost is a prostaglandin analog." It says this is the same family as the drug in Latisse.

These are her claims. They are not court findings.

What can I use on my lashes instead?

Quick answer: a clean spoolie and a drop of plain oil at night, with nothing from the drug family in it.

First, the honest limit. A plain oil will not grow lashes the way a drug does.

Wash your face at night. Then brush a drop of castor oil through your top lashes with a clean spoolie. Wash the spoolie once a week.

If your eye hurts after a lash serum, stop using it tonight. If the pain stays or your sight changes, call your eye doctor today.

Common questions about prostaglandins in lash serums

What is a prostaglandin analog in a lash serum?

It is a lab-made cousin of prostaglandins, chemicals your body makes on its own. Drugs in this family are used in eye drops for high eye pressure, and they can grow lashes as a side effect. Some lash serums use them to make lashes longer, thicker and darker.

Did Europe ban prostaglandins in lash serums?

Not yet, as far as public records show. In February 2026, Europe's consumer safety panel said none of the three it checked can be considered safe in lash and brow serums. That panel gives expert advice to the European Commission. Its opinion is not a law. In September 2026, the Commission sent a draft rule to the Council of the European Union that would ban prostaglandins and their analogs in cosmetics. There was no sign it had been adopted as of October 27, 2026.

If my serum has none of these names, is it safe?

Not necessarily. A missing name only means you did not find this one family. These lists come from one European panel and one study, so they are not every lash-growing ingredient. Ask the company in writing if the serum holds a prostaglandin analog under any name.

What should I do if my eye hurts after using a lash serum?

Stop using it tonight. If the pain stays or your sight changes, call your eye doctor today.
