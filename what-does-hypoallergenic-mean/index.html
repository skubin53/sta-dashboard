What Does "Hypoallergenic" Mean on a Body Wash?

What the word promises, who checks it and who does not, why the FDA's rule for it was struck down, and what to use instead.

By Shannon Nicole, founder of Switch to America. Seven years helping women switch to a toxin-free home, and a health coach for over 20 years. Published October 29, 2026.

"Hypoallergenic" means whatever the company selling it wants it to mean. The FDA says there is no federal definition of the word, and companies do not have to send the FDA any proof. The FDA did write a rule for the word in 1975, but a federal appeals court struck it down in 1977.

"Hypoallergenic" is the company's own promise. The FDA does not define it, and the company does not have to show the FDA any proof.

What does "hypoallergenic" mean?

Quick answer: the word is meant to tell you a product is less likely to cause a skin reaction. But no federal rule says what that has to mean.

"Hypo" means under, or less than normal. So "hypoallergenic" sounds like fewer allergic reactions.

The FDA's page on the word is blunt. It says: "There are no Federal standards or definitions that govern the use of the term 'hypoallergenic.' The term means whatever a particular company wants it to mean."

The same page says the word may help sell products, "but dermatologists say it has very little meaning."

The FDA said more back in 1978. It wrote that "there is no such thing as a 'nonallergenic' cosmetic." It meant no cosmetic can be promised to never cause an allergic reaction. It also wrote that it knew of no studies showing "hypoallergenic" cosmetics cause fewer reactions than regular ones.

Source: FDA, Hypoallergenic Cosmetics, including its reprint of an April 1978 FDA Consumer article.

Does anyone check "hypoallergenic" before it goes on the bottle?

Quick answer: not the FDA. Companies do not have to send the FDA proof of the claim.

Here are the FDA's words. Makers of cosmetics labeled hypoallergenic "are not required to submit substantiation of their hypoallergenicity claims to FDA." Substantiation means proof.

So the company that fills the bottle picks the meaning. Then it prints the word. The FDA does not have to see any proof first.

Why is there no rule for the word?

The FDA tried to make one. It proposed a rule in February 1974 and issued it on June 6, 1975.

Under that rule, a company could call a product hypoallergenic only if tests in people showed it caused fewer skin reactions than similar products. The label also had to say the product was "less likely to cause adverse reactions than some competing products."

Two makers of "hypoallergenic" cosmetics sued. A lower court sided with the FDA. Then, on December 21, 1977, a federal appeals court in Washington, D.C., reversed. It called the FDA's action "arbitrary and capricious." The main proof behind the FDA's definition was a consumer survey, and the court found it was not reliable.

The court also noted that an American Medical Association skin committee had said the word "has outlived its usefulness, is misleading, and should be dropped from the labeling of cosmetic products."

The FDA summed up the result in 1978. Companies "may continue to label and advertise their cosmetics as 'hypoallergenic' or make similar claims without any supporting evidence."

Sources: Almay, Inc. v. Califano, 569 F.2d 674, U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit, decided December 21, 1977; FDA, Hypoallergenic Cosmetics.

Does "hypoallergenic" mean there are no allergens inside?

Quick answer: no. In one study, most moisturizers that claimed to be hypoallergenic still had a common skin allergen in them.

In a study published in 2017, a team led by skin doctors at Northwestern University looked at the best-selling body moisturizers at three big online stores. They found 174 different products. Of those, 18 claimed to be hypoallergenic.

Of those 18, 15 had at least one ingredient on a standard list of common skin allergens. Skin doctors use that list for patch tests. That is 83%.

The authors wrote that claims like "hypoallergenic" are "marketing tools with minimal to no regulatory oversight or substantiation."

That study was on moisturizers, not body washes. But the word has no set meaning on any bottle.

Source: Xu S, Kwa M, Lohman ME, Evers-Meltzer R, Silverberg JI, Consumer Preferences, Product Characteristics, and Potentially Allergenic Ingredients in Best-selling Moisturizers, JAMA Dermatology, November 2017 (full text).

What do the Dove lawsuits say about "hypoallergenic"?

Quick answer: shoppers have sued over the word on Dove Sensitive body wash. In these suits, no court has found the label false.

In federal court in New Jersey, two shoppers sued Unilever over Dove Sensitive Skin Body Wash. They allege it contains known skin sensitizers. Those are ingredients that can set off skin allergies. These are allegations, not findings.

Unilever asked the judge to throw the case out. It pointed to the FDA's guidance on the word, the judge wrote. That guidance says the word has no federal definition and means "whatever a particular company wants it to mean."

On June 30, 2026, Judge Evelyn Padin turned down most of that request. She wrote that the shoppers "plausibly plead that the Product is not 'hypoallergenic' as labeled." She also noted that the sources Unilever cited did not agree on one meaning of the word.

That means the case can go on. It is not a finding that the label is false. She also threw out some claims, including fraud, for now. The shoppers filed an amended complaint on July 30, 2026.

Unilever also argued that the ingredient list on the back fixed the front. The judge disagreed. She wrote that shoppers expect the back of the bottle to confirm the front, not to correct it.

In California, three shoppers sued Conopco, a Unilever company, on May 8, 2026. They allege their lab testing found d-Limonene in Dove Sensitive Hypoallergenic Body Wash. Their complaint calls it "one of the 26 recognized fragrance allergens." They dropped the case on June 30, 2026. No judge ruled on their claims.

What should I use instead?

Quick answer: a cleaner body wash, from a company that stands behind its words.

You do not have to redo your whole bathroom tonight. Start with the bottle in your shower. It touches your skin every day.

  • Stop letting one word choose for you. "Hypoallergenic" on the front is the company's promise, not proof.
  • Ask the company. Ask what "hypoallergenic" or "gentle" means to them, what proof they have, and whether there is any added fragrance. A company that stands behind its words will give you a straight answer.
  • Watch for look-alike words. "Unscented" and "fragrance free" do not mean the same thing either. I explain the difference in Does "Unscented" Mean Fragrance Free?
  • Trust your skin. If a wash leaves you itchy, red or tight, stop using it. The word on the front does not outrank your own skin.
