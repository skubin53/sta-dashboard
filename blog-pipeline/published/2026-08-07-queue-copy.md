<h2>Is store-bought ground beef real beef?</h2>
<p>Yes, and I am going to start by killing the scariest thing you have probably been told. Mechanically separated beef, the paste people picture when they hear "pink slime," is not in your ground beef. Federal regulation states flatly that <a href="https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-9/chapter-III/subchapter-A/part-319/subpart-A/section-319.5">mechanically separated beef "is inedible and prohibited for use as human food."</a> That has been the law since 2004.</p>
<p>The real answers are less dramatic and more useful. Most conventional US cattle carry an FDA-approved hormone implant. Ammonium hydroxide is legal in food. And in fiscal 2025, USDA tested 12,106 samples of raw ground beef and found adulterant E. coli in 35 of them. That is 0.17 percent.</p>

<p>You are at the meat case on a Tuesday with a package in your hand, and it is the most ordinary thing in the world. It is what is for dinner. Then somebody sends you a video, and now you are looking at the same package thinking, what is this, actually.</p>

<p>Here is what the federal record says. Some of it will make you angry. Some of it will let you put the package in the cart without a knot in your stomach.</p>

<h2 id="msb">Is mechanically separated beef hiding in your ground beef?</h2>

<p><span class="quick-answer">Quick answer:</span> no, and the law says so in one sentence.</p>

<div class="sta-myth">
<h3>Let me correct something first</h3>
<p>USDA's regulation at 9 CFR 319.5 states that <a href="https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-9/chapter-III/subchapter-A/part-319/subpart-A/section-319.5">"Mechanically Separated (Beef) is inedible and prohibited for use as human food."</a> The ban went in during January 2004, after the first US case of mad cow disease.</p>
<p>If you have been told there is beef paste in your ground beef, that is not true, and I am not going to pretend otherwise to make a better story.</p>
</div>

<p>Beef recovered by advanced meat recovery equipment, under a separate rule at 9 CFR 318.24, is legally meat and can be used. Worth knowing. Just not the thing in the video.</p>

<h2 id="hormones">Do American cattle really get hormones, and how much?</h2>

<p><span class="quick-answer">Quick answer:</span> yes, most of them, and the doses are published down to the milligram.</p>

<p>FDA has approved steroid implants for beef cattle <a href="https://www.fda.gov/animal-veterinary/product-safety-information/steroid-hormone-implants-used-growth-food-producing-animals">since the 1950s</a>. One approved implant, spelled out at <a href="https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/21/522.2477">21 CFR 522.2477</a>, contains 200 mg of trenbolone acetate and 20 mg of estradiol.</p>

<p>Now the part that has to be said in the same breath. The implant is a pellet under the skin of the ear, and the ear is discarded at slaughter. FDA also caps added estradiol at <a href="https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-E/part-556/subpart-B/section-556.240">0.2 parts per billion in muscle</a>.</p>

<p>So "your beef is loaded with hormones" is not what the record shows. What it shows is a routine, legal, deliberately dosed practice that nothing on the package will tell you about either way.</p>

<h2 id="europe">Why does Europe refuse hormone beef when FDA says it is safe?</h2>

<p><span class="quick-answer">Quick answer:</span> two regulators looked at overlapping evidence and made opposite calls about uncertainty.</p>

<p>The European Union has prohibited hormonal growth promoters in farm animals since 1981. Its scientific committee described oestradiol 17β as a complete carcinogen, and in the same breath conceded that the available data <a href="https://food.ec.europa.eu/food-safety/chemical-safety/hormones-meat_en">"would not allow a quantitative estimate of the risk."</a></p>

<p>That is the whole argument. Europe could not say how much exposure causes how much harm and banned it anyway on precaution. FDA weighed the same uncertainty and approved. Neither one is lying to you.</p>

<h2 id="co">Is your beef gassed to keep it looking red?</h2>

<p><span class="quick-answer">Quick answer:</span> carbon monoxide packaging is legal in the US, nothing has to disclose it, and the strongest claim against it came from two congressmen whose bill went nowhere.</p>

<p>In 2007, Representatives Stupak and Markey introduced a bill to require disclosure, alleging that <a href="https://www.markey.senate.gov/news/press-releases/july-20-2007-markey-stupak-introduce-bill-to-label-carbon-monoxide-blasted-meat">"blasting seafood, poultry and meat with carbon monoxide can make meat that has spoiled appear to be fresh, red and wholesome when, in reality it has spoiled."</a></p>

<p>That is an allegation by legislators, not an agency finding, and the bill died in subcommittee. At a hearing that year a meat scientist testified that spoilage still announces itself by smell. Your nose is a better freshness test than your eyes.</p>

<h2 id="ammonia">Is ammonia really used on beef?</h2>

<p><span class="quick-answer">Quick answer:</span> ammonium hydroxide is a legal food ingredient with no numeric limit, and it is in a lot more than beef.</p>

<p>FDA affirmed ammonium hydroxide as generally recognized as safe for direct use in human food at <a href="https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2023-title21-vol3/xml/CFR-2023-title21-vol3-sec184-1139.xml">21 CFR 184.1139</a>, as a leavening agent, a pH control agent and a surface-finishing agent. There is no ceiling written into it beyond good manufacturing practice. It shows up in baked goods and cheese.</p>

<p>Here is where most articles go wrong. The specific use on beef trimmings sits with USDA, not FDA, and I could not verify it against a primary USDA page, so I am not asserting it. What is verifiable: a substance with no numeric limit and no label disclosure is in the food supply.</p>

<h2 id="contam">How often is store ground beef actually contaminated?</h2>

<p><span class="quick-answer">Quick answer:</span> less often than the headlines suggest, and I would rather you have the real number than a scary one.</p>

<p>In fiscal year 2025, USDA's Food Safety and Inspection Service tested <a href="https://www.fsis.usda.gov/sites/default/files/media_file/documents/FY2025-Sampling-Summary-Report.pdf">12,106 raw ground beef samples for adulterant Shiga toxin-producing E. coli. Thirty-five came back positive, nine of them O157</a>. That is a volume-weighted prevalence of 0.17 percent. Salmonella prevalence in the same program was 2.02 percent.</p>

<p>Here is what surprised me most. Federal attribution data puts beef <em>second</em> for E. coli O157 illness at 18.6 percent, well behind vegetable row crops at 67.9 percent. If you are worried about E. coli, the bagged salad deserves more attention than the ground beef.</p>

<h3>The receipts</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Established fact.</strong> In 2018 and 2019, CDC investigated a Salmonella Newport outbreak in which <a href="https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/salmonella/newport-10-18/index.html">403 people were infected across 30 states and 117 were hospitalized</a>, with no deaths. CDC concluded ground beef from JBS Tolleson was a likely source. Roughly 12 million pounds were recalled.</li>
<li><strong>Regulatory action, not an allegation.</strong> In 2023, USDA announced a Class I recall of <a href="https://www.fsis.usda.gov/recalls-alerts/weinstein-wholesale-meats-inc--recalls-raw-ground-beef-burger-products-due-possible">about 2,122 pounds of raw ground beef patties that may have contained pieces of white neoprene</a>. Zero pounds were recovered. No confirmed adverse reactions.</li>
<li><strong>Allegation only.</strong> The carbon monoxide labeling claim above came from a 2007 bill that died in subcommittee.</li>
<li><strong>Cuts against the story, and it belongs here.</strong> USDA's own FY2025 testing found no adulterant E. coli in 99.8 percent of ground beef samples. That is the agency's number, not the industry's.</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="faq">What else do people ask?</h2>

<p><strong>Is there pink slime in my ground beef?</strong><br>
No. Mechanically separated beef has been classified inedible and banned from human food under 9 CFR 319.5 since January 2004. Mechanically separated pork and chicken are still legal in other products, which is where the confusion comes from.</p>

<p><strong>Does grocery store beef contain added hormones?</strong><br>
Most conventionally raised US cattle get an FDA-approved steroid implant. It goes in the ear, the ear is discarded at slaughter, and FDA caps added estradiol at 0.2 parts per billion in muscle. If you do not want it at all, you need beef raised without implants.</p>

<p><strong>How likely is it that the package I just bought has E. coli?</strong><br>
In fiscal 2025, USDA found adulterant E. coli in 35 of 12,106 ground beef samples, a prevalence of 0.17 percent. Real but uncommon, and cooking to 160°F destroys it.</p>

<p><strong>Is carbon monoxide used to make old meat look fresh?</strong><br>
It is legal and does not have to be disclosed. Whether it masks spoilage was alleged by two congressmen in 2007, never established by any agency. Judge freshness by smell, not color.</p>

<p><strong>So is grocery beef safe to eat?</strong><br>
By the federal record, cooked properly, usually yes. The argument for changing where you buy is not that store beef will poison you tonight. It is that you are not told what was done to it.</p>
