<h2 id="does-baby-powder-cause-cancer">Does baby powder cause cancer?</h2>

<p class="qa"><em>Quick answer: The World Health Organization's cancer agency classified talc as "probably carcinogenic to humans" in July 2024. That rating was based on limited evidence in humans, sufficient evidence in animals, and strong mechanistic evidence. Talc that contains asbestos is in the highest risk category, alongside asbestos itself.</em></p>

<p>In July 2024, the International Agency for Research on Cancer put talc in <a href="https://monographs.iarc.who.int/news-events/volume-136-talc-and-acrylonitrile" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Group 2A, probably carcinogenic to humans</a>. Twenty-nine scientists from thirteen countries sat in a room in Lyon and went through the evidence together.</p>

<p>I want to be straight with you about what that rating means, because both sides of this fight describe it wrong. The agency found <strong>limited</strong> evidence of cancer in humans. Not sufficient. Limited. It found sufficient evidence in animals and strong mechanistic evidence, which is the reason for the 2A rating rather than something lower. That is a real finding and it is also not the same thing as proof that a bottle of powder caused a particular woman's cancer.</p>

<p>The part with no ambiguity in it is the other half of that same announcement. Talc containing asbestos was not re-evaluated. It stays in Group 1, the category for things known to cause cancer in humans, filed under asbestos.</p>

<p>Which puts the whole question on one thing. Was there asbestos in the bottle.</p>

<h2 id="why-is-asbestos-in-the-conversation-at-all">Why is asbestos in the conversation at all?</h2>

<p class="qa"><em>Quick answer: Talc and asbestos form in the same rock. The FDA's own rulemaking says so plainly. You cannot see it, smell it, or test for it in your kitchen.</em></p>

<p>Talc is mined. It comes out of the ground, and the deposits it comes out of are the same geological formations that host asbestos. That is not an accusation anybody has to prove in court. It is in the FDA's own proposed rule: asbestos is found in the same rock types that host talc deposits.</p>

<p>So contamination is not a conspiracy theory. It is a mining problem. The question was never whether it could happen. It was whether it did, how often, and whether anyone was checking.</p>

<p>The FDA did check. In 2019 it contracted a lab to test 52 talc-containing cosmetic products and <a href="https://www.fda.gov/food/hfp-constituent-updates/fda-releases-data-agencys-year-long-sampling-assignment-test-talc-containing-cosmetic-products" target="_blank" rel="noopener">found asbestos in nine of them</a>. Not baby powder in every case. Eye shadow palettes and shimmer powders, including products from brands sold to children.</p>

<p>Now here is the part I am including even though it makes this story less dramatic, because leaving it out would be exactly the kind of thing this blog exists to call out. The FDA kept testing. In <a href="https://www.fda.gov/food/hfp-constituent-updates/fda-releases-data-agencys-2021-testing-talc-containing-cosmetic-products-asbestos" target="_blank" rel="noopener">2021</a>, 2022 and 2023 it tested fifty samples a year and found asbestos in none of them. Zero, three years running.</p>

<p>The FDA also says something about its own 2019 numbers that almost nobody quotes. Those 52 products were not picked at random. The agency deliberately included children's products and products that third parties had already reported as contaminated. In its words, the positive results "should not be interpreted as reflecting the overall frequency of positive samples in the marketplace."</p>

<p>That matters. Anyone telling you every bottle on the shelf is full of asbestos is not reading the same data I am.</p>

<h2 id="what-did-johnson-johnson-actually-agree-to-and-did-they-admi">What did Johnson &amp; Johnson actually agree to, and did they admit anything?</h2>

<p class="qa"><em>Quick answer: $5.5 billion, roughly 76,000 ovarian cancer claims, announced July 27, 2026. No admission. The company called the claims meritless and said it was confident it would have won.</em></p>

<p>This is the freshest thing in this article and it happened while I was writing it. On July 27, 2026, Johnson &amp; Johnson announced a proposed resolution of about <a href="https://www.insurancejournal.com/news/national/2026/07/28/879253.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener">76,000 ovarian cancer claims for $5.5 billion</a>. Ninety-five percent of claimants have to accept it before it takes effect. The first payment, no more than $3 billion, is expected in 2027.</p>

<p>The company's own words, and I am quoting them directly because they are entitled to their position: "While we are confident the company would have ultimately prevailed with further litigation, as it has in the vast majority of cases tried to date, this resolution allows the company to put this matter behind it."</p>

<p>They have denied for fifteen years that talc causes cancer, and they have denied that their talc contained asbestos. They won a California bellwether trial as recently as June 2026. A settlement is not a confession, and I am not going to tell you it is.</p>

<p>What I will tell you is what they did with the product itself. Johnson &amp; Johnson pulled talc-based baby powder from the United States and Canada in 2020, and from the rest of the world in 2023. They replaced it with cornstarch. A company that is completely certain a product is safe does not usually take it off every shelf on earth.</p>

<h2 id="if-the-fda-tested-it-and-found-nothing-why-is-anyone-still-w">If the FDA tested it and found nothing, why is anyone still worried?</h2>

<p class="qa"><em>Quick answer: Because the rule that would have required companies to test every batch was withdrawn in November 2025. Testing is not mandatory. The FDA's clean results came from fifty samples a year out of a market with thousands of products in it.</em></p>

<p>In December 2024, under the Modernization of Cosmetics Regulation Act, the FDA proposed a rule that would have required manufacturers to test talc products for asbestos using two specific microscopy methods, keep the images and the data for at least three years, and either test the finished product or verify their supplier's certificate every year.</p>

<p>On November 28, 2025, that proposal was <a href="https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/12/27/2024-30544/testing-methods-for-detecting-and-identifying-asbestos-in-talc-containing-cosmetic-products" target="_blank" rel="noopener">withdrawn</a> by the Secretary of Health and Human Services, citing a plan to reconsider the best means of addressing the issue.</p>

<p>So we are back where we started. Some companies test. Some do not. Nobody has to. And the fifty samples a year the FDA pulls are a spot check on a market with thousands of products in it, not a guarantee about the specific bottle in your bag.</p>

<p>That is the real answer to why people are still worried, and it has nothing to do with whether you trust Johnson &amp; Johnson. It has to do with the fact that no one is required to look.</p>

<h2 id="what-do-i-use-instead">What do I use instead?</h2>

<p class="qa"><em>Quick answer: Pediatricians say skip powder entirely, including cornstarch. The American Academy of Pediatrics says plainly: avoid using baby powder. A plain barrier ointment and a dry bottom do the same job with no airborne particles at all.</em></p>

<p>I expected to write a section here telling you to switch to a cornstarch powder. Then I read what the American Academy of Pediatrics actually says, and it is simpler and better than that.</p>

<p>Their guidance on <a href="https://www.healthychildren.org/English/safety-prevention/all-around/Pages/How-to-Choose-Safer-Personal-Care-Products.aspx" target="_blank" rel="noopener">HealthyChildren.org</a> is four words long. "Avoid using baby powder." They point out that talc-based powder can contain asbestos fibers, that breathing in a large amount of talc can lead to severe lung disease, and that cornstarch powder still carries a risk of respiratory irritation if it is inhaled.</p>

<p>Babies breathe fast and their lungs are still being built. A cloud of any fine powder next to that face is the problem, and swapping which powder makes the cloud does not solve it.</p>
