<h2 id="what-is-actually-in-a-bottle-of-avocado-oil">What is actually in a bottle of avocado oil?</h2>

<p><em>Quick answer: Often sunflower oil, safflower oil, or soybean oil. In the 2026 testing, every single salad dressing sold as avocado oil came back mixed with something else.</em></p>

<p>The most recent testing was published on July 15, 2026 in the journal <em>Applied Food Research</em>. A team at UC Davis led by Selina Wang bought 54 products labeled as being made with avocado oil, from online retailers and California stores, and ran them through authenticity testing. The results, published by <a href="https://www.ucdavis.edu/food/news/avocado-oil-chip-youre-eating-may-not-be-made-pure-avocado-oil" target="_blank" rel="noopener">UC Davis</a>, break down like this:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>93%</strong> of chips labeled "100% pure avocado oil" were adulterated</li>
<li><strong>71%</strong> of mayonnaise failed the same test</li>
<li><strong>100%</strong> of salad dressings failed</li>
<li><strong>89%</strong> across all avocado oil products tested</li>
</ul>

<p>This is not the first time. Back in 2020, the same lab tested bottled avocado oil itself and found that <a href="https://www.ucdavis.edu/food/news/study-finds-82-percent-avocado-oil-rancid-or-mixed-other-oils" target="_blank" rel="noopener">82% of samples were either stale before the expiration date printed on them, or mixed with other oils</a>. In three of those bottles, the product sold as pure or extra virgin avocado oil was found to be nearly 100% soybean oil.</p>

<p>The researcher who ran that testing put it plainly at the time: <em>"I was surprised some of the samples didn't contain any avocado oil."</em></p>

<p>Six years apart, two studies, and the number barely moved. That is the part worth sitting with. This was not a bad batch or one dishonest company. It is how the category has been operating the whole time you have been buying it.</p>

<h2 id="is-every-oil-like-this-or-is-it-just-avocado">Is every oil like this, or is it just avocado?</h2>

<p><em>Quick answer: In this round of testing, avocado was the outlier. Only 1 of 20 olive oil products failed the same purity test.</em></p>

<p>I want to be straight with you here, because the honest version of this is more useful than the scary version.</p>

<p>The same UC Davis team ran 20 products labeled with olive oil through the same testing, as a comparison group. One failed. Twenty tested, one problem. Against 89% for avocado.</p>

<p>So no, this is not a story about how everything in your kitchen is poison and nothing can be trusted. It is a story about one specific category where the fraud is unusually easy and unusually common, because avocado oil is expensive to produce, hard to test for, and carries a health halo that lets a bottle charge double.</p>

<p>That matters for you practically. It means you do not have to burn your kitchen down this week. You have one thing to look at, not forty. And if you have been feeling like the more you learn the more impossible it all gets, this is the rare case where the problem got smaller when someone actually measured it.</p>

<h2 id="so-what-do-i-cook-with-instead">So what do I cook with instead?</h2>

<p><em>Quick answer: Something you can trace to a person. The problem with the bottle was never the ingredient, it was that nobody could verify it.</em></p>

<p>Here is the trap in this whole conversation. The instinct is to go find a better bottle. A more expensive one, a fancier brand, one with a certification stamp on the front. But look at what the testing actually found. The bottles that failed were the premium ones. The "100% pure" ones. The label was not a signal of anything, because the label is the thing being faked.</p>

<p>You cannot solve a labeling problem by reading labels more carefully.</p>

<p>What ends the guessing is knowing where the fat came from and who rendered it. Beef tallow from a farmer whose cattle you can go look at is not a mystery product. It does not need a certification, because you did not need a stranger's word for it. It has been what American families cooked in for most of the country's history, right up until factories found something cheaper to sell.</p>

<h2 id="am-i-the-only-one-who-feels-lied-to-about-this">Am I the only one who feels lied to about this?</h2>

<p><em>Quick answer: No. There are active class action lawsuits from shoppers who felt exactly the way you do right now.</em></p>

<p>In September 2024, a shopper filed suit against Aldi in federal court in Illinois over its Simply Nature "100% Pure Avocado Oil." The complaint alleges the bottle contained undisclosed cheaper oils, and it cites independent testing from UC Davis that reportedly identified oleic sunflower and safflower oils in a product whose ingredient list named only refined avocado oil. Similar complaints have been filed over store brands at Target and Walmart. These are allegations, and the companies dispute them.</p>

<p>But that is a lot of people, in a lot of states, who stood in an aisle and made the same careful choice you made, and later found out the bottle was not what it said.</p>

<p>You are not paranoid and you are not gullible. You were sold something by people who knew you could not check.</p>

<h2 id="frequently-asked-questions">Frequently asked questions</h2>

<p class="qa"><strong>Is avocado oil bad for you?</strong><br>
That is not quite the right question, and it is the one the industry would rather you argue about. Real avocado oil is a fine cooking fat. The problem found in testing was that most products were not real avocado oil. You cannot evaluate whether something is good for you when you do not know what it is.</p>

<p class="qa"><strong>Can I tell if my bottle is fake at home?</strong><br>
Not reliably. The adulterants used are chosen specifically because they are hard to detect by taste, color or smell. UC Davis needed laboratory testing to find them. If a home test worked, this fraud would not be this widespread.</p>

<p class="qa"><strong>Is there a brand that passed?</strong><br>
In the 2020 bottled-oil testing, a small number of samples came back pure. But the 2026 results on processed foods show the problem got worse, not better, in the years since. Chasing the one brand that passed a test six years ago is still playing their game. It puts you back in the aisle, trusting a claim.</p>

<p class="qa"><strong>Am I overreacting about this?</strong><br>
No. There are federal lawsuits from shoppers who feel the same way, and a university lab that has now published on this three separate times because the problem kept not going away. Noticing something is off is not overreacting. It is the thing that makes you the one in your family who catches it.</p>

Not with your whole kitchen. Start by finding out which room in your house is actually the worst one, because for most families it is not the one they expect. That takes about a minute and costs nothing.</p>
