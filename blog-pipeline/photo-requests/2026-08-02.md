## Why the villain frame is built this way

On the cleaning posts the villain could be the bottle sitting alone in cold light. That
does not work for cookware, because a pan sitting on a counter is just a pan. The danger
in this story only exists at temperature. So the villain frame is the empty pan on a high
flame with the air above it visibly distorted by heat. No smoke and no fumes, because the
moment it reads as smoke it becomes a fire hazard photo instead of a coating photo, and
that is the wrong fear.

   said the oil should look "too pale and flat" and it came back nearly colourless, so it
   read as a bottle of water rather than a bottle of bad oil. When a brief asks for

## WHAT HAPPENED (read this, it changes what "done" means)

**1. Thirty images across six live posts were about to vanish.**

I audited every published post today. Six of them (bromate, Dawn, Fabuloso, Scentsy,
Mascara, Lysol) were serving **every single image straight off Higgsfield CloudFront
URLs**. Those links expire. When they did, thirty pictures would have disappeared from
the live blog silently, with no error, no alert, nothing. The exact same failure took
all five images off the live Ziploc post once already.

They are now rescued: pulled down, converted to WebP under 150 KB, committed into the
repo at `scan.ismyhometoxic.com/blog-images/`, and every live post repointed. Zero
expiring links anywhere on the blog now.

**What this means for you: nothing changes about how you deliver.** Keep dropping
CloudFront URLs into `photos-completed/` exactly as you do. There is now a script on
this end (`blog-images.py`) that pulls every delivery down, converts it, commits it to
the repo and rewrites the manifest to permanent URLs **before** anything is published.
The pipeline is fixed on my side. You do not have to compress anything, resize
anything, or host anything. Just keep delivering.

The one thing I would ask: if a delivery is ever a re-run or a replacement, say so in
the note, so I do not convert the wrong version.

**2. THE LABEL RULE CHANGED. Show the real branded product now.**

This reverses the old "no readable label text anywhere" instruction that has been in
every brief I have written you. Shannon, today, on your new Pine-Sol set:

> "PIne sol ones are perfect. I want the bottle to say Pine Sol. It has to relate or
> ppl won't read the blog post. These pictures are relatable. Pine sol is toxic. We
> talk about it, so let's show the actual real bottles."

So going forward:

- **DO** show the actual, recognisable, correctly-branded product the post is about.
  Right bottle shape, right colour, real front branding. A generic unlabelled bottle
  does not make a reader think of the thing under her own sink, which is the entire job.
- **DO** match the real product FORM and how it is actually used. Pine-Sol is poured
  into a mop bucket, not sprayed. An aerosol is sprayed. Get the gesture right.
- **DO** use text overlays that we author. Shannon asked for the "What's REALLY in your
  avocado oil?" overlay and called it perfect. If an overlay would land, use it.
- **STILL BANNED, and this is what the rule was always actually about:** a person
  **reading, holding up, squinting at or inspecting** a label, and any ingredient or
  back panel. The villain is the COMPANY. It is never the shopper studying fine print.

**3. Two craft notes from this week's sets.**

- **Continuity.** On the avocado set the hero was a grey-haired woman in a patterned
  cardigan and the turning-point hands belonged to somebody else in a navy sweater.
  The reader is meant to follow ONE woman through the arc. Same woman, same clothes,
  same hands in frames 1, 3 and 5 of every set. Frame 2 has no people. Frame 4 adds a
  second, clearly different woman on purpose.
- **Do not overshoot "subtly wrong."** The avocado villain brief asked for oil that
  looked "too pale and flat" and it came back nearly colourless, so it read as a bottle
  of water on a post about oil. Shannon rejected it outright. When a brief says
  something should look subtly wrong, subtle is the operative word.
