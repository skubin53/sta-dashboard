<h2>The short answer</h2>
<p>In February 2023 Colgate-Palmolive recalled about <strong>4.9 million bottles</strong> of Fabuloso Multi-Purpose Cleaner in the US, plus about 56,000 in Canada. Not for a toxic chemical. <a href="https://recalls-rappels.canada.ca/en/alert-recall/fabuloso-multi-purpose-cleaner-lavender-scent-165l-recalled-due-potential-microbial">Health Canada's notice</a> says the bottles "do not contain the intended levels of preservative which may lead to the growth of bacteria." The safety net was left short, and bugs could grow in the bottle.</p>
<p>Today's bottles are not those bottles. What is still true is what is on the label, and what is not. Colgate's own ingredient list has ten entries, and Colgate itself flags two of them: <strong>Fragrance</strong> and <strong>Glutaral</strong>. The amount of neither is disclosed, and US law does not make them tell you.</p>

<h2>What was the Fabuloso recall actually for?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: bacteria, caused by too little preservative. Not a toxic ingredient.</p>

<p>This is the part almost every article online gets backwards, so it is worth being precise. On February 8, 2023 the <a href="https://www.cpsc.gov/Recalls/2023/Colgate-Palmolive-Recalls-Fabuloso-Multi-Purpose-Cleaners-Due-to-Risk-of-Exposure-to-Bacteria">US Consumer Product Safety Commission announced Recall 23-115</a>, covering about 4.9 million bottles of Fabuloso Multi-Purpose Cleaner.</p>

<p>The hazard, in the CPSC's own words: the products "can contain Pseudomonas species bacteria, including Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Pseudomonas fluorescens." The notice says people with weakened immune systems, external medical devices, or underlying lung conditions "face a risk of serious infection that may require medical treatment," and that the bacteria "can enter the body if inhaled, through the eyes, or through a break in the skin."</p>

<p>Health Canada ran the Canadian arm of the same recall the same day, and its notice is the clearest statement of the cause anywhere: the products "do not contain the intended levels of preservative which may lead to the growth of bacteria and other microorganisms during the product's shelf life and during manufacturing."</p>

<p>Read that twice. A cleaning product was recalled because it did not have <em>enough</em> preservative in it. The thing that was supposed to keep the bottle from growing life was short.</p>

<p><strong>Nobody was reported hurt.</strong> CPSC lists incidents and injuries as "None reported," and Health Canada states the company had received no reports of incidents or injuries in either country as of February 6, 2023. That matters, and any article telling you otherwise is guessing.</p>

<h2>Are the bottles on shelves today affected?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: no. The recall covered a specific manufacturing window in December 2022 and January 2023.</p>

<p>The recalled product carries lot codes whose first eight digits run 2348US78 through 2365US78, and 3001US78 through 3023US78. It sold between December 2022 and January 2023 for $1 to $11 at Amazon, Dollar General, Family Dollar, Home Depot, Sam's Club and Walmart.</p>

<p>The CPSC notice also carries an explicit carve-out: "No Fabuloso Antibacterial variants or other Fabuloso products are impacted by this recall."</p>

<p>So if you are holding a bottle you bought last week, it is not a recalled bottle. The question worth asking is not about that batch. It is about what is in the bottle you have, all the time, by design.</p>

<h2>What is actually in Fabuloso?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: ten things, and Colgate flags two of them itself.</p>

<p>Colgate publishes the list on its own <a href="https://smartlabel.colgatepalmolive.com/#/fabuloso/en-us/35000530325">SmartLabel page</a>. For Fabuloso 2X Multi-Purpose Cleaner in Lavender, last updated July 22, 2026:</p>

<p>Water. Sodium Dodecylbenzenesulfonate. Fragrance. C9-11 Alketh-8. Deceth-8. Sodium C10-16 Alketh Sulfate. Sodium Laureth Sulfate. Citric Acid. Glutaral. Colorants.</p>

<p>Two of those ten carry Colgate's own "Designated List" symbol, which the page explains marks "ingredients below that are fragrance allergens, or that appear on Designated Lists." The two are <strong>Fragrance</strong> and <strong>Glutaral</strong>.</p>

<p>That is not an outside group's opinion. That is the manufacturer, on its own website, three weeks ago, pointing at two of its own ten ingredients.</p>

<h2>What is Glutaral, and why is Colgate flagging it?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: Glutaral is another name for glutaraldehyde, a preservative and biocide.</p>

<p>The New Jersey Department of Health's Right to Know fact sheet lists "Glutaral" as a synonym for glutaraldehyde, CAS 111-30-8, and describes its uses as including "a preservative, biocide, hardener, and tanning agent."</p>

<p>That sheet's hazard summary says contact with the liquid and vapor "can severely irritate and burn the skin and eyes," that inhaling it "can irritate the nose, throat and lungs causing coughing, wheezing and/or shortness of breath," and that it "may cause a skin allergy and an asthma-like allergy."</p>

<p>NIOSH thought the chemical was worth its own publication, <a href="https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/publications/hcp/numbered/2001-115.html">Glutaraldehyde: Occupational Hazards in Hospitals</a>, to make workers aware of its adverse health effects. California's OEHHA sets a <a href="https://oehha.ca.gov/air/allrels.html">chronic inhalation reference level of 0.08 micrograms per cubic meter</a> for it, with the respiratory system as the target organ.</p>

<p><strong>Here is the honest limit of all that.</strong> Those are workplace air limits, written for concentrated hospital-strength solutions, not for a mopped floor. And nobody outside Colgate knows how much glutaral is in a bottle of Fabuloso, because Colgate discloses that it is present and never discloses how much.</p>

<p>That is the actual finding. Not that the number is bad. That there is no number.</p>

<h2>What does "fragrance" hide, and why is that legal?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: one word can stand in for dozens of chemicals, and federal law allows it.</p>

<p>Colgate's own FAQ ends its ingredient answer with the line "Contains fragrance allergen(s)." It then names zero of them.</p>

<p>That is not a loophole Colgate invented. <a href="https://www.cpsc.gov/Business--Manufacturing/Business-Education/Business-Guidance/FHSA-Requirements">CPSC's own guidance</a> on the Federal Hazardous Substances Act lists what a hazardous-substance label must carry: a signal word, the principal hazards, the chemical name of the substances responsible for those hazards, precautions, first aid, storage, "keep out of reach of children," and the maker's address. A full ingredient list is not on that list.</p>

<p>The reason a Fabuloso ingredient list exists online at all is one state. California's <a href="https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billNavClient.xhtml?bill_id=201720180SB258">Cleaning Product Right to Know Act of 2017</a> made manufacturers post their intentionally added ingredients from January 2020. And even that law only forces a fragrance allergen to be named once it hits 0.01 percent of the product, and still lets a company withhold an ingredient as confidential business information.</p>

<p>No federal law made Colgate publish that list. One state did, nine years ago, and it still does not have to tell you what is in the smell.</p>

<h2>Can you use Fabuloso on counters where food goes?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: Colgate says it is not for dishes, and the EPA label for the antibacterial version restricts even a highchair to the non-food area.</p>

<p>Colgate's FAQ answers this directly: "Can I use Fabuloso to clean my dishes? No. Fabuloso products are not formulated or intended to be used on dishes."</p>

<p>The federally enforceable label tells you more. Fabuloso Complete Antibacterial is registered with EPA as <a href="https://www3.epa.gov/pesticides/chem_search/ppls/004582-00074-20210210.pdf">Reg. No. 4582-74</a>. Its directions say it is "to clean and sanitize hard, non-food, non-porous surfaces." In the approved list of places you may use it, a highchair appears with a qualifier: "Highchair (non food contact area)."</p>

<p>The tray your baby eats off is not on that list. The legs are.</p>

<p>Two more things on that label are worth your time. The active ingredient is L-lactic acid at 2.00 percent, which means the other <strong>98.00 percent is listed only as "inert ingredients,"</strong> and federal pesticide law does not require those to be named. And the "kills 99.9% of bacteria" claim is footnoted to exactly two organisms: Staphylococcus aureus and Klebsiella pneumoniae.</p>

<h2>Is it safe to simmer Fabuloso on the stove?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: no, and Colgate says so on its own website.</p>

<p>There is a trend of simmering cleaner on the stove to make a house smell nice. Colgate's FAQ answers it in one word: "Can I heat Fabuloso Professional, Fabuloso 2X Concentrated Formula, or Fabuloso Antibacterial to use as an air freshener or to refill a plug in? <strong>No, Fabuloso products should not be heated.</strong>"</p>

<p>The EPA-stamped label says the same thing in capitals: "DO NOT SWALLOW. DO NOT HEAT." It also says "Do not mix with other household cleaners to avoid irritating fumes," and Colgate's FAQ specifically rules out mixing any Fabuloso with chlorine bleach.</p>

<p>And on the Safety Data Sheet Colgate writes for industrial customers, the company classifies its own product as a skin irritant and a serious eye irritant, signal word "Warning," and recommends "protective gloves, eye protection, face protection." That is the same liquid that goes in a bucket with no gloves in a million kitchens.</p>

<h2>What did the EPA order off shelves in 2024?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: illegally imported Fabuloso disinfectant products, not the bottle at Walmart. The distinction matters and most articles blur it.</p>

<p>On May 6, 2024, the Kansas and Missouri Departments of Agriculture, working with EPA, <a href="https://www.epa.gov/newsreleases/kansas-city-grocery-stores-ordered-stop-selling-illegal-fabuloso-disinfectant-products">ordered 17 Kansas City-area grocery stores to stop selling certain Fabuloso household disinfectant products</a>. EPA said those products "contain an active ingredient, glutaraldehyde, that is not authorized in the U.S. as a household disinfectant," and listed potential health risks including "throat and lung irritation, asthma and difficulty breathing, dermatitis, nasal irritation, sneezing, wheezing, burning eyes, and conjunctivitis."</p>

<p>In August 2024, EPA Region 7 <a href="https://www.epa.gov/newsreleases/epa-region-7-orders-grocery-distributors-kansas-missouri-and-nebraska-stop-sale">ordered three distributors</a> in Missouri, Kansas and Nebraska to do the same.</p>

<p><strong>Be clear about what this was and was not.</strong> Those were illegally imported products with labels primarily in Spanish, sold into Hispanic neighbourhoods. EPA did not ban Fabuloso, and the US product on an ordinary shelf is not what was ordered off.</p>

<p>But hold two facts next to each other. EPA says glutaraldehyde is <em>not authorized as a household disinfectant active in the United States</em>. And Colgate's own US ingredient list has Glutaral on it, as a preservative rather than as a registered active, in an amount nobody outside the company knows.</p>

<h2>What should you use instead?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: something whose disinfecting ingredient is named on the front, in an amount you can read.</p>

<p>You do not have to fix your whole house tonight. Deal with the one you use most, which for most women is the floor cleaner, because it goes on the surface your toddler and your dog are on all day.</p>

<p>What you want is boring and specific: a cleaner that names its active ingredient, tells you the concentration, and does not use one word to cover the smell. If a company will tell you that before the law forces it to, that tells you something about the company.</p>

<h3>Shannon's Advice</h3>
<p class="advice-tag">Straight talk from a nutrition coach</p>
<p>I have never had a purple bottle under my sink. Not now, not thirty years ago when I was young and broke and nobody was handing me a list. I worked it out one product at a time and I got plenty of it wrong first, so I am not going to stand here and act like this is obvious.</p>
<p>Here is what actually gets me about this one. They recalled five million bottles because they did not put in enough of the chemical that stops things growing in there. Then the same label tells you, in their own words, not to heat it, not to wash dishes with it, and not to let it near bleach, and the sheet they write for their industrial customers says wear gloves and eye protection. Pick something that prints its active ingredient and the amount right on the front. If a company will tell you before anybody makes them, that is usually a company worth buying from.</p>

<h2>What else do people ask?</h2>

<p><strong>Is Fabuloso a disinfectant?</strong> Regular Fabuloso is not. Only the EPA-registered Antibacterial and Complete lines make sanitizing claims, and only on hard, non-porous, non-food surfaces.</p>

<p><strong>Does Fabuloso contain quats?</strong> The liquid does not. Fabuloso Complete Antibacterial's active is L-lactic acid at 2 percent. Four quaternary ammonium compounds do appear in the Fabuloso Complete Disinfecting Wipes, through the Lonza base registration those wipes are sold under.</p>

<p><strong>Is Fabuloso safe for pets?</strong> There is no veterinary body or poison centre warning about Fabuloso specifically, and the <a href="https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/aspca-poison-control/poisonous-household-products">ASPCA says</a> most cleaning products are safe when used per label directions. The one instruction worth following is Colgate's own, printed on the EPA label: "Store in a cool, well-ventilated area inaccessible to small children and pets."</p>

<p><strong>Was anyone hurt by the recalled bottles?</strong> Not according to the agencies. CPSC lists "None reported," and Health Canada said the company had received no reports of incidents or injuries in either country.</p>
