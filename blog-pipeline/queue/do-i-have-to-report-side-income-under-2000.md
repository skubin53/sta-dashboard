<h2 id="what-changed">What did the IRS change for 2026?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: one line for the business that pays you. It now sends a 1099-NEC at $2,000 or more. The first ones go out in early 2027.</p>

<p>It is a Saturday in November. The folding table is back in your trunk. The last wreath went out the door in tissue paper, and a payment just landed in your app.</p>

<p>Then somebody at the craft fair said it. Under $2,000, you do not even have to report it.</p>

<p>It sounds right, and the number is real. It just answers a different question.</p>

<p>The change came from the tax law signed July 4, 2025. It is in the <a href="https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1099mec">IRS instructions for the 1099-NEC</a>. For tax years after 2025, the line "increased to $2,000 and may be adjusted for inflation beginning in calendar year 2027."</p>

<p>The box 1a line reads, "Enter nonemployee compensation (NEC) of $2,000 or more." Nonemployee means you are not on their payroll.</p>

<p>This is year one. The IRS tells businesses to use these forms "to file 2026 information with the IRS in early 2027."</p>

<h2 id="still-counts">Does money under $2,000 still count?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: yes. The IRS says you report side money even with no form. Cash counts too.</p>

<p>The IRS said it plainly on March 31, in <a href="https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/filing-tips-and-updates-for-gig-economy-workers">Tax Tip 2026-26</a>. Its examples of gig work include "selling goods online." And it says you report that money even if it is:</p>

<ul>
<li>"From part-time, temporary or side work."</li>
<li>"Not reported on an information return form," like a 1099 or a W-2.</li>
<li>"Paid in any form, including cash, property, goods, or virtual currency."</li>
</ul>

<p>So the cash in the envelope counts. The payment in your app counts too.</p>

<p>Payment apps have their own line, and it is high. The same tip says you get a 1099-K if you "receive more than $20,000 and have over 200 transactions in a year." Under that, the app does not have to send one. The money still counts.</p>

<p>There is one more IRS line, and it is much lower. It sits on the <a href="https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/self-employment-tax-social-security-and-medicare-taxes">IRS self-employment tax page</a>. The IRS says you file Schedule SE when your "net earnings from self-employment" were "$400 or more." Net means what is left after your business costs.</p>

<p>Self-employment tax is the Social Security and Medicare you pay when you work for yourself. And it applies "no matter how old you are and even if you are already receiving Social Security or Medicare."</p>

<p>Now the part that cuts the other way. The change is real. Fewer forms may land in your mailbox this January. That is less paper. Your money counts the same.</p>

<p>The receipts in the glove box, the payments in two apps, the cash in the kitchen drawer, the price tags in your apron pocket, that feeling that the money was keeping track of you instead of the other way around.</p>

<h2 id="box-2">What is the box 2 checkbox on a 1099-NEC?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: an X a company marks when it sold you $5,000 or more of products to resell. No dollar amount goes in it.</p>

<p>If you buy products and sell them yourself, this box is about you.</p>

<p>The <a href="https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1099mec">IRS instructions for box 2</a> tell the company when to mark the X. It goes on when the company sold you "$5,000 or more of consumer products" to resell.</p>

<p>The sales have to happen "anywhere other than in a permanent retail establishment." That means outside a store, like your table at the fair.</p>

<p>The next line says, "Do not enter a dollar amount in this box." So the X tells the IRS you bought to resell. It does not show what you sold.</p>

<p>It may not even come on the form. The IRS says the report to you "may be in the form of a letter."</p>

<p>And so your own records carry the rest. Which is the part you control.</p>

<h2 id="records">What records should you keep for holiday sales?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: any system that "clearly shows your income and expenses." A notebook and two envelopes can do that.</p>

<p>The <a href="https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/what-kind-of-records-should-i-keep">IRS records page</a> says, "You may choose any recordkeeping system suited to your business." It does not ask for software.</p>

<h3>Write each sale down the day it happens</h3>

<p>The date, what sold, and how they paid. Cash, check or app. The IRS calls this money your gross receipts. Its list of proof includes "receipt books" and "deposit information."</p>

<h3>Keep the paper for what you bought</h3>

<p>The IRS says "purchases are the items you buy and resell to customers." Other business costs it calls expenses. Keep the receipt, the card statement or the invoice for each.</p>

<h3>Sort it by year</h3>

<p>The IRS says to keep papers "in an orderly fashion and in a safe place." Its example is to "organize them by year and type of income or expense." One envelope for money in, one for money out. Start a new pair every January.</p>

<p>Side money you cannot track is money you cannot build on. With a record, you can see what sold and what sat. And so you can plan next year.</p>
