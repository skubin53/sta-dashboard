<h2 id="the-short-answer">The short answer</h2>
<p>In 2021 researchers at the University of Notre Dame tested more than 200 cosmetics sold in the United States and Canada for fluorine, which is the marker for PFAS, the forever chemicals.</p>
<p><strong><a href="https://news.nd.edu/news/use-of-pfas-in-cosmetics-widespread-new-study-finds/">Forty eight percent of the lip products came back high. Among long lasting lipsticks it was sixty two percent.</a></strong></p>
<p>The word most people never got told is where lipstick goes. It is on the part of your face you eat with. You put it on in the morning and by evening a good deal of it is not on your mouth any more.</p>

<h2 id="what-did-the-study-find">What did the study actually find?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: high fluorine in roughly half of lip products, and highest of all in the long wear ones.</p>

<p>The Notre Dame team screened 231 cosmetics bought in the United States and Canada, including foundations, concealers, eye products and lip products. <a href="https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/hold-blush-cosmetics-may-contain-toxic-forever-chemicals-180978036/">They were looking for total fluorine, which is the practical way to flag PFAS without testing for hundreds of individual compounds one at a time.</a></p>

<p>Lip products came back at forty eight percent high fluorine. Long lasting lipstick specifically came in at sixty two percent. Waterproof mascara and long wear foundation ran high for the same reason.</p>

<p>The study won the 2021 Best Paper Award from its journal, <a href="https://science.nd.edu/news/study-about-pfas-in-cosmetics-gains-distinction-with-2021-best-paper-award/">Environmental Science and Technology Letters</a>, which is not nothing for a piece of work that made an entire industry uncomfortable.</p>

<p><strong>The finding that matters most is not the percentage.</strong> It is that the great majority of the products testing high did not list any PFAS on the label at all.</p>

<h2 id="why-is-it-in-lipstick">Why would anyone put forever chemicals in a lipstick?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: because they are what makes it stay on.</p>

<p>PFAS are used for water resistance and film forming. That is the entire appeal. They spread smoothly, they resist water and oil, and they hold a film in place for hours.</p>

<p>Which is exactly what a long wear lipstick is sold on. Eight hour wear. Transfer proof. Kiss proof. Those claims on the front of the packaging and the chemistry inside the tube are the same fact described two different ways.</p>

<p><strong>So the marketing was never lying to you.</strong> It told you plainly that this one stays put. It just never explained what makes it stay put, or that the same property is why these compounds are called forever chemicals in the first place. They do not break down easily in a landfill, in water, or in a person.</p>

<p>And here is the part that decides it for me. If a company is using them and not putting them on the ingredient list, then studying that list gets you nowhere. Half the products in that study would have passed your inspection in the shop.</p>

<h2 id="what-is-in-the-tube">What else is in the tube?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: pigment, wax, oil, preservative, fragrance, and whatever came along for the ride.</p>

<h3>PFAS, the fluorinated compounds</h3>
<p>The long wear mechanism. Found at high fluorine levels in forty eight percent of lip products tested and sixty two percent of long lasting lipsticks, mostly without appearing on the label.</p>

<h3>Lead</h3>
<p>Never an added ingredient. It turns up as a trace contaminant in colour additives, which is why <a href="https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetic-products/fda-issues-final-guidance-industry-lead-cosmetic-lip-products-and-externally-applied-cosmetics">the FDA issued guidance recommending a maximum of 10 parts per million of lead in lip products</a>. Guidance is the word to notice. It is a recommendation, not a limit anybody has to meet.</p>

<h3>Fragrance</h3>
<p>One word standing in for a mixture. <a href="https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetic-ingredients/fragrances-cosmetics">The FDA permits fragrance combinations to be listed as a single word</a> because they are treated as trade secrets. On a lip product it is sitting on skin you lick.</p>

<h3>Parabens</h3>
<p>Preservatives, and the reason a tube lasts two years in a warm handbag. They are permitted here. The EU restricted several of them and prohibited others outright, which is the same two systems, two answers pattern that runs through most of this.</p>

<h3>Wax and oil base</h3>
<p>Usually the least interesting part of the tube, and the part nobody argues about.</p>

<h2 id="does-that-mean-it-is-hurting-me">Does that mean it is hurting me?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: the study found the chemicals. It did not follow anybody around to see what they did.</p>

<p>Total fluorine is a screening marker. A high result says fluorinated compounds are present, and the researchers confirmed PFAS in a smaller subset they tested in detail. It is not a measurement of what enters a person or what it does once there.</p>

<p>The FDA has not banned PFAS in cosmetics. There is no recall here, no fine, no court finding. That matters and I am not going to leave it out to make the story tidier.</p>

<p>What I am left with is simpler than a study. Roughly half the lip products tested contained a class of chemicals known for not breaking down, most of them did not say so, and the product goes on the one part of your face you cannot help swallowing from. Nobody needed to prove harm for that to be enough for me.</p>

<h2 id="why-this-matters-after-forty">Why does this one land differently after forty?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: because it is thirty years of daily use, not one night out.</p>

<p>Lipstick is not an occasional product for most women my age. It went on before the school run, before work, before every wedding and every funeral. Call it two hundred days a year for thirty years and the number gets uncomfortable.</p>

<p>And nobody is coming off it. I am not going to tell a woman to stop wearing lipstick, because that is not the trade being offered here. The colour is not the problem. The company behind the tube is.</p>

<h2 id="what-do-i-use-instead">What do I use instead?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: colour from a company that answers to its members rather than to a shelf, and a lower tolerance for the words long wear.</p>

<p>Start with the claim rather than the shade. Eight hour wear, transfer proof and kiss proof are the language of a film that is engineered to stay, and that engineering is where the fluorine showed up in the study. A lipstick you reapply after lunch is a lipstick doing less chemistry on your mouth.</p>

<p>I am not going to send you to the counter to inspect the ingredient list. The Notre Dame result is the proof that will not work. Most of the products that tested high never listed it. You cannot read what a company chose not to print.</p>

<p>That is why I stopped shopping in those stores altogether and moved my family onto a private membership store. Not a different shelf. A different supply chain, with a name attached to it and a person I can call.</p>

<h2 id="faq">What else do people ask?</h2>

<p><strong>Does the study name the brands that tested high?</strong> The published work reported results by product category rather than publishing a brand by brand list, which is one of the honest frustrations of it. The pattern by category is the usable finding.</p>

<p><strong>Is a lip balm safer than a lipstick?</strong> Balms are usually simpler, with fewer pigments and no long wear film. The study found the highest fluorine in the products engineered to stay put, and a balm is not one of those.</p>

<p><strong>Is drugstore worse than department store?</strong> No. Price was not the pattern. Products at both ends of the market tested high, which is why the answer is who made it rather than what it cost.</p>

<p><strong>Does high fluorine prove PFAS harmed anyone?</strong> No, and this post does not claim it. Fluorine is a screening marker for the presence of these compounds. It measures what is in the tube, not what happens in a person.</p>
