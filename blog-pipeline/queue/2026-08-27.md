<h2 id="the-short-answer">The short answer</h2>
<p>In 2021, the University of Notre Dame tested more than 200 makeup products. They looked for fluorine. Fluorine is the flag for PFAS, the forever chemicals.</p>
<p><strong><a href="https://news.nd.edu/news/use-of-pfas-in-cosmetics-widespread-new-study-finds/">Forty eight percent of the lip products came back high. Among long lasting lipsticks it was sixty two percent.</a></strong></p>
<p>Now think about where lipstick sits. On the part of your face you eat with.</p>

<p>You put it on in the morning. By night a lot of it is gone. It did not fall off.</p>

<h2 id="what-did-the-study-find">What did the study actually find?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: about half the lip products. Worst in the long wear ones.</p>

<p>The team tested 231 products bought in shops here and in Canada. Foundation. Concealer. Eye makeup. Lip products.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/hold-blush-cosmetics-may-contain-toxic-forever-chemicals-180978036/">They measured total fluorine.</a> It is the quick way to flag PFAS. You do not have to test for hundreds of them one at a time.</p>

<p>Lip products came back at 48 percent high. Long lasting lipstick came back at 62 percent.</p>

<p>Waterproof mascara ran high too. Same reason.</p>

<p>This was not a fringe study. <a href="https://science.nd.edu/news/study-about-pfas-in-cosmetics-gains-distinction-with-2021-best-paper-award/">Its journal gave it the 2021 Best Paper Award.</a></p>

<p><strong>The number is not the point.</strong> Most of the products that tested high did not list PFAS at all.</p>

<h2 id="why-is-it-in-lipstick">Why would anyone put forever chemicals in a lipstick?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: because they are what makes it stay on.</p>

<p>PFAS shrug off water and oil. They spread smooth. They hold a thin film in place for hours.</p>

<p>That is the whole appeal.</p>

<p>Which is what a long wear lipstick is sold on. Eight hour wear. Transfer proof. Kiss proof.</p>

<p>The promise on the front and the chemistry in the tube are one thing said twice.</p>

<p><strong>So the ad was never lying to you.</strong> It said this one stays put. It just never said what makes it stay put.</p>

<p>That same trait is why they are called forever chemicals. They do not break down easily. Not in a dump. Not in water. Not in a person.</p>

<p>Here is the part that decides it for me.</p>

<p>If they use it and do not list it, reading the list gets you nowhere. Half the products in that study would have passed your check in the shop.</p>

<h2 id="what-is-in-the-tube">What else is in the tube?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: colour, wax, oil, fragrance, and whatever came along too.</p>

<h3>PFAS, the fluorinated compounds</h3>
<p>This is what makes it last. High fluorine showed up in 48 percent of lip products and 62 percent of the long wear ones. Mostly with nothing on the label.</p>

<h3>Lead</h3>
<p>Nobody adds lead. It rides in with the colour. That is why <a href="https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetic-products/fda-issues-final-guidance-industry-lead-cosmetic-lip-products-and-externally-applied-cosmetics">the FDA suggests a top level of 10 parts per million in lip products</a>.</p>

<p>Watch the word suggests. It is advice. Nobody has to meet it.</p>

<h3>Fragrance</h3>
<p>One word covering a whole mix. <a href="https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetic-ingredients/fragrances-cosmetics">The FDA lets them use just that one word</a>, because the recipe is a trade secret.</p>

<p>On a lipstick, it sits on skin you lick.</p>

<h3>Parabens</h3>
<p>These keep a tube good for two years in a warm handbag. They are allowed here.</p>

<p>Europe limited some and banned others. Two systems. Two answers.</p>

<h3>Wax and oil base</h3>
<p>The dull part of the tube. Nobody argues about this bit.</p>

<h2 id="does-that-mean-it-is-hurting-me">Does that mean it is hurting me?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: the study found the chemicals. It did not follow anyone.</p>

<p>Fluorine is a flag, not a verdict. A high result says these compounds are in there.</p>

<p>The team did confirm PFAS in a smaller batch they checked closely.</p>

<p>But it does not measure what gets into a person, or what it does.</p>

<p>The FDA has not banned PFAS in makeup. No recall. No fine. No court ruling.</p>

<p>That matters, and I am not leaving it out.</p>

<p>What I am left with is simpler than a study.</p>

<p>Half the lip products held chemicals that do not break down. Most did not say so. And it goes on the one part of your face you swallow from.</p>

<p>Nobody had to prove harm for that to be enough for me.</p>

<h2 id="why-this-matters-after-forty">Why does this one land differently after forty?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: because this is thirty years of days, not one night out.</p>

<p>Lipstick is not a now and then thing for women my age.</p>

<p>It went on before the school run. Before work. Before every wedding and every funeral.</p>

<p>Two hundred days a year, for thirty years. Do that sum once.</p>

<p>And nobody is giving it up. I would not ask you to.</p>

<p>The colour is not the problem. The company behind the tube is.</p>

<p>Start with the promise, not the shade.</p>

<p>Eight hour wear. Transfer proof. Kiss proof. Those words describe a film built to stay. That is where the fluorine showed up.</p>

<p>A lipstick you put on again after lunch is doing less to your mouth.</p>

<p>I am not sending you to the counter to read the ingredient list.</p>

<p>The Notre Dame result is proof that will not work. Most of the ones that tested high never listed it.</p>

<p>You cannot read what they chose not to print.</p>

<h2 id="faq">What else do people ask?</h2>

<p><strong>Does the study name brands?</strong> No. It reported by product type, not brand by brand. That is a fair frustration. The pattern by type is what you can use.</p>

<p><strong>Is a lip balm safer?</strong> Balms are simpler. Less colour, and no long wear film. The highest fluorine was in the products built to stay put. A balm is not one of those.</p>

<p><strong>Is drugstore worse than department store?</strong> No. Price was not the pattern. Cheap and dear both tested high. That is why the answer is who made it, not what it cost.</p>

<p><strong>Does high fluorine prove harm?</strong> No, and I am not saying it does. Fluorine is a flag that these compounds are present. It measures the tube, not the person.</p>
