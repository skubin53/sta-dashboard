<h2 id="the-short-answer">The short answer</h2>
<p>You put your lipstick on before you left the house this morning. By tonight most of it is gone off your lips. It did not fall off.</p>
<p>In 2021 the University of Notre Dame tested more than 200 makeup products for fluorine. Fluorine is the flag for PFAS, the forever chemicals.</p>
<p><strong><a href="https://news.nd.edu/news/use-of-pfas-in-cosmetics-widespread-new-study-finds/">Forty eight percent of the lip products came back high. Among long lasting lipsticks it was sixty two percent.</a></strong></p>

<p>That is the one part of your face you eat with.</p>

<h2 id="what-did-the-study-find">What did the study actually find?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: about half the lip products. Worst in the long wear ones.</p>

<p>The team tested 231 products bought in shops here and in Canada. Foundation, concealer, eye makeup, lip products.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/hold-blush-cosmetics-may-contain-toxic-forever-chemicals-180978036/">They measured total fluorine.</a> It is the quick way to flag PFAS without testing for hundreds of them one at a time.</p>

<p>Lip products came back at 48 percent high. Long lasting lipstick came back at 62 percent.</p>

<p>Waterproof mascara ran high too. Same reason.</p>

<p>This was not a fringe study. <a href="https://science.nd.edu/news/study-about-pfas-in-cosmetics-gains-distinction-with-2021-best-paper-award/">Its journal gave it the 2021 Best Paper Award.</a></p>

<p><strong>The number is not the point.</strong> Most of the tubes that tested high did not list PFAS at all.</p>

<h2 id="why-is-it-in-lipstick">Why would anyone put forever chemicals in a lipstick?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: because they are what makes it stay on.</p>

<p>PFAS shrug off water and oil, and they hold a thin smooth film in place for hours.</p>

<p>That is the whole appeal.</p>

<p>Which is what long wear lipstick is sold on. Eight hour wear, transfer proof, kiss proof.</p>

<p>The promise on the front and the chemistry in the tube are one thing said twice.</p>

<p><strong>So the ad was never lying to you.</strong> It said this one stays put. It just never said what makes it stay put.</p>

<p>That is why they are called forever chemicals. They do not break down easily, not in a landfill, not in water, and not in you.</p>

<p>Here is the part that decides it for me.</p>

<p>If they use it and do not print it, turning the tube over gets you nowhere. Half the products in that study would have passed your check in the shop.</p>

<h2 id="what-is-in-the-tube">What else is in the tube?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: colour, wax, oil, fragrance, and whatever came along for the ride.</p>

<h3>PFAS, the forever chemicals</h3>
<p>This is what makes it last. High fluorine showed up in 48 percent of lip products and 62 percent of the long wear ones. Mostly with nothing on the box.</p>

<h3>Lead</h3>
<p>Nobody adds lead on purpose. It rides in with the colour.</p>

<p>There is <a href="https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/potential-contaminants-cosmetics/lead-cosmetics">a federal paper suggesting a top level of 10 parts per million in lip products</a>.</p>

<p>Watch that word. Suggesting. It is advice, and nobody has to meet it. Nobody is checking your tube either. The company selling it is the only one deciding what goes in.</p>

<h3>Fragrance</h3>
<p>One word covering a whole mix. <a href="https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetic-ingredients/fragrances-cosmetics">The rules here let them print that one word and stop</a>, because the recipe counts as a trade secret.</p>

<p>So the tube can say fragrance and mean thirty things. And it sits on skin you lick all day.</p>

<h3>Parabens</h3>
<p>These keep a tube good for two years in a warm handbag. They are allowed here.</p>

<p>Europe restricted some and banned others outright. Same tube, two answers, and you live on the side that says yes.</p>

<h3>Wax and oil base</h3>
<p>The dull part of the tube. Nobody argues about this bit, and I am not going to pretend they do.</p>

<p>Here is what is in your handbag, and what each row does not prove.</p>

<h2 id="does-that-mean-it-is-hurting-me">Does that mean it is hurting me?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: the study found the chemicals. It did not follow anyone.</p>

<p>Fluorine is a flag, not a verdict. A high result says these compounds are in the tube.</p>

<p>The team did confirm PFAS in a smaller batch they checked closely.</p>

<p>It does not measure what got into you, or what it did once it was there.</p>

<p>Nobody has banned PFAS in makeup here. No recall on it, no fine, no court ruling.</p>

<p>That matters, and I am not leaving it out.</p>

<p>What I am left with is simpler than a study.</p>

<p>Half the lip products held chemicals that do not break down. Most of them said nothing on the box. And it goes on the one part of your face you swallow from.</p>

<p>Nobody had to prove harm for that to be enough for me.</p>

<h2 id="why-this-matters-after-forty">Why does this one land differently after forty?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: because this is thirty years of days, not one night out.</p>

<p>You have not worn lipstick now and then.</p>

<p>You put it on before work, before school drop off, before every wedding and every funeral.</p>

<p>Two hundred days a year for thirty years. Do that sum once.</p>

<p>And your lips are not like the skin on the back of your hand. They are thin, you lick them, you drink your coffee straight through them.</p>

<p>You are not giving it up. I am not asking you to. I wear lipstick and I am not putting it down.</p>

<p>The colour is not the problem. The company behind the tube is.</p>

<h2 id="what-do-i-use-instead">What do I use instead?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: colour from a company that answers to the people shopping with it. And less patience with the words long wear.</p>

<p>Start with the promise on the front, not the shade.</p>

<p>Eight hour wear, transfer proof, kiss proof. Every one of those words describes a film built to stay put, and that is where the fluorine turned up.</p>

<p>A lipstick you put on again after lunch is doing less to your mouth.</p>

<p>I am not sending you to the makeup counter to squint at tiny print on the back of a tube.</p>

<p>You already know how that goes. You stand there turning it over, pretending you know what you are looking at.</p>

<p>The joke is on you. They are not even printing the worst of it.</p>

<p>The Notre Dame result proves that. Most of the tubes that tested high never listed it.</p>

<p>You cannot read what they chose not to print.</p>

<p>That is why my family buys through a private membership store instead. We are the manufacturer, so there is no middleman and no markup. One name on it, and a person who picks up the phone.</p>

<h2 id="faq">What else do people ask?</h2>

<p><strong>Does the study name the brands?</strong> No. It reported by product type, not tube by tube. That is a fair thing to be annoyed about. The pattern by type is what you can use.</p>

<p><strong>Is a lip balm safer?</strong> Balms are simpler. Less colour, and no film built to last eight hours. The highest fluorine sat in the ones engineered to stay put, and a balm is not one of those.</p>

<p><strong>Is drugstore worse than department store?</strong> No. Price was not the pattern. The cheap ones and the expensive ones both tested high. Which is why the question is who made it, not what you paid.</p>

<p><strong>Does high fluorine prove harm?</strong> No, and I am not saying it does. Fluorine is a flag that these compounds are in there. It measures the tube, not you.</p>
