<h2 id="what-did-they-promise">What did the company promise you?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: your memory back, "clinically shown," from a protein first found in a jellyfish.</p>

<p>Quincy Bioscience makes Prevagen. <a href="https://ag.ny.gov/press-release/2024/attorney-general-james-wins-trial-against-quincy-bioscience-deceptive-and">New York's attorney general says Quincy put memory claims on the box from at least 2011.</a> National TV and radio ads followed in 2013, the state says.</p>

<p>One TV ad asked, <a href="https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/cases/quincy_bioscience_complaint-filed_version.pdf">"Can a protein originally found in the jellyfish improve your memory?"</a> Then it answered itself. "Our scientists say yes."</p>

<p>By the state's count, <a href="https://ag.ny.gov/press-release/2024/attorney-general-james-wins-trial-against-quincy-bioscience-deceptive-and">a 30-day supply can cost up to $89.95</a>.</p>

<p>The attorney general said Quincy "took advantage of the very real fear of cognitive decline." That fear is real. The state says it is what Quincy sold to.</p>

<h2 id="what-is-in-it">What is actually in Prevagen?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: a jellyfish protein, and the state says Quincy's own papers doubted it ever reaches your brain.</p>

<h3>Apoaequorin, the jellyfish protein</h3>

<p>The box is sold on a protein called apoaequorin, first found in a jellyfish that glows. Newer boxes add vitamin D.</p>

<p>The pitch was simple. Swallow the protein and it replaces what your brain loses with age.</p>

<p>But protein gets digested. <a href="https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/cases/quincy_bioscience_2019.02.20_dkt_50_opinion_of_2nd_circuit.pdf">The FTC alleged Quincy's own safety tests found it is "rapidly digested in the stomach."</a></p>

<p>At trial, the state says, Quincy's own papers admitted it is <a href="https://ag.ny.gov/press-release/2024/attorney-general-james-wins-trial-against-quincy-bioscience-deceptive-and">quickly digested and unlikely to reach the brain</a>.</p>

<p>So the pill promised to feed your brain. By the state's account, Quincy's own files said it likely never gets there.</p>

<h2 id="what-did-the-jury-find">What did the jury find?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: none of the eight claims had reliable science behind them. That includes "clinically shown to improve memory."</p>

<p>The FTC and New York sued Quincy in January 2017. The FTC said Quincy leaned on one study it ran itself.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/cases/quincy_bioscience_complaint-filed_version.pdf">The FTC alleged that study beat a dummy pill on none of nine memory and thinking tests.</a> Then, it alleged, the results were sliced more than 30 other ways. Most still showed nothing, the complaint says.</p>

<p><a href="https://ag.ny.gov/press-release/2024/attorney-general-james-wins-trial-against-quincy-bioscience-deceptive-and">In 2024 the case went to a two-week trial in Manhattan.</a> And so a jury got to weigh all of it.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/quincy_order_and_judgment.pdf">The jury found none of the eight claims was backed by "competent and reliable scientific evidence."</a> On November 18, 2024, Judge Louis L. Stanton ordered Quincy to "immediately remove" all eight.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/quincy_order.pdf">Weeks later he spelled out that the ban runs nationwide.</a> He wrote that Quincy kept using the claims after trial. To him, that was "a near assurance" Quincy would do it again.</p>

<p>So the words that sold you that box? A federal judge ordered them off it.</p>

<h2 id="the-other-side">What if it helps me anyway?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: you may feel it does. The judge saw no evidence of harm, but no reliable science showed it works.</p>

<p>The case was not a clean sweep. Here is the other side, straight.</p>

<p><strong>One.</strong> <a href="https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/quincy_order_and_judgment.pdf">The jury found only two of the eight claims "materially misleading."</a> That means misleading on a point that matters to a buyer. Both were about memory problems that come with age.</p>

<p><strong>Two.</strong> The judge wrote there was "no evidence that Prevagen, or the challenged statements, had actually caused harm." He also turned down New York's demand for money.</p>

<p><strong>Three.</strong> The court did not ban Prevagen, and it is still sold. <a href="https://www.mintz.com/insights-center/viewpoints/2406/2026-03-11-cognitive-dissonance-ii-oral-arguments-second-circuit">Quincy appealed, and three appeals judges heard the case on February 26, 2026.</a></p>

<p>So why does it still sit wrong with me? Because "no harm" is not the same as "it works." You paid for memory. Nobody could show you the science for it.</p>

<p>And I will say the hard part out loud. Nothing I tell you below is a memory fix, and I am not going to promise you one.</p>

<h2 id="faq">What else do people ask?</h2>

<p><strong>Is Prevagen safe to take?</strong></p>
<p>The case was not about safety. The judge wrote there was no evidence Prevagen had caused harm. The jury found that none of its eight memory claims had reliable science behind them.</p>

<p><strong>Is Prevagen still sold in stores?</strong></p>
<p>Yes. The court did not ban it. It ordered the eight memory claims off every box and ad. Quincy has appealed.</p>

<p><strong>Did Quincy have to pay a fine?</strong></p>
<p>No. The judge turned down New York's demand for penalties. He found no proof of damages. What Quincy lost was the words that sold the box.</p>

<p><strong>Was I foolish to buy it?</strong></p>
<p>No. You bought a box that said "clinically shown" from a store you trusted. A jury later found that claim had no reliable science behind it. The mistake was the company's, not yours.</p>
