<h2 id="raise">Why did my raise stay small when my review said exceeds?</h2>

<h2 id="google">What did Google get sued for?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: the lawsuit said Google put women in lower levels than men. Google denied it and agreed to pay $118 million to settle.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.cnbc.com/2019/06/11/gender-pay-gap-womens-earnings-peak-11-years-before-mens-payscale.html">A 2019 Payscale study of 1.4 million workers found college-educated women's pay peaked at 44.</a> Men's kept climbing until 55.</p>

<p>Something stops the climb. At Google, a lawsuit said it was the level.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.seattletimes.com/business/google-to-pay-118-million-to-more-than-15000-women-in-gender-bias-settlement/">The suit was filed in 2017 in San Francisco County Superior Court.</a> It accused Google of slotting women into lower salary levels than men. It also said Google promoted women more slowly.</p>

<p>In June 2022 Google agreed to pay $118 million to settle. The deal covered up to 15,500 women in 236 job titles. Google denied wrongdoing. It said the deal came "without any admission or findings."</p>

<p><a href="https://abcnews.go.com/Business/google-pay-38-million-alleged-discrimination-women-asians/story?id=75633080">In February 2021 Google had already agreed to pay more than $3.8 million.</a> The Labor Department said a routine review found pay gaps hitting female software engineers. Google denied any violations.</p>

<p>Google denied wrongdoing both times, and it agreed to pay both times. If your company has levels too, find out which one you were handed.</p>

<h2 id="level">How does a level put a lid on your pay?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: your raise is a percentage inside a pay band. Every band has a top.</p>

<h3>The level you are handed on day one</h3>

<p>You get a level when you are hired. That level sets the range your pay can move in. Start one level low. Every raise after that is a percentage of a smaller number.</p>

<h3>The top of the band in your forties</h3>

<p>Climb long enough and you reach the top of your band. Up there, a great review has very little room left to move you.</p>

<h3>The promotion that opens the next band</h3>

<p>The only way out of a band is up a level. That is the exact step the lawsuit said Google gave women more slowly.</p>

<p>Look at the row about when your raise gets decided. That is the one you have been living.</p>

<h2 id="against">Isn't this just because I took time off for family?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: that is Payscale's own best guess. It still leaves your great review unexplained.</p>

<p>That is a fair question. <a href="https://www.payscale.com/featured-content/gender-pay-gap">Payscale gives its most likely reason</a> the gap grows with age. It is "women becoming mothers and taking lower-paying jobs." That is a real reason. I am not going to wave it away.</p>

<p>Google has a defense too. <a href="https://www.seattletimes.com/business/google-to-pay-118-million-to-more-than-15000-women-in-gender-bias-settlement/">In 2022 it said it had run a pay-equity check for nine years.</a> In 2020 alone, it raised pay for 2,352 employees.</p>

<p>So I will not tell you it is all the level. I cannot promise you a raise, and I will not promise you any income either.</p>

<p>But look at what the deal asked for. An outside economist was to review Google's pay studies. A separate psychologist was to look at how Google assigns job levels at hiring. That second fix went straight to the level.</p>

<p>And you are not the only one doing this math. Up to 15,500 women were covered by the Google settlement alone.</p>

<p>Email HR one plain question. What is my level, and what is the top of my band? The answer tells you how much room is left. And so it tells you about your next raise too.</p>
