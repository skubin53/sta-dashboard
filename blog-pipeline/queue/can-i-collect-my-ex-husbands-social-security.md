<h2 id="can-you">Can you really collect on your ex-husband's record?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: yes, if the marriage lasted 10 years and you are single now. Nothing in the rule asks for his permission.</p>

<p>You still know his Social Security number by heart. It sat on every tax return you signed for years.</p>

<p>Social Security asks for it when you call for an estimate. Without it, they ask for his birth date and his parents' names.</p>

<p>Now his side. Usually he has to be getting his own benefit already.</p>

<p>If he has not filed yet, you can still claim. He must be at least 62, and you must be divorced at least 2 years. <a href="https://secure.ssa.gov/poms.nsf/lnx/0300202005">Social Security's staff manual</a> says he "need not have filed a claim for benefits."</p>

<p>So the door is real. The question is what is behind it.</p>

<h2 id="how-much">How much would you actually get?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: at most half of his full amount, and only if you wait until 67. At 62 it is about a third.</p>

<p>Here is the math.</p>

<p>Say he would get $100 at his full retirement age. The most you can get is $50. That is only at your own full retirement age. If you were born in 1960 or later, that is 67.</p>

<p>Start at 62 and <a href="https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-20/section-404.410">the rulebook cuts it</a>. Claiming 3 years early takes 25 percent off. Claiming 2 more years early takes 10 percent more. And so your $50 becomes $32.50.</p>

<p>It does not come on top of your own check. Social Security says it plainly: "We don't add the payment amounts together." You get whichever is bigger.</p>

<p>If yours is already bigger than half of his, his record adds nothing. And there used to be a way to use both records.</p>

<h2 id="the-2015-law">What did a 2015 law take away from you?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: taking his half while your own kept growing. If you were born on or after January 2, 1954, that is gone.</p>

<p>Under the old rule, at full retirement age, you could take only the ex-spouse check. Your own kept growing.</p>

<p>On November 2, 2015, a budget law changed that. <a href="https://secure.ssa.gov/poms.nsf/lnx/0200204035">Social Security's staff manual</a> says it covers anyone born "January 2, 1954 or later."</p>

<p>Now, applying for one counts as applying for both. The manual calls it deemed filing.</p>

<p>That is you, and nobody asked you. And so what is left on his record is one choice: when to start.</p>

<p>There is one place the old choice still lives. Social Security's own watchdog checked it this year.</p>

<h2 id="if-he-dies">What happens if he dies first?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: it can rise to all of his amount, and your choice comes back. A March 2026 audit looked at how that choice gets handled.</p>

<p>If he dies, you claim as his surviving divorced spouse. <a href="https://www.ssa.gov/survivor/amount">Social Security says</a> payments start at 71.5 percent of his benefit. They can reach 100 percent at your full retirement age.</p>

<p>The 2015 law skipped this one. The staff manual says deemed filing "does not apply to survivor benefits." So you could take his check first, then switch to your own at 70 if yours is bigger.</p>

<p>In March 2026, Social Security's own Inspector General checked how the agency pays widows and widowers. <a href="https://oig.ssa.gov/~assets/uploads/032409.pdf">It estimated</a> the agency underpaid <strong>8,618 of them about $50.4 million</strong>. It says staff got the math wrong when the spouse died before 62.</p>

<p>It also pulled 50 files where both checks started the same month. In 37, nobody wrote down whether the choice to wait was explained. The report says that does not mean nobody explained it.</p>

<p>But Social Security will not reopen those 37 claims. The report warns of outcomes that "may be irreversible."</p>

<p>The same office raised this in 2018. As of January 2026, the report says, the agency had added no "enhancements or new controls."</p>

<p>The rules are theirs, and so is the math.</p>

<h2 id="faq">What else are women asking me about this?</h2>

<p><strong>Can I collect his and then switch to mine?</strong></p>
<p>Not the way it used to work. If you were born on or after January 2, 1954, applying for one counts as applying for both. You get whichever is bigger. The switch still works on a survivor claim.</p>

<p><strong>What if I remarry?</strong></p>
<p>In most cases, <a href="https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-20/section-404.332">the ex-spouse check stops</a> if you marry someone else. <a href="https://www.ssa.gov/survivor/eligibility">Survivor benefits</a> are different. A wedding at 60 or later keeps that door open.</p>

<p><strong>What if he remarries?</strong></p>
<p>It does not change yours. Payments to ex-spouses do not count toward the family maximum. So your check and his new wife's do not shrink each other.</p>

<p><strong>Can I collect it and still work?</strong></p>
<p>Yes, with a yearly limit before your full retirement age. <a href="https://www.ssa.gov/family/reporting">Social Security lists</a> wages over $24,480 in 2026 as something to report. <a href="https://www.ssa.gov/news/en/cola/factsheets/2027.html">For 2027</a>, the limit is $25,560. Earn above the limit and your payment is cut for a while.</p>
