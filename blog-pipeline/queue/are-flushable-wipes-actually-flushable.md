<h2 id="the-short-answer">The short answer</h2>
<p>No. Not the way you think, and not the way toilet paper does.</p>
<p>A lab at Ryerson University in Toronto tested 101 single use products. Twenty three of them said flushable on the front.</p>
<p><strong><a href="https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/toronto/flushable-wipes-ryerson-report-1.5083455">Not one wipe passed. The only things that fell apart properly were the eleven rolls of ordinary toilet paper they used to check the test.</a></strong></p>
<p>The word has been in a federal court since 2014. Nobody has been paid yet.</p>

<h2 id="after-you-flush">What happens after you flush one?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: it leaves your house whole.</p>

<p>Toilet paper is built to fall apart in seconds. That is the entire job.</p>

<p>A wipe is built to do the opposite. It has to survive being wet in a packet for a year and then take a good scrub without tearing.</p>

<p>So it is made of long fibres held together in a sheet, not short ones pressed loosely.</p>

<p>That sheet goes down. It does not come apart. It catches on the next thing it meets.</p>

<p>The Ryerson team put 101 products through a sewer test. Twenty three of them were sold as flushable.</p>

<p><strong>Not one of the 101 passed.</strong></p>

<p>The eleven rolls of plain toilet paper they ran as a check came apart exactly as they should. That is how you know the test was fair.</p>

<h2 id="who-decided">Who decided they were flushable?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: the people selling them.</p>

<p>Nobody outside the industry set that word. There is no test you have to pass before you print it.</p>

<p>In 2015 the Federal Trade Commission went after a manufacturer called Nice-Pak over exactly this.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2015/05/wet-wipe-manufacturer-agrees-substantiate-flushability-advertising-claims-under-settlement-ftc">The agency said the company had claimed its wipes were safe for sewers, safe for septic tanks, and broke apart shortly after flushing.</a></p>

<p>The reason it gave was the plain one. Because of what they are made of, the wipes did not break down in water in a reasonably short time.</p>

<p>Here is the part that matters in your house.</p>

<p>Nice-Pak did not sell under its own name. It made the store brands. Costco. CVS. Target. BJ's.</p>

<p>So this was never only the expensive ones.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2015/11/ftc-approves-final-order-requiring-wet-wipe-manufacturer-substantiate-flushability-advertising">The final order came later that year.</a> The company has to be able to prove it before it says it.</p>

<p><strong>Read that again. The rule is that you have to prove it before you say it.</strong></p>

<p>That was 2015. Somebody had to be made to agree to that.</p>

<h2 id="the-courts">What did the courts actually say?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: nothing yet, and that is the story.</p>

<p>In 2014 a case was filed against Kimberly-Clark in federal court in New York.</p>

<p>It covers wipes sold as Cottonelle, Scott, Huggies Pull-Ups, Poise and Kotex.</p>

<p>People said the wipes clogged pipes, septic systems and sewer lines. No court has ruled on whether that is true.</p>

<p>A settlement was reached. Up to $20 million.</p>

<p>Now look at the window it covers. <a href="https://www.flushablewipessettlement.com/">February 21, 2008 to May 19, 2022.</a></p>

<p>Fourteen years of that word on the front of the packet.</p>

<p>And it is still not finished. In July 2025 the Court of Appeals set aside the order approving the settlement and sent it back.</p>

<p>The claims period is closed. No date for paying anyone has been set.</p>

<p><strong>Twelve years in court. Nobody paid.</strong></p>

<h2 id="the-law">Is there a law about this?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: yes, and read what it actually does.</p>

<p><a href="https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billTextClient.xhtml?bill_id=202120220AB818">California passed one in October 2021.</a> Illinois, Oregon and Washington got there first.</p>

<p>It makes wipes carry Do Not Flush, in a size you can find, with a symbol. Up to $100,000 a violation.</p>

<p>Sounds like the problem got solved.</p>

<p>It did not. That law is aimed at the wipes that already admit they are not flushable.</p>

<p>The ones still printing flushable on the front are the ones it does not touch.</p>

<p>So the packet that says Do Not Flush is the honest one. The one saying flushable is the one nobody is testing.</p>

<h2 id="have-i-wrecked-my-pipes">Have I been wrecking my own pipes?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: maybe not, and I am not going to pretend I know.</p>

<p>Let me put the other side properly, because it is real.</p>

<p>Nobody has proved it in that court case. There has been no trial and no finding, only an agreement to pay, and even that has not held.</p>

<p>The Ryerson work was a test rig. Your house is not a test rig.</p>

<p>Plenty of homes flush these for years and never call anyone.</p>

<p>Older pipes, tree roots and a long run to the main all matter more than the wipe does.</p>

<p>So no, I cannot tell you the wipes did it.</p>

<p>What I can tell you is what the word was worth.</p>

<p>You paid more for flushable. That was the whole reason the word was on there.</p>

<p>I am not telling you to go back to paper. You bought these for a reason and it was a good one.</p>

<p>Put a small bin with a lid beside the toilet. That is the whole fix.</p>

<p>It costs nothing and it ends the argument tonight.</p>

<p>Then there is the other half, and it is the half I care about.</p>

<p>Somebody printed a word on a packet, charged you extra for it, and twelve years later a court still has not made them answer for it.</p>

<p>You cannot fix that by turning the tub over in the shop.</p>

<p>That is why my family buys through a private membership store instead. We are the manufacturer, so there is no middleman and no markup. One name on it, and a person who picks up the phone.</p>

<h2 id="faq">What else do people ask?</h2>

<p><strong>Are the ones that say Do Not Flush safer?</strong> Not for your skin, no. But that packet is being honest with you, and the one saying flushable is the one nobody has to test. That tells you something.</p>

<p><strong>Is a store brand worse than a name brand?</strong> No. The regulator's 2015 case was against the company making the store brands for Costco, CVS, Target and BJ's. Price was never the pattern.</p>

<p><strong>Do I have a claim in that lawsuit?</strong> No. The claims period is closed and the approval was set aside in July 2025. There is nothing to file.</p>

<p><strong>Will one wipe block my drain?</strong> Almost certainly not. It is the same one every day for years, meeting older pipe and tree roots, that ends in a plumber's bill.</p>
