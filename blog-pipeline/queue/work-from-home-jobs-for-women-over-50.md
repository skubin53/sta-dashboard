<h2 id="the-short-answer">The short answer</h2>
<p>Yes, real ones are out there. <a href="https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2026/22-6-percent-of-workers-teleworked-in-march-2026.htm">In March 2026, 24.9% of working women did paid work from home</a>, the Bureau of Labor Statistics says.</p>
<p>The fake ones want your money before they ever pay you. The FTC said it plainly in February 2026. <strong><a href="https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2026/02/how-avoid-side-hustle-scam">"No legitimate gig will make you pay for expenses, training, or anything else to get the job."</a></strong></p>
<p>The FTC alleged that Arise Virtual Solutions sold work from home as "Better Than a Job." Then it charged people fees to start, the FTC said. Arise agreed to pay $7 million to settle in 2024. It did not admit the charges.</p>
<p>So ask one thing before you say yes. Who pays whom first?</p>

<h2 id="fake-signs">What do the fake work from home jobs have in common?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: the money runs the wrong way. You pay to get the job. The FTC calls that a sure sign of a scam.</p>

<p>The FTC's February 24, 2026 alert lists three signs.</p>

<p>First, big money for little work. Watch for ads that promise "fast cash" for "minimal work."</p>

<p>Second, the rush. "If someone insists a gig will disappear if you don't accept it immediately, walk away." A real employer gives you time to think.</p>

<p>Third, the bill. Someone makes you pay to train, or buy a kit, before you start. The FTC puts it bluntly: "Anyone who does is a scammer."</p>

<p>Now the texts. <a href="https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2026/04/job-offer-text-probably-scam">On April 30, 2026, the FTC warned</a> about fake recruiters. They say they work for a company you know. They offer an "online assessor" job, or just a "remote position."</p>

<p>The new twist is the reply. They ask you to text back "YES" or "INTERESTED." The FTC says do not do it, however polished the message looks.</p>

<p>Then the money talk starts. It might be a check to deposit, and part of it to send back. It might be a task scam. You rate things in an app until you must put in your own money.</p>

<p>"Real employers will never contact you that way," the FTC says.</p>

<p>The FBI counts this too. In <a href="https://www.ic3.gov/AnnualReport/Reports/2025_IC3Report.pdf">its crime report for 2025</a>, people aged 50 to 59 reported losing $100,853,442 to job scams. No age group lost more.</p>

<p>Not every bill comes from a stranger, though. Some come from a company with big brands for clients.</p>

<h2 id="arise">What did the FTC say Arise charged people who wanted to work from home?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: fees to start and fees to stay on, the FTC alleged. In 2021 alone, it says, people paid Arise $5,627,889 in required fees.</p>

<p>You want work you can do from your own desk, on your own hours. That is a fair thing to want. The trouble is what Arise asked for first, by the FTC's account.</p>

<p>Arise Virtual Solutions handles customer service calls for big companies. Its agents work from home. About 90% of them are women, says <a href="https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/arise_complaint.pdf">the FTC's July 2024 complaint</a>. Its ads, the complaint says, told them to "Kiss Your 9-to-5 Goodbye."</p>

<p>Then came the costs. By the complaint's count, each required training course ran about $10 to $250. It puts the background check at $30. And it says a "platform usage fee" of $19.75 came out of the pay twice a month.</p>

<p>The computer, the office gear and the fast internet were yours to buy, the FTC says. Hundreds of dollars before your first call.</p>

<p>Picture what the FTC describes. The computer in its box, the login, the fee twice a month, the calls you waited for, that feeling that the job was working you instead of the other way around.</p>

<p>People who left were "generally not refunded" for the kit, the training or the check, the FTC says.</p>

<p>Arise stopped charging for training in July 2022, after the FTC started asking questions, the complaint says.</p>

<p>Arise agreed to a <a href="https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/68908871/federal-trade-commission-v-arise-virtual-solutions-inc/">$7 million judgment in July 2024</a>. The order says it "neither admits nor denies" the charges. <a href="https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/08/ftc-sends-more-67-million-consumers-impacted-gig-work-companys-deceptive-earnings-claims">In August 2025 the FTC sent refunds</a> to 98,254 people.</p>

<h2 id="every-one">Isn't every work from home job a scam, then?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: no. Plenty are real, and the FTC's own numbers cut against the scary version.</p>

<p>Here is the part I know you are thinking. At my age, am I the easy mark?</p>

<p>The record says otherwise. <a href="https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/P144400-OlderAdultsReportDec2025.pdf">A December 2025 FTC report</a> looked at people 60 and older. They were "much less likely" than younger adults to report losing money to job scams.</p>

<p>Their reported job scam losses still rose nearly 300% in 2024 over 2023, the same report says. Task scams helped drive it. And so age does not make you an easy mark. It makes one wrong yes cost more.</p>

<p>Arise has a side too. Its statement, reported by the <a href="https://www.news4jax.com/business/2024/07/02/ftc-says-gig-company-arise-misled-consumers-about-how-much-money-they-could-make-on-its-platform/">Associated Press</a>, says it "vehemently" disagrees with the FTC's allegations. It called the deal "not an admission or finding of liability or wrongdoing."</p>

<h2 id="before-you-say-yes">What should you ask before you say yes to any job from home?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: three questions, all built on FTC advice. The last one costs one cup of coffee with a friend.</p>

<h3>Who pays whom first?</h3>

<p>If you must pay to get the job, stop there. The FTC's line: "Never pay to get paid or get a job."</p>

<h3>Can I talk to someone who did it last year?</h3>

<p>The FTC says to ask current and past workers about their "experiences, income, and any out-of-pocket expenses." The ones who left will tell you what the ad left out.</p>

<h3>What does someone I trust think?</h3>

<p>Read the offer out loud to a friend before you answer it. That is the FTC's advice too: talk to someone you trust.</p>

<h2 id="faq">What else do you want to know?</h2>

<p><strong>Is Arise a scam?</strong></p>
<p>The FTC's complaint does not use that word. It alleged misleading pay claims and fees to start. Arise settled for $7 million without admitting the charges. The order bars it from pay claims it cannot back up.</p>

<p><strong>What if I already replied YES to one of those texts?</strong></p>
<p>Stop answering them. Do not send money, your bank account number or your Social Security number. Report it to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.</p>

<p><strong>What paperwork should I get before I pay to start a business from home?</strong></p>
<p>Say you must pay to start, and the seller says it will find you customers. Then the FTC's Business Opportunity Rule applies. You get key facts in writing, at least seven calendar days before you sign or pay. The FTC alleged Arise failed for years to hand them over.</p>
