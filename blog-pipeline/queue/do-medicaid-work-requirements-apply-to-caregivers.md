<h2 id="the-short-answer">The short answer</h2>
<p>It can apply to you. And caring for your mother can take you back out of it.</p>
<p>Here is the short version. The 2026 federal Medicaid rule can exclude family caregivers of a disabled person. It says that can mean <strong><a href="https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/06/03/2026-11094/medicaid-program-community-engagement-requirement-for-certain-individuals">"an older adult who requires assistance"</a></strong> with bathing, dressing and eating.</p>
<p>The rule even says family caregivers "may not realize that they qualify." Its word is exclusion, not exemption. It is the same door.</p>
<p>Your state decides if your mother fits. The danger is its 30 day letter. Call your state Medicaid office and confirm it first.</p>

<h2 id="does-it-apply">Do the Medicaid work rules apply if you take care of your mother?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: they can. Then the rule carves out family caregivers, and that can be you.</p>

<p>You left your job to care for your mother. Now a letter about Medicaid and 80 hours a month sits on her kitchen counter.</p>

<p>You stand at her stove at six, stirring her soup, and you count it up. The baths, the meals, the drives, the nights in the chair by her bed. Does any of it count?</p>

<p>The federal Medicaid agency, CMS, put out the rule on June 1, 2026. It covers adults 19 to 64 in Medicaid's low-income adult group. <a href="https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-06-03/pdf/2026-11094.pdf">Page 33351 asks for</a> work, school or service "for at least 80 hours a month."</p>

<p>Your care can be the reason the rule skips you.</p>

<h2 id="two-tests">What are the two tests for the caregiver exemption?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: live with her or be her relative, and help her on a regular basis. If neither, it takes 80 hours a month.</p>

<p>You pass if you live with her or you are her relative. Your help has to come "on a regular basis." And it cannot be "solely incidental in nature."</p>

<p>A daughter is named. <a href="https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-42/section-435.554">The rule's list of relatives</a> includes the disabled person's "son, daughter."</p>

<p>If you live with her or are related, CMS says you do not have to show a set number of hours.</p>

<p>Pay does not change it. The tests apply whether you are "a paid or an unpaid family caregiver."</p>

<p>One more path covers a friend or neighbor you are not related to and do not live with. That takes at least 80 hours a month.</p>

<p>Which row is you? Next, the question is about her.</p>

<h2 id="does-she-count">Does your mother count as disabled under the rule?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: she can, even if she has never been on disability. Needing help to bathe or dress can count.</p>

<p>The rule borrows from the Americans with Disabilities Act. <a href="https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-28/section-35.108">That means</a> a problem in her body or mind that substantially limits a basic part of life. It does not have to be severe. Caring for herself, walking, seeing and eating are all on its list.</p>

<p>She does not need to be on disability through any program. CMS says so on page 33369.</p>

<p>And age is no bar.</p>

<p>Your state must check that she fits. But CMS says the state may not make you give her name. Her privacy stays hers.</p>

<p>That leaves one place you can still get caught.</p>

<h2 id="thirty-days">Where does the paperwork catch you?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: at the 30 day letter. If your state cannot confirm you from its records, it asks you, and a clock starts.</p>

<p>If your state cannot confirm you, it sends a notice. <a href="https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-06-03/pdf/2026-11094.pdf">The rule gives you</a> "30 calendar days from the date the notice is received." In that time you show you met the rule, or that it does not apply.</p>

<p>Your coverage stays on for those 30 days. If they pass with no answer, the state must first check if you qualify another way. If not, it sends written notice and ends it by the end of the next month.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-06-03/pdf/2026-11094.pdf">CMS expects this itself, on page 33460.</a> It says some people who may meet the rule will still lose coverage over paperwork. Its examples: not answering a request, or not sending enough proof.</p>

<p>From January 1, 2027, another part of the law makes most people this rule covers renew every six months.</p>

<p>From January 1, 2028, if its records fall short, your state must ask for papers you can reasonably get.</p>

<h2 id="find-you">Isn't the state supposed to find you on its own?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: it is supposed to try first. It still turns to you when its records are not enough.</p>

<p>I know what you are thinking. Medicaid already knows who lives in your house. Shouldn't that settle it?</p>

<p>Partly, yes. The rule tells states to use the household facts they already have. It tells them to ask for what they need in one request. That is real, and I will not pretend it is nothing.</p>

<p>But there is a catch. CMS wrote: "Many family caregivers do not automatically identify themselves as caregivers." And each state decides what proof is enough.</p>

<h2 id="gather">What should you gather before your state's letter comes?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: a plain weekly record of what you do for her, and your renewal month on the calendar.</p>

<h3>Write down what you do for her</h3>

<p>Keep a notebook on her counter. Each week, write the dates and what you did. The baths, the meals, the rides, the night you sat up with her. When the state asks, your answer is already written.</p>

<h3>Make sure your file shows where you live</h3>

<p>If you live with her, check that your Medicaid file says so. The rule tells states to use that record first.</p>

<h3>Keep the hours anyway</h3>

<p>Say your state finds you do not fit. Your care can still count. CMS says a family caregiver's help "can also qualify as unpaid work." And <a href="https://theshannonnicole.com/post/does-self-employment-count-for-medicaid-work-requirements">a business you own counts as work</a> under the same rule.</p>

<h3>Call your state Medicaid office</h3>

<p><a href="https://www.healthcare.gov/medicaid-chip/getting-medicaid-chip/">HealthCare.gov lists every state's Medicaid office.</a> Ask how they confirm a family caregiver, and when your next renewal is. Their answer is the one that counts.</p>

<p>You are far from alone. The rule cites Pew Research Center: 10 percent of all U.S. adults say they care for a parent 65 or older.</p>

<h2 id="faq">What else are women asking me about this?</h2>

<p><strong>Is there a caregiver exemption from Medicaid work requirements?</strong></p>
<p>Yes. The rule calls it an exclusion. It covers you if you care for a disabled person and pass one of its tests. Your state decides if you fit. Call its Medicaid office to confirm.</p>

<p><strong>Do I have to be paid to count as a family caregiver?</strong></p>
<p>No. CMS says the tests apply whether you are paid or unpaid. A paycheck for your care does not change them.</p>

<p><strong>What if I do not qualify for the exclusion?</strong></p>
<p>Your care can still count. CMS says that help can count as unpaid work. Write your hours down every week.</p>

<p><strong>When will my state check on me?</strong></p>
<p>States must start by January 1, 2027, and some may start sooner. A state with a delay must start by December 31, 2028. If you are on Medicaid now, your first check can come at your first renewal after your state starts.</p>
