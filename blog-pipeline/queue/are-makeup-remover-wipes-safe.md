<h2 id="what-got-recalled">What exactly got recalled?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: one lot of the soft blue makeup wipes. About 1,312 cases of them.</p>

<p>You know the pouch. Soft blue. A little flap on top.</p>

<p>It sits on the nightstand. You reach for it half asleep.</p>

<p>In September 2025 the maker put out a recall.</p>

<p>Kenvue, the company behind Neutrogena, pulled its Makeup Remover Ultra-Soft Cleansing Towelettes. The 50-count packs, sold 25 to a case.</p>

<p>One lot. The code is 1835U6325A. About 1,312 cases.</p>

<p>Here is where they went. Texas, South Carolina, Georgia and Florida.</p>

<p>The reason, in the recall's own words, is that the wipes tested positive for a bacteria.</p>

<h2 id="what-was-in-it">What was growing in the wipe?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: a germ that lives in water and shrugs off the stuff meant to stop it.</p>

<p>The germ has a long name. Pluralibacter gergoviae. Say it how you like.</p>

<p>It lives in water and soil. And it keeps turning up in beauty products.</p>

<p>Here is the part that matters. <a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10675545/">Researchers found this germ can live through the preservatives meant to keep wipes clean.</a></p>

<p>So the very thing meant to keep a wipe safe does not always stop it.</p>

<p>What can it do? The research lists lung, bladder and eye infections. In the worst cases, a blood infection. Mostly in people already worn down.</p>

<p>Now think about a makeup wipe. You drag it across your eyes. Your lips. A spot that has broken open.</p>

<h2 id="why-not-stopped">Why did the preservative not stop it?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: because this germ learned to live through the preservative.</p>

<p>Nobody explains this bit, so here it is.</p>

<p>A wipe is mostly water. Water grows things. So makers add preservatives to hold the germs back.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/potential-contaminants-cosmetics/microbiological-safety-and-cosmetics">The FDA says it plainly: cosmetics do not have to be germ-free. Staying safe rests on the preservative doing its job.</a></p>

<p>But this germ is known to shrug off parabens and other common preservatives.</p>

<p>So the pack was preserved the normal way. The germ did not care.</p>

<h2 id="the-other-side">The case against my own story</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: three fair points cut against me, and they are all true.</p>

<p>Here are the strongest points on the other side.</p>

<p><strong>One.</strong> The FDA called this a Class II recall. That means any harm is expected to be temporary or reversible, not the worst kind.</p>

<p><strong>Two.</strong> No illnesses were reported. Neutrogena says it has had none.</p>

<p><strong>Three.</strong> It was one lot, and they found it themselves and pulled it. That is a company doing the right thing. I will not pretend it is not.</p>

<p>So why does it still sit wrong with me?</p>

<p>Because of what a wipe is. You reach for it tired, at night, and you trust it to clean your face.</p>

<p>For that batch, the wipe was the dirtiest thing you touched all day.</p>

<p>And nothing on the front told you.</p>

<h3>The receipts</h3>
<ul>
<li><a href="https://www.fda.gov/safety/recalls-market-withdrawals-safety-alerts">US Food and Drug Administration, cosmetics recalls and alerts</a>. Kenvue Brands, LLC issued a voluntary recall on 19 September 2025 of Neutrogena Makeup Remover Ultra-Soft Cleansing Towelettes, 50-count, lot number 1835U6325A, after its own testing found the wipes positive for the bacteria Pluralibacter gergoviae. The recall covered about 1,312 cases distributed in Texas, South Carolina, Georgia and Florida. The FDA classified it as a Class II recall on 3 October 2025, meaning any adverse health effects are expected to be temporary or reversible. The company reported no illnesses.</li>
<li><a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10675545/">"Genomic Insights into Pluralibacter gergoviae," National Library of Medicine (PMC)</a>. Pluralibacter gergoviae is an opportunistic pathogen repeatedly isolated from personal care products, with a known resistance to parabens and other preservatives and the potential to cause infection in vulnerable people.</li>
<li><a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6986549/">"Understanding the challenges of non-food industrial product contamination," PMC (National Library of Medicine)</a>. Peer-reviewed work on how Gram-negative bacteria such as Pluralibacter gergoviae survive and adapt to preservative systems in cosmetics and personal care products, linking preservative resistance to changes in the bacterial cell membrane.</li>
<li><a href="https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/potential-contaminants-cosmetics/microbiological-safety-and-cosmetics">US Food and Drug Administration, Microbiological Safety and Cosmetics</a>. Cosmetics are not required to be sterile, and their microbial safety relies on the preservative system. Water-based products such as wipes are the most vulnerable to microbial growth.</li>

<h2 id="faq">What else do people ask?</h2>

<p><strong>Are Neutrogena makeup wipes safe now?</strong> The recall covered one lot, 1835U6325A, made and sold in 2025. That lot is gone. This is a fact about how one batch went wrong, not a warning about a pack bought last week.</p>

<p><strong>How would I have known?</strong> By the lot code on the pack. Nothing on the front would tell you a thing.</p>

<p><strong>Do preservatives keep wipes safe?</strong> Usually. But this germ is known to resist them, which is how the batch got through in the first place.</p>

<p><strong>Did anybody get sick?</strong> The company says it received no reports of illness. I will not claim more than that.</p>
