<h2 id="what-the-lawsuit-says">What does the 2026 LiLash lawsuit say?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: that LiLash was sold as a beauty serum "with no warning of serious side effects or risks."</p>

<p>You want your lashes back. Nothing wrong with wanting that. The suit says the problem is what Cosmetic Alchemy put in the tube to give them to you.</p>

<p><a href="https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.cacd.1036485/gov.uscourts.cacd.1036485.1.0.pdf">On August 31, 2026, Sandra Meyers sued Cosmetic Alchemy, the company behind LiLash, in federal court in California.</a> She says she bought LiLash more than once from August 2022 to May 2024, and paid over $100.</p>

<p>Her complaint says thousands of buyers paid about $90 to $150 for LiLash and its brow serum, LiBrow. It says both were sold as beauty products, not drugs, with no warning.</p>

<p>It also says her last use caused "discomfort, eye inflammation, and swelling." That is her claim.</p>

<p>It all turns on one long word near the end of LiLash's ingredient list.</p>

<h2 id="is-it-prostaglandin-free">Is LiLash prostaglandin free?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: no. LiLash's own page calls its key ingredient "a prostaglandin analog," a cousin of a prescription lash drug.</p>

<h3>Dehydrolatanoprost, second from last on the list</h3>

<p>The word is dehydrolatanoprost. <a href="https://web.archive.org/web/20261012202159/https://www.lilash.com/products/lash-serum?">LiLash's product page calls it "a prostaglandin analog" made from "naturally occurring fatty acids."</a> It says this is the part of LiLash that does the work.</p>

<p>The complaint calls it "a synthetic substance." It says the company put it "second-to-last" on the list, with nothing to say what it is.</p>

<p>Then the page changed. <a href="https://web.archive.org/web/20260220064048/https://www.lilash.com/products/lash-serum">A saved copy from February 2026 answers "Is LiLash safe?" with "Yes!"</a> By October, that answer was gone, and the line about "a prostaglandin analog" was there.</p>

<p>The suit says changes like that may have come after Sandra Meyers wrote to the company, before suing. <a href="https://web.archive.org/web/20261010042240/https://www.lilash.com/pages/faq">The FAQ page kept its answer. It still says "Is LiLash Safe? Yes!"</a></p>

<p>That "Yes!" is the answer you got. A drug in the same family has to print a very different one.</p>

<h2 id="dry-itchy-eyes">My eyes are dry and itchy. Is it the serum?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: nobody can promise you that from a web page, but its prescription cousin lists dry, itchy eyes on its label.</p>

<p>That cousin is Latisse. The suit says its drug, bimatoprost, sits in the same family as the one in LiLash.</p>

<p><a href="https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=34f83d9d-2c64-463e-8a90-9a460fedfead">The Latisse label lists itchy eyes, red eyes, darker skin, dry eye and redness around the eye.</a> In a four month study of 278 adults, those hit fewer than 4%. It also warns the colored part of your eye can darken, and says that change "is likely to be permanent."</p>

<p>That drug comes with its warning in writing. The complaint says LiLash did not.</p>

<p>The suit's list for LiLash runs longer. It names dry eye that will not quit, swelling, redness and color change. Those are its claims.</p>

<p><a href="https://health.ec.europa.eu/publications/prostaglandins-and-prostaglandin-analogues-used-cosmetic-products_en">Europe's consumer safety panel looked at this family in 2022 and named the one in LiLash.</a> It noted "concerns over the safety" of these in beauty products. They work in tiny amounts, and they go right by the eye.</p>

<h2 id="the-other-side">My friend loves her LiLash lashes. Is she wrong?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: her lashes are real, and the suit is about what buyers were told.</p>

<p>Here is the part I know you are thinking. Your friend has used it for years and her eyes look fine. That counts, and I am not going to argue with her mirror.</p>

<p>There is more on her side. The suit asks for money, not for injuries. The Latisse label puts its common side effects under 4%. <a href="https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/74726580/sandra-meyers-v-cosmetic-alchemy-llc/">And as of the court file's latest entry, on October 1, 2026, no judge has ruled.</a></p>

<p>LiLash's FAQ does warn about "dryness or redness" at first. It calls that a "phase in" period.</p>

<p>So why would I still tell you to put the tube away? Because you asked if it was safe, and the answer was "Yes!" The suit says that "Yes!" left out what a drug in the same family has to print.</p>

<h2 id="what-instead">What can I use on my lashes instead?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: a clean spoolie and a drop of plain oil at night, with nothing from the drug family in it.</p>

<p>First, the honest limit. A plain oil will not grow lashes the way a drug does. I am not going to pretend it will.</p>

<p><a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pharma.2026.06.007">French researchers looked at 79 lash serums in 2026.</a> About 30% held a prostaglandin analog. The rest leaned on plain things like castor oil, and so the swap is plain.</p>

<p>Wash your face at night, then brush a drop of castor oil through your top lashes with a clean spoolie. Wash the spoolie once a week.</p>
