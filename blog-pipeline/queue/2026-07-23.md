<h2>What makes waterproof mascara stay put all day?</h2>
<p><strong>Quick answer:</strong> the same thing you would never choose to press against your eye on purpose.</p>
<p>Regular mascara gives up. It flakes by lunch, it runs when you cry at the school play, and honestly that is the mascara behaving. Waterproof and smudge-proof formulas do not give up because many of them are built on PFAS, the family of forever chemicals engineered to repel water and oil. That is the whole trick. The film that keeps your lashes perfect at hour twelve is the same water-hating chemistry that does not break down in your body and does not break down in the world. Brushed on at the lash line, it sits at the very edge of the thin, wet membrane of your eye. The receipt for all of this is further down, and it is not from a blog. It is from a university lab.</p>

[IMAGE: Close but not macro shot of a single waterproof mascara tube and wand resting on a cold countertop, neutral clinical light, a faint reflection. No readable label text, pulled back so any writing is illegible. Cool, unsettled tone.]

<h2>But it is just a little mascara, right?</h2>
<p><strong>Quick answer:</strong> you do not have to fix your whole bathroom today. Start with the one thing you put closest to your eyes, every morning, without fail.</p>
<p>This is the objection I hear from every smart woman who looks at her counter and feels the overwhelm rise. If one tube is a problem, then what about the foundation, the whole drawer? Here is the mercy in it. You do not owe the drawer anything today. Mascara earns the first look because of where it goes and how often you use it. It is the product you apply nearest the tear duct, day after day, year after year. So you make one swap. One. The overwhelm shrinks the moment the job gets small enough to actually do.</p>

<p>You cannot read your way to safety here, because the study found PFAS mostly does not appear on the label at all. It is not printed anywhere for you to catch, so stop trying to win at reading. The real move is to stop handing your trust to the companies that hide this in the first place. Switch to a source you already trust to be clean. That can be a small clean-beauty maker whose entire reason for existing is leaving the forever chemicals out, or better, a store that has already done the vetting for you, so you never have to play label detective again. You are not looking for a better label. You are looking for people you do not have to double-check.</p>

<section class="proof">
  <h2>The receipt: what the lab actually found</h2>
  <p>In 2021, researchers at the University of Notre Dame screened 231 popular makeup products bought across the United States and Canada, measuring total fluorine as a marker for PFAS. The work was led by principal investigator Graham Peaslee, a physics professor, with lead author Heather D. Whitehead, and it was <a href="https://news.nd.edu/news/use-of-pfas-in-cosmetics-widespread-new-study-finds/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">published on June 15, 2021 in the peer-reviewed journal Environmental Science and Technology Letters</a>, titled Fluorinated Compounds in North American Cosmetics.</p>
  <p>Waterproof mascara was among the worst categories in the study. Roughly <a href="https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/hold-blush-cosmetics-may-contain-toxic-forever-chemicals-180978036/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">82 percent of the waterproof mascaras tested showed high fluorine levels</a>, the marker the researchers used for the probable presence of PFAS. About half of all 231 products tested came back high, with foundations and long-wear lipsticks also landing near the top.</p>
  <p>The label problem is the part that stings. The researchers pulled 29 of the high-fluorine products for deeper lab analysis and found each one contained between 4 and 13 individual PFAS compounds. Only one of those 29 products listed any PFAS as an ingredient. Across all the products tested, the label failed to disclose PFAS to the shopper in 88 percent of cases. This is exactly why squinting at the fine print does not save you here.</p>
  <p>Why the eyes specifically. Eye makeup goes on around the eyes and near the tear duct, a thin mucous membrane where compounds can be absorbed, along with the chance of inhaling or swallowing tiny amounts. As Peaslee has noted, PFAS is persistent. Once it enters the bloodstream it stays and builds up over time. Broader research links PFAS exposure to health harms including certain cancers such as kidney and testicular, thyroid and immune effects, high cholesterol, liver damage, and lower birth weight in newborns. The Green Science Policy Institute, a collaborator on the study, <a href="https://greensciencepolicy.org/pfas-in-cosmetics/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">reported the same core finding</a>: probable PFAS in just over half the products, most common in waterproof mascara, and rarely disclosed on the label.</p>
  <p class="citation"><em>Primary citation: Whitehead et al., Environ. Sci. Technol. Lett. 2021, <a href="https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acs.estlett.1c00240" target="_blank" rel="noopener">DOI 10.1021/acs.estlett.1c00240</a>.</em></p>
</section>

<h2>Frequently asked questions</h2>

<h3>Is all mascara a problem, or only the waterproof kind?</h3>
<p>The lab found waterproof mascara was one of the worst categories, with roughly 82 percent testing high for the PFAS marker. Plain, washable mascaras were far less likely to come back high. Singling out the waterproof tube is not overreacting. It is aiming at the real culprit.</p>

<h3>If PFAS is not on the label, how am I supposed to avoid it?</h3>
<p>You are not the crazy one for feeling the label let you down. In the study, 88 percent of products did not disclose PFAS to the shopper. You do not beat this by reading, because it is not printed there to find. You beat it by refusing to buy from the companies that play this game at all, and letting a source you already trust do the vetting for you.</p>

<h3>I have used waterproof mascara for years. Did I already ruin something?</h3>
<p>Try to let that fear go, because it does not help you and this is not a diagnosis. The only moment you actually control is the next tube you buy. Swapping it now makes you the woman who caught it, and catching it is the win.</p>

<h3>What do I actually buy instead?</h3>
<p>A plain, washable mascara from a source you already trust to leave the forever chemicals out, whether that is a small clean-beauty maker whose whole line refuses them or a store that has already vetted the shelf for you. You are not hunting for the right label. You are handing the checking to someone who has earned it. That one quiet switch is who you are becoming: the person in the house who sees it first.</p>
