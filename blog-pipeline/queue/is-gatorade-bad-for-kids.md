<h2 id="whats-in-the-bottle">What is actually in that orange bottle?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: mostly water and sugar, plus a lab-made dye. The big bottle holds 48 grams of added sugar.</p>

<p>You stood at the cooler longer than you meant to, one orange bottle sweating in your hand.</p>

<p>You want the kids to drink something after they run. That is fair. The problem is what PepsiCo puts in the bottle.</p>

<h3 id="sugar">Sugar, 48 grams in the big bottle</h3>

<p><a href="https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.nysd.664535/gov.uscourts.nysd.664535.36.0.pdf">A lawsuit against PepsiCo counts 48 grams of added sugar in a 28-ounce bottle.</a></p>

<p><a href="https://www.oag.ca.gov/news/press-releases/attorney-general-becerra-announces-settlement-gatorade-over-allegedly-misleading">The American Heart Association says a child should get no more than 25 grams a day.</a> And so one bottle is nearly two days' worth for a child.</p>

<h3 id="dyes">Red 40, Yellow 5, Yellow 6 and Blue 1, the colors</h3>

<p>The bright color is not fruit. <a href="https://www.yahoo.com/news/articles/bye-bye-red-40-gatorade-225742340.html">A March 2026 news story says the classic fruit punch uses Red 40.</a> Cool Blue uses Blue 1. Lemon lime uses Yellow 5. Orange uses Yellow 6.</p>

<p><a href="https://oehha.ca.gov/sites/default/files/media/downloads/risk-assessment/press-release/fooddyepressrelease041621.pdf">In 2021, a California state office studied these four dyes and three more. It found they "can result in hyperactivity" in some children.</a> That finding was about the dyes in general.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/150-million-americans-feel-dehydrated-gatorade-aims-to-change-how-people-think-about-hydration-302743912.html">In April 2026, PepsiCo said three flavors would drop the dyes "later this fall."</a> Cool Blue was not one of them.</p>

<p>Then there is what the bottle says about water.</p>

<h2 id="better-than-water">Does Gatorade really hydrate better than water?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: not on a normal day, going by Gatorade's own site. It says, "The goal isn't to replace water." A 2026 lawsuit says the label is false.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/150-million-americans-feel-dehydrated-gatorade-aims-to-change-how-people-think-about-hydration-302743912.html">In April 2026, PepsiCo gave Gatorade a new look.</a> Its release says the classic bottle "Hydrates Better than Water." It pitches it to "accompany an afternoon walk."</p>

<p>That is your walk, not a two-hour practice in July.</p>

<p>Gatorade's own site tells a smaller story. <a href="https://www.gatorade.com/resources/how-gatorade-works-and-why-it-can-hydrate-better-than-water">In February 2026 it put the benefit "during exercise," especially past an hour.</a> It also said, "Water replaces fluid."</p>

<p><a href="https://www.courthousenews.com/gatorades-thirst-quenching-claims-challenged-in-class-action/">In May 2026, shoppers sued PepsiCo in federal court in New York.</a> Their latest complaint alleges the label is false unless you are sweating hard.</p>

<p>For "the sideline bench, the kitchen counter," they say, it "is simply untrue." That is their claim, not a ruling.</p>

<p>It is not the first time a state has gone after Gatorade over water.</p>

<h2 id="in-trouble-before">Has Gatorade been in trouble over water before?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: yes. In 2017 Gatorade paid California $300,000 over a free phone game.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.oag.ca.gov/news/press-releases/attorney-general-becerra-announces-settlement-gatorade-over-allegedly-misleading">In September 2017, California sued The Gatorade Company and settled the same day.</a> The game was called "Bolt!"</p>

<p>By the state's account, a cartoon runner sped up when he touched a Gatorade. He slowed down when he touched a drop of water.</p>

<p>The state says the tutorial told players, "Keep Your Performance Level High By Avoiding Water." By its count, the game was downloaded more than 2.3 million times.</p>

<p>Gatorade paid $300,000. The deal bars it from showing water in a bad light in any ad. It also bars ads where over 35 percent of the audience is under 12.</p>

<p>Nine years later, the bottle says it beats water.</p>

<h2 id="the-other-side">Isn't Gatorade made for kids who play hard?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: for a long, sweaty practice, it has a job. Most days, the children's doctors point to water.</p>

<p>Here is the part I know you are thinking. Gatorade was made for athletes, and the kids are athletes.</p>

<p>That is fair. In a long, hard practice, a body loses salt and energy, not just water. Gatorade's own site puts that line past an hour.</p>

<p>And no one has proven one bottle after a game hurt a healthy kid. I am not going to pretend they have.</p>

<p>So why does it still sit wrong with me? Because the bottle is not just sold for the tournament anymore. It is sold for your afternoon walk.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.oag.ca.gov/news/press-releases/attorney-general-becerra-announces-settlement-gatorade-over-allegedly-misleading">The American Academy of Pediatrics says children "rarely need sports drinks."</a> It says "water, not sports drinks" should come first.</p>

<p>You are not the only one bothered. Eight shoppers have put their names on the joint complaint.</p>

<h2 id="what-instead">What do you pour instead?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: cold tap water in a steel bottle, and cut fruit from a farm you know.</p>

<p>Stop buying the case for the sideline. Fill a steel bottle at the tap the night before.</p>

<p>For the cooler, find a farm near you that grows fruit. Get to know the farmer. Ask what is ripe this week. A grape does not need Red 40 to look like a grape.</p>

<p>Water alone will not put back the salt from a two-hour tournament in July. That is a real limit. For a normal Saturday game, water is what the children's doctors point to.</p>

<h2 id="faq">What else do people ask?</h2>

<p><strong>Is one bottle after a game really that bad?</strong></p>
<p>No one has proven one bottle hurts a healthy kid. The trouble is the habit. The lawsuit counts 48 grams of added sugar in the big bottle. A child's daily limit is 25 grams.</p>

<p><strong>Is Gatorade Zero or Lower Sugar any better?</strong></p>
<p>Less sugar, yes. Both still carry the same "hydrates better than water" claim the lawsuit calls false. The shoppers also allege the Lower Sugar "no artificial" claim is false. They say its citric acid is not from fruit. It is made in a factory from black mold.</p>

<p><strong>Doesn't a kid need the salt and sugar after practice?</strong></p>
<p>After a long, hard, sweaty practice, it can help. Gatorade's own site puts that line past 60 minutes. For a normal game, water is what the children's doctors point to.</p>
