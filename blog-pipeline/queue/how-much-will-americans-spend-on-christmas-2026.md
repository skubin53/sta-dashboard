<h2 id="how-much">How much will the average American spend on Christmas this year?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: about $1,011 on gifts, and $1,654 for the whole house.</p>

<h3>The gifts</h3>

<p><a href="https://news.gallup.com/poll/716462/holiday-shopping-plans-point-average-retail-year.aspx">Gallup asked Americans in October.</a> The average answer was $1,011 for gifts. That is about the same as the last two years.</p>

<p>About a third plan to spend $1,000 or more. More than one in four plan to spend under $500.</p>

<p>And 24% plan to spend less than last year. Only 18% plan to spend more.</p>

<h3>The whole house</h3>

<p><a href="https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/about/press-room/deloitte-holiday-spending-set-to-increase.html">Deloitte asked 4,000 people</a> in late summer. It puts the average home at $1,654, up 4%. Of that, $988 goes on things and $666 on outings.</p>

<p>Gallup has asked about Christmas spending every fall since 2006. This year's answer barely moved. Your list is not out of line.</p>

<h3>The whole country</h3>

<h2 id="who-decides">Who decides what your December costs?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: you pick the names. The stores pick the prices, and the rules.</p>

<p>You decide who is on the list. That part is yours, and it always was.</p>

<p>The price tag is not. The same NRF release names <a href="https://nrf.com/media-center/press-releases/nrf-forecasts-2026-holiday-sales-growth-as-consumers-remain-resilient">inflation and rising gas prices</a> this year. Those are set far from your kitchen. Nobody asked you to vote on that.</p>

<p><a href="https://nrf.com/media-center/press-releases/affordability-shapes-winter-holiday-shopping-according-to-nrf-holiday-survey">Its October survey</a> found 56% of shoppers say the economy will change what they spend.</p>

<p>The same survey says 64% are likely to wait until Thanksgiving weekend to do most of their shopping. The store picks the week of the sale. You just show up for it.</p>

<p>And so you shop the sales. You wait for the deal. You do everything right on your side of the counter.</p>

<p>The other side of the counter writes its own rules. Here is what that looked like at Amazon.</p>

<h2 id="prime">What did the FTC say Amazon did with Prime?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: the FTC alleged Amazon signed people up without their consent and made leaving hard. Amazon denies it, and agreed to pay $2.5 billion.</p>

<h3>The sign up</h3>

<p>Prime costs <a href="https://www.npr.org/2025/09/25/nx-s1-5551026/amazon-prime-lawsuit-ftc-settlement">$14.99 a month, or $139 for the whole year</a>. It renews by itself.</p>

<p>The <a href="https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/09/ftc-secures-historic-25-billion-settlement-against-amazon">FTC alleged</a> Amazon built confusing screens that signed people up for Prime without their knowledge.</p>

<h3>The way out</h3>

<p>The FTC alleged leaving was hard on purpose. By the FTC's account, the cancel steps had a name inside Amazon. <a href="https://www.pbs.org/newshour/economy/amazon-to-pay-2-5-billion-to-settle-ftc-allegations-it-duped-customers-into-enrolling-in-prime">AP reported it was called "Iliad,"</a> after the old poem about a long siege. By that account, you had to say yes to leaving on three pages.</p>

<h3>The settlement</h3>

<p>The FTC first sued in 2023. In September 2025, days into the trial in Seattle, Amazon settled. The FTC says it is a $1 billion fine. Another $1.5 billion goes back to about 35 million people.</p>

<p>Amazon admitted no wrongdoing. It said it has "always followed the law."</p>

<h3>The refunds</h3>

<p>In September 2026, <a href="https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/09/ftc-announces-additional-payments-consumers-stemming-ftcs-amazon-prime-settlement">the FTC said</a> Amazon had paid more than $845 million so far. The top payment went up from $51 to $200. New rounds go out on their own, with no form to fill in. If payments fall short of the target by February 2027, a final round starts by April 2027.</p>

<p>So look at who wrote those screens. Not you. You clicked the button they put in front of you.</p>

<h2 id="this-week">What would I do first this week?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: write your number down, check what renews on its own, then start one thing of your own.</p>

<h3>Write your number down</h3>

<p>Put the list on paper, with a price next to each name. Add it up once, in daylight. A number on paper is easier than a number in your stomach at midnight.</p>

<h3>Look at what renews on its own</h3>

<p>Open your bank app and find every charge that comes back each month. Ask one question of each. Did you choose it this year, or did it choose you?</p>

<p>If someone calls about a Prime refund and asks you for money, hang up. <a href="https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/09/ftc-announces-additional-payments-consumers-stemming-ftcs-amazon-prime-settlement">The FTC says</a> no one from Amazon will ever ask you for money to get one.</p>
