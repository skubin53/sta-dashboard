<h2 id="what-new-york-found">What did New York's list show for The Pink Stuff spray?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: the company's own number, 4.5 parts per million, against a limit of 1.</p>

<p>You bought it because it worked. Maybe you saw it in a video first. The scorch came off. The sink came up white. You felt on top of things for a minute.</p>

<p>Nobody told you it came with a number.</p>

<p>New York set a limit on one chemical in home cleaners. It is called 1,4-dioxane.</p>

<p><a href="https://dec.ny.gov/environmental-protection/pollution-prevention/household-personal-cosmetic-dioxane-limits">The limit for home cleaners dropped to 1 part per million on December 31, 2023.</a> If a company could not meet it, it could ask the state for a pass. The state calls it a waiver.</p>

<p>To get one, the company had to show it was trying to bring the level down. That was the deal.</p>

<p>The Pink Stuff is sold by Star Brands. <a href="https://www.dec.ny.gov/fs/projects/waivers/1-4DApprovedWaivers.xlsx">Look at the state's list for 2024 to 2025. Its Miracle Wash-Up Spray sat at 4.5 parts per million.</a> That is four and a half times the limit.</p>

<p>The year before, the same spray was on the list. Same number. 4.5.</p>

<p>And so a whole year went by, and the level did not move at all.</p>

<p>How many products still had a pass by the end? In the year through December 2024, the list held 1,083 entries. In the last year, it held 14. The Pink Stuff spray was one of them.</p>

<h2 id="why-not-on-the-bottle">Why is 1,4-dioxane never on the bottle?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: no one adds it on purpose, so no one has to list it.</p>

<h3>1,4-dioxane</h3>

<p><a href="https://dec.ny.gov/environmental-protection/pollution-prevention/household-personal-cosmetic-dioxane-limits">New York says it is left out of the list on the bottle because it is a byproduct.</a> It forms while some soaps and foaming agents are made.</p>

<p>So it can be in the bottle and nowhere on it.</p>

<p><a href="https://cfpub.epa.gov/ncea/iris2/chemicalLanding.cfm?substance_nmbr=326">The US EPA calls it "likely to be carcinogenic to humans."</a> That means likely to cause cancer.</p>

<p><a href="https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP/ToxFAQs/ToxFAQsDetails.aspx?faqid=954&amp;toxid=199">The CDC says your skin can touch it when you use a detergent that holds it.</a> A dish spray is a detergent. You use it at the sink, and your hands are in the water.</p>

<p>You did not miss anything. The company had its number. It gave that number to the state. It never had to give it to you.</p>

<h2 id="the-other-side">Is there a case for The Pink Stuff?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: three fair points, and each one is true.</p>

<p><strong>One.</strong> The New York list named the wash-up spray. It did not name the famous paste in the tub.</p>

<p><strong>Two.</strong> <a href="https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP/ToxFAQs/ToxFAQsDetails.aspx?faqid=954&amp;toxid=199">The CDC says the few studies in people do not show whether it causes cancer in humans.</a> The cancer was found in lab rats.</p>

<p><strong>Three.</strong> New York made companies count. A brand that never sold there was never counted. So a name missing from the list proves nothing.</p>

<p>So why does it still sit wrong with me?</p>

<p>Because the company had two years and one number. It asked for a pass again instead of a cleaner spray.</p>

<p><a href="https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP/ToxFAQs/ToxFAQsDetails.aspx?faqid=954&amp;toxid=199">The CDC says companies now bring 1,4-dioxane down to low levels before it goes into home products.</a> So it can be done. By the last year, only 14 products on that list still had one.</p>

<p>And the bottle on your sink looked the same the whole time.</p>

<h2 id="in-everything">Isn't this in everything anyway?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: you cannot fix it all tonight, so start with the one at your sink.</p>

<p>You might be thinking, it is in everything. Why bother?</p>

<p>That is fair. You cannot fix a whole house tonight. So do not try.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.epa.gov/assessing-and-managing-chemicals-under-tsca/risk-evaluation-14-dioxane">The US EPA says 1,4-dioxane can stay behind in detergents and cleaning products.</a> That is the shelf by your sink.</p>

<p>So start with the one thing you use every day, with your hands in it. For you, that might be the dish spray.</p>

<p>Then the next bottle beside it.</p>

<h2 id="what-do-i-do">What do I use instead?</h2>

<p>For the oven door, mix baking soda with a little water into a thick paste. Spread it on the scorch. Let it sit, then wipe it off with a damp cloth.</p>

<p>It takes a little longer. There is nothing hidden in it.</p>
