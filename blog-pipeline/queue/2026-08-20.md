<h2 id="the-short-answer">The short answer</h2>
<p>On January 1, 2025 a nonstick pan became illegal to sell in the state of Minnesota.</p>
<p><strong><a href="https://www.pca.state.mn.us/air-water-land-climate/2025-pfas-prohibitions">The Minnesota Pollution Control Agency put it in writing: Teflon is a member of the PFAS family, and as a PFAS, Teflon is prohibited from use in cookware.</a></strong></p>
<p>The same pan is still on the shelf in the other forty nine states. Nothing about the pan changed. Only the state line did.</p>

<h2 id="what-happens-when-it-gets-hot">What actually happens when the pan gets hot?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: past roughly 500 degrees the coating stops being a coating and starts being fumes.</p>

<p>You know the pan. Black, light, twelve years old, and the one you reach for on a Tuesday when everybody is hungry and nobody is patient. It went on the burner while you found the eggs. Then it sat there empty for four minutes because the phone rang.</p>

<p>That is the whole scenario. Nothing dramatic, nothing careless. An empty pan and a phone call.</p>

<p>An empty pan climbs fast, because there is no food in it soaking up the heat. <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK594276/">The medical literature on polymer fume fever, catalogued in StatPearls at the National Library of Medicine, describes what follows plainly: breathe the degradation products of an overheated fluoropolymer and you get chills, fever, headache and body aches four to ten hours later, usually clearing inside two days.</a></p>

<p>Doctors have a nickname for it. They call it Teflon flu.</p>

<p><strong>Here is the part that stayed with me.</strong> It arrives four to ten hours after you cooked. By then dinner is done, the kitchen is clean, and you are on the sofa. Nobody connects the two. You tell yourself you are coming down with something.</p>

<h2 id="what-is-the-coating">What is the coating made of?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: a plastic film, plus the chemistry that was used to make it.</p>

<h3>PTFE, polytetrafluoroethylene</h3>
<p>The slippery layer itself. It is the compound sold under the Teflon name, and Minnesota's environmental agency states directly that it belongs to the PFAS family and is banned in cookware there as of January 2025. Intact and cool, it is one of the most chemically stable materials ever made. Hot and scratched is a different question.</p>

<h3>PFOA, the one they used to make it</h3>
<p>Not in the pan. In the factory. PFOA was a processing aid used to manufacture the coating, and it is the compound at the centre of every lawsuit you have heard about. <a href="https://www.epa.gov/assessing-and-managing-chemicals-under-tsca/fact-sheet-20102015-pfoa-stewardship-program">The EPA invited eight companies into a voluntary program in 2006 with two goals: a 95 percent cut by 2010, and elimination from emissions and products by 2015.</a> All eight reported they met it. American nonstick made since then is PFOA free, and the companies say so on the box.</p>

<h3>The replacements</h3>
<p>PFOA left and other fluorinated compounds took the job. <a href="https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/04/26/2024-07773/pfas-national-primary-drinking-water-regulation">In April 2024 the EPA finalised the first legally enforceable national drinking water limits for PFAS, setting PFOA and PFOS at 4 parts per trillion and adding a limit for GenX, one of the replacement chemistries.</a> Four parts per trillion is about four drops spread through a thousand Olympic swimming pools. That is where a federal agency put the ceiling.</p>

<h3>Forever, and why that word is not marketing</h3>
<p>PFAS are called forever chemicals because the carbon fluorine bond is the strongest single bond in organic chemistry. It does not break down in a landfill, in groundwater, or in a person. That property is exactly why the coating works, and exactly why it is a problem once it leaves the pan.</p>

<h2 id="did-they-know">Did the company know before you did?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: the EPA says the information goes back to 1981.</p>

<p>This is the part that settles it, and it is not a chemistry question. It is a character question.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.epa.gov/archive/epapages/newsroom_archive/newsreleases/fdcb2f665cac66bb852570d7005d6665.html">On December 14, 2005 the EPA announced a settlement with DuPont over PFOA: a $10.25 million civil penalty plus $6.25 million in environmental projects.</a> The agency's own words in that release were, "This is the largest civil administrative penalty EPA has ever obtained under any environmental statute. Not by a little, by a lot."</p>

<p>Seven of the eight counts were alleged violations of Section 8(e) of the Toxic Substances Control Act. That section says one thing. If you learn your chemical poses a substantial risk to human health or the environment, you tell the EPA.</p>

<p><strong>The release describes the violations as multiple failures to report information about substantial risk of injury that the company obtained "from as early as 1981 and as recently as 2004."</strong></p>

<p>Read that date again. 1981. Twenty three years of holding it, and a federal enforcement action is what finally pried it loose. The same EPA release names what the chemistry was for: non-stick surfaces on cookware.</p>

<p>The money kept coming after that. <a href="https://cen.acs.org/policy/litigation/Court-approves-10-billion-PFAS/102/i11">A federal judge approved a class settlement in which 3M expects to pay roughly $10.3 billion over thirteen years to more than 11,000 public water systems</a> over alleged PFAS contamination, which 3M settled without admitting liability.</p>

<h2 id="does-a-scratch-matter">Does a scratched pan matter?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: a scratch is coating that has left. The only open question is where it went.</p>

<p>Turn your pan to the light. The criss cross marks are the metal spatula. The dull patches are the dishwasher. The bare grey showing through is coating that used to be there and is not any more.</p>

<p>It did not evaporate. It went into whatever you cooked, and into whoever ate it.</p>

<p>Now do the same with the air fryer basket, because it carries the same family of coating and it runs hotter and drier than any pan you own. Most people have never once thought about the air fryer, and it has been in the house three years going four nights a week.</p>

<h2 id="is-it-poisoning-me">Is my pan poisoning me right now?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: no agency has said that, and I am not going to pretend otherwise.</p>

<p>Here is the case against my own argument, in full.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-177/subpart-B/section-177.1550">The FDA has authorised perfluorocarbon resins for repeated food contact use since the 1960s, at 21 CFR 177.1550.</a> Cool, intact PTFE is a very large and very stable molecule, and large polymerised molecules are poorly absorbed by the body when swallowed. There has been no recall of nonstick cookware. No federal finding that a frying pan gave anybody a disease. That record penalty was for failing to report, not for proving harm to a cook. And the PFOA those lawsuits are about left American manufacturing a decade ago.</p>

<p>All of that is true, and leaving it out would make this post weaker rather than stronger.</p>

<p>What I am left with is not a study. It is a pattern. A company held risk information from 1981 to 2004 and paid the largest penalty in EPA history rather than volunteer it. One state read the same file and banned the product outright. The other forty nine did not.</p>

<p>Nobody has to prove harm to me for that pattern to be enough.</p>

<h2 id="what-do-i-cook-in-instead">What do I cook in instead?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: a material instead of a coating, and stop replacing the same pan every three years.</p>

<p>Cast iron and plain stainless steel have no coating to come off, because the surface is the metal. Cast iron gets slicker the longer you own it. Stainless will never be nonstick and does not pretend to be, and a properly hot pan with fat in it releases food anyway, which is how your grandmother cooked eggs for sixty years without owning a single black pan.</p>

<p>I will not send you down the aisle to compare boxes. Every nonstick pan on that shelf carries PFOA free on the front, every one of them is telling the truth, and all of it answers a question nobody was asking. You cannot read your way out of this one. You can only stop buying from the people who did it.</p>

<p>That is why my own family does not buy from those shelves at all. We shop through a private membership store instead. Not a better aisle. A different supply chain, with a name attached and a person who answers the phone.</p>

<p>Start with the pan you use most. Not all of them, not today. One.</p>

<h2 id="faq">What else do people ask?</h2>

<p><strong>Is ceramic nonstick a different thing?</strong> Ceramic coatings are a different chemistry from PTFE, so the fluoropolymer story above does not apply to them. They also wear out faster, which is the trade. The honest limit is that ceramic coatings are far less studied than PTFE, so there is less to tell you either way.</p>

<p><strong>How hot does my pan actually get?</strong> Hotter than most people think, and fastest when it is empty. A dry pan on a high burner can pass 500 degrees in a few minutes because there is no food absorbing the heat. Preheating with the fat already in it is the easiest single change you can make today.</p>

<p><strong>My pan says PFOA free. Is that enough?</strong> It is accurate, and it is answering the wrong question. PFOA has been out of American nonstick manufacture since the 2015 phase out, so every pan on the shelf can print it. The coating itself is still a fluoropolymer.</p>

<p><strong>Is the air fryer coated too?</strong> Most nonstick air fryer baskets use the same family of coating, and they run hot and dry by design. If you are checking pans, check the basket.</p>

<p><strong>Does a scratch mean I have to throw it out today?</strong> No, and panic is not the plan. Start with the one you use most days and work down from there.</p>
