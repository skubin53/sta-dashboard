<h2 id="what-the-lawsuit-says">What does the Native lawsuit say is in the unscented stick?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: ginger root extract. The complaint calls it a fragrance, and its price table puts Unscented 93 cents an ounce higher.</p>

<p>You stood in the store aisle longer than you meant to, a white stick in each hand. You put the scented one back.</p>

<p>No shame in that. Unscented was printed big on the front. The ginger sat in the fine print, the complaint says.</p>

<p>You want a deodorant that smells like nothing. That is a fair thing to want. The complaint says the problem is the word Procter &amp; Gamble put on the front.</p>

<p>Native looks like a small natural brand. <a href="https://www.cnbc.com/2017/11/15/pg-has-acquired-native-natural-deodorant-brand.html">P&amp;G bought it in November 2017, CNBC reported.</a> Back then the stick ran about $12, more than double some P&amp;G brands.</p>

<p><a href="https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.nysd.670442/gov.uscourts.nysd.670442.1.0.pdf">The shopper who sued bought hers at a Target in New York in the spring of 2026.</a> She saw "unscented" and believed there was no fragrance in it, the complaint says.</p>

<p>It says the stick holds ginger root extract. It calls that "an aromatic essential oil" with "a warm, spicy, and fresh scent."</p>

<p>Then it lays out the price. Native Sea Salt &amp; Cedar was $13.47 for 3.1 ounces. Native Unscented was $13.99 for 2.65 ounces.</p>

<p>Here is the math. By the complaint's own numbers, the scented stick was $4.35 an ounce. Unscented was $5.28.</p>

<p>So by those numbers, you paid more for less stick. That would sting all on its own.</p>

<h3 id="ginger-root">Zingiber officinale, the ginger on the list</h3>

<p>The case is about a word, and a price. The front says Unscented, and the complaint says ginger is in the stick.</p>

<p>The complaint also says shoppers had "no reason to know." It says "average consumers may not know" this extract gives off a scent.</p>

<h2 id="is-there-a-payout">Is there a Native settlement or payout?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: no. The case was dropped on October 2, 2026, with no money set up for shoppers.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/74642895/chiaravalloti-v-procter-gamble-company/">On October 2, 2026, the shopper's lawyers filed a notice of voluntary dismissal, the court record shows.</a> That is her side dropping the case.</p>

<p>The record does not say why. It shows no ruling on the label and no class settlement. So there is no claim form.</p>

<p>The suit only ever asked to cover New York shoppers.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/74912980/chiaravalloti-v-the-procter-gamble-company/">The same day, a new federal case with the same name was opened in California, the court record shows.</a> Its papers are not public yet.</p>

<p>A dropped case is not a cleared label, though. The word is still the question.</p>

<h2 id="the-other-side">Is there a case for P&amp;G?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: yes, a fair one. No court found the label false, and the suit never said the stick hurt anyone.</p>

<p><strong>One.</strong> A complaint is one side's story. This one was dropped before P&amp;G ever answered it in court.</p>

<p><strong>Two.</strong> Ginger root is a plant, and nobody in the suit said it made anyone sick.</p>

<p><strong>Three.</strong> <a href="https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.nysd.623881/gov.uscourts.nysd.623881.42.0.pdf">In April 2025 a federal judge in New York threw out a different shopper suit against Native.</a> It went after the "72-hour odor protection" promise. The judge wrote that Native had given the shoppers' lawyers evidence it "had in fact clinically tested the deodorant."</p>

<p>So why does it still sit wrong with me?</p>

<p>Because the ad industry's own reviewers have flagged words on Native twice before.</p>

<p><a href="https://bbbprograms.org/media/newsroom/decisions/native-pg">In June 2024, an ad industry watchdog called NAD looked at Native's claims.</a> It said "simple ingredients" was not supported for its deodorants. It asked P&amp;G to change it or drop it. To be fair, the same review backed the "Clean. Simple. Effective." line.</p>

<p>The same decision asked P&amp;G to drop "Born in the USA." NAD said P&amp;G "did not provide any evidence" its products were made in the USA. P&amp;G said it "respectfully disagrees" with parts of it, and agreed to comply.</p>

<p><a href="https://bbbprograms.org/media/newsroom/decisions/narb-recommends-native-cosmetics-discontinue-moisture-absorption-and-underarm-wetness-protection-cla">In December 2019, a review panel looked at two Native wetness claims.</a> P&amp;G had owned Native for two years. The panel found Native "failed to provide a reasonable basis" for saying its deodorant absorbs wetness and protects against it. Native agreed to drop both.</p>

<p>And so the fix is a different company to buy from.</p>

<h2 id="faq">What else do people ask?</h2>

<p><strong>Is there a Native deodorant settlement I can claim?</strong></p>
<p>Not from this case. It was dropped on October 2, 2026, with no ruling and no class settlement. There is no claim form.</p>

<p><strong>Who did the Native lawsuit cover?</strong></p>
<p>Only New York. It asked to cover buyers there from the past four years.</p>

<p><strong>Did the lawsuit say the ginger hurt anyone?</strong></p>
<p>No. It names no one the ginger hurt. But it does say ginger root extract has compounds that can cause "skin sensitivity." It asked for money over the price, not over anyone's skin.</p>
