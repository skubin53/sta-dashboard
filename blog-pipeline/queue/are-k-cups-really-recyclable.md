<h2 id="what-the-sec-said">What did the SEC say Keurig got wrong?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: it told investors the pods get recycled, and left out who refused to take them.</p>

<p>You know the pod. A little white tub. A foil lid you peel back.</p>

<p>It has felt like the responsible one. It has a number on it.</p>

<p>In its 2019 and 2020 reports, Keurig said its testing "validate[d] that [K-Cup pods] can be effectively recycled."</p>

<p>Here is what it did not say. Two of the largest recycling companies in the country had "significant concerns." They said they did not plan to accept the pods.</p>

<p>The SEC called the reports incomplete and inaccurate. Keurig paid 1.5 million dollars. It did not admit or deny a thing.</p>

<p>So the claim that let you feel fine about the pod was missing the part that mattered.</p>

<h2 id="can-it-be-recycled">So can a K-Cup actually be recycled?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: on paper yes, in your town almost never.</p>

<p>A K-Cup is a number 5 plastic. Polypropylene.</p>

<p>On paper, number 5 can be recycled. Where you live, it usually is not.</p>

<p>The EPA keeps the count. <a href="https://www.epa.gov/facts-and-figures-about-materials-waste-and-recycling/plastics-material-specific-data">In 2018, only 8.7 percent of all plastic was recycled.</a> A soda bottle did far better than that. A tiny tub did far worse.</p>

<p>Small light things slip through the sorting machines. They are too little to catch.</p>

<p>So the pod goes in the blue bin at your house and comes out at the landfill anyway.</p>

<p>The same gap between the label and the truth shows up all over the kitchen. <a href="https://join.switchtoamerica.com/post/are-plastic-cutting-boards-safe">The cutting board is another one.</a></p>

<h2 id="why-did-i-rinse">Why did I rinse them all this time?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: because a label told you to, and the rule says a label has to be true.</p>

<p>You did everything right. You peeled the lid. You tipped out the grounds. You rinsed the cup.</p>

<p>Seven hundred small acts of care a year. Done on a promise.</p>

<p>There is a rule about that promise. <a href="https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/topics/truth-advertising/green-guides">The FTC says a company can only call a thing "recyclable" plainly if most people can actually recycle it where they live.</a></p>

<p>If most people cannot, the plain word is misleading.</p>

<p>That is the whole fight. Not whether a lab can do it once. Whether your town does it at all.</p>

<h2 id="faq">What else do people ask?</h2>

<p><strong>Is a K-Cup recyclable at all?</strong> The pod is a number 5 plastic, which can be recycled in theory. In practice most curbside programs do not take small pods, and the EPA puts the overall plastic recycling rate under 9 percent.</p>

<p><strong>Did Keurig get in trouble for this?</strong> The SEC charged it over the recyclability claim in its investor reports. Keurig paid a 1.5 million dollar penalty in September 2024 without admitting or denying the findings.</p>

<p><strong>Do I have to keep rinsing them?</strong> That depends on your town. If your program does not accept number 5 pods, rinsing does not change where they end up.</p>

<p><strong>Are the pods bad for my health?</strong> This story is about the recycling claim, not your body. I will not claim more than that.</p>
