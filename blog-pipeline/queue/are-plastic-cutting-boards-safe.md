<h2 id="off-the-board">What comes off the board and into the carrots?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: tiny bits of the board itself.</p>

<p>Go look at your board. The white one by the sink.</p>

<p>Run your thumb over it. You can feel the grooves.</p>

<p>Every groove is plastic that used to be there.</p>

<p>It did not evaporate. It came off on the knife.</p>

<p>A team at North Dakota State University put a number on it. Their work ran in <em>Environmental Science &amp; Technology</em> in May 2023.</p>

<p>They chopped carrots. Then they counted what came off.</p>

<p><a href="https://portal.findresearcher.sdu.dk/en/publications/cutting-boards-an-overlooked-source-of-microplastics-in-human-foo/">Their estimate for one person, for one year: 7.4 to 50.7 grams of plastic off a polyethylene board.</a></p>

<p>Fifty grams is under two ounces. Of plastic. In your food.</p>

<p>Most of the pieces were smaller than a grain of salt.</p>

<p><a href="https://time.com/7336434/plastic-cutting-board-material-microplastics-alternatives/">Time said it plainer in November 2025. One knife stroke can release 100 to 300 bits.</a></p>

<p>About half stay on the board and wash down the drain. The other half you eat.</p>

<h2 id="which-plastic">Which plastic is your board made of?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: one of two, and both of them shed.</p>

<h3>Polyethylene</h3>

<p>The soft white board. The one that scratches easy.</p>

<p>The 2023 count came to 14.5 to 71.9 million bits a year.</p>

<h3>Polypropylene</h3>

<p>The harder board. Often colored. Often the thin bendy mats.</p>

<p>That one came to 79.4 million bits a year.</p>

<p>By weight it shed 5 to 60 percent more than polyethylene. By count, 14 to 71 percent more.</p>

<p>And so the tougher board is not the safer board.</p>

<p>You cannot tell which one is on your counter by looking at it.</p>

<p>That is the whole trouble. Nothing is printed on a cutting board.</p>

<h2 id="mice">What happened when they fed it to mice for twelve weeks?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: the wood group came out cleanest.</p>

<p>The 2023 study only counted. It did not follow the plastic into a living body.</p>

<p>A second team did that. <a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11980920/">Their work ran in <em>Environmental Health Perspectives</em> in April 2025.</a></p>

<p>They chopped mouse food on three boards. Polypropylene, polyethylene, and willow wood.</p>

<p>Then they fed it to the mice. Four weeks for one group, twelve for another.</p>

<p>Here is what turned up at twelve weeks.</p>

<p>The polypropylene group had more inflammation markers in the blood. The gut wall measured worse too.</p>

<p>The polyethylene group had a shift in their gut bugs.</p>

<p>The wood group had the lowest inflammation of the three.</p>

<p>The wood food had no plastic in it at all. The team looked and found none.</p>

<h2 id="the-other-side">The case against my own story</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: here is what cuts the other way.</p>

<p>I will not hide the parts that do not help me.</p>

<p><strong>One.</strong> <a href="https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2023/06/230601155739.htm">The 2023 team also dropped the plastic onto mouse cells in a dish. Nothing happened. The cells lived fine for 72 hours.</a></p>

<p><strong>Two.</strong> Wood boards shed more bits, not fewer. In some tests, 4 to 22 times more. Those bits are wood, and no yearly figure was worked out for them.</p>

<p><strong>Three.</strong> Mice are not you. Those mice ate board food at every meal for twelve weeks.</p>

<p><strong>Four.</strong> <a href="https://www.ndsu.edu/news/ndsu-microplastics-study-featured-time-magazine">The university says it plainly. There is no conclusive evidence yet showing harm from cutting board microplastics in people.</a></p>

<p>So why does this still sit wrong with me?</p>

<p>Because of the machine they counted with.</p>

<p>It could only see bits bigger than 20 micrometers. Syeed Iskander, who led the work, told Time the smaller ones were there too.</p>

<p>Every number up there is a floor. Not a ceiling.</p>

<p>His own words on all of it: "There's a lot of uncertainty."</p>

<p>And so I am not waiting for certainty about my own dinner.</p>

<p>And you are not the only one who went looking.</p>

<p>Women my age ask me about this board most weeks.</p>

<p>So no. You are not paranoid. You are early.</p>

<h2 id="what-i-use">What do I chop on instead?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: wood. One board, and it lasts.</p>

<p>Isn't plastic in everything? Yes. It is.</p>

<p>That is exactly why you do not start with everything.</p>

<p>Change the one thing your knife lands on every day. That is it.</p>

<p>Get a solid wood board. Not the pressed kind held together with glue.</p>

<p>Wash it by hand. Stand it up to dry. Rub oil into it once a month.</p>

<p>Wood gets better with age. Plastic only gets worse.</p>

<p>And the grooved plastic one? Throw it out.</p>

<p>Not next month. Tonight, on your way past the bin.</p>

<p>Here is the part nobody says out loud.</p>

<p>There is nothing on a cutting board to compare. Nothing to weigh up.</p>

<p>So the only question left is who made it, and whether they would tell you what went in it.</p>

<p>And so the fix is not a smarter shopper. It is a better supplier.</p>

<p>That is why my family buys through a private membership store instead. We are the manufacturer. No middleman, no markup. One name on it, and a person who picks up the phone.</p>

<h2 id="faq">What else do people ask?</h2>

<p><strong>Does washing the board get the plastic off?</strong> About half of it. Time reported that roughly half the bits stay on the board and go down the drain when you wash it. The other half is already in the food. Washing sorts out the board, not the dinner.</p>

<p><strong>Is a bamboo board the same as a wood board?</strong> No. Bamboo is a grass, cut into strips and glued into a board. The two studies here tested plastic and solid wood. Willow was the wood in the 2025 one. Solid wood is the swap with the data behind it.</p>

<p><strong>My board is scratched to bits. Is that worse?</strong> The people who did the counting say to swap boards more often, or move to wood. A grooved board is a board that has already given up plastic.</p>

<p><strong>Do I have to bin every plastic thing in the kitchen tonight?</strong> No. Do the board first, because a knife hits it every single day. Then find the next one. One a week is a quick pace.</p>
