<h2 id="what-was-in-the-bottle">What was in the bottle that said formaldehyde free?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: formaldehyde, up to 12 percent of it, by the state's count.</p>

<p>Somewhere in your 40s, the frizz got worse. Now the humid mornings always win.</p>

<p>And so you booked the treatment. It cost more than your last good pair of shoes.</p>

<p>You sat in that chair for hours, and your eyes watered. Your stylist got up and opened a window. Neither of you said a word about it.</p>

<p>No shame in that. You trusted what the front of the bottle told you.</p>

<p>Now look at what the labs found in it.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.cdph.ca.gov/Programs/CCDPHP/DEODC/OHB/CSCP/CDPH%20Document%20Library/BrazilianBlowoutQA.pdf">In March 2011, California's health department said at least four labs had tested it. They found 4 to 12 percent formaldehyde.</a></p>

<p><a href="https://www.osha.gov/hair-salons/background">In December 2010, a federal worker safety lab tested one bottle in an Ohio salon.</a> The lab is called NIOSH. The label said "Formaldehyde Free." It held 11 percent.</p>

<h3 id="methylene-glycol">Formaldehyde and methylene glycol</h3>

<p>So how can a bottle say one thing and hold another? The gas has a second name.</p>

<p>Mixed with water, it goes by methylene glycol. Heat turns it back into gas, the health department says. The dryer does it, and so does the flat iron.</p>

<p>And so it comes up off your own head, right under your nose.</p>

<h2 id="why-did-my-eyes-water">Why did my eyes water in the chair?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: the gas can irritate your eyes and nose, OSHA says, and the heat puts it in the air.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.osha.gov/news/newsreleases/osha-national-news-release/20110922">In September 2011, OSHA said its air tests found the gas at hazardous levels.</a> That was in salons using the maker's Acai smoothing solution.</p>

<p>The state health department said stylists and clients reported burning eyes and throats. Some reported nosebleeds.</p>

<p>That August, the maker wrote to salon owners. It said all of OSHA's air tests on its solution came in under the limit.</p>

<p>OSHA wrote back to say it was wrong. Its news release called what salons got "factually incorrect information."</p>

<p>So your watering eyes were not in your head. That open window had a reason.</p>

<h2 id="what-did-they-agree-to">What did Brazilian Blowout agree to in California?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: to pay $600,000 and stop calling two solutions formaldehyde free. It admitted nothing.</p>

<p>The state sued the maker, GIB, LLC, in November 2010. It said the maker broke five state laws. False ads and no cancer warning were two of them.</p>

<p><a href="https://oag.ca.gov/sites/all/files/agweb/pdfs/prop65/brazilian-blowout.pdf">The court papers list what the state says the company told buyers: "formaldehyde free," "hyde free," "salon-safe" and "safe."</a></p>

<p>In January 2012, the maker settled. It agreed to pay $300,000 in penalties and $300,000 for the state's costs.</p>

<p>It agreed to pull claims like those from its ads and its website. It agreed to the CAUTION sticker, too.</p>

<p>The same papers say it does not admit it broke any law.</p>

<p>Put side by side, the front of the bottle and the lab do not match.</p>

<h2 id="the-other-side">The case against my own story</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: three fair points, and they are all true.</p>

<p><strong>One.</strong> Your stylist breathes it far more often than you do. OSHA's limits are built for workers like her, in salon air all day. You sit in that chair a few times a year.</p>

<p><strong>Two.</strong> In the Ohio salon NIOSH tested, every air reading was under OSHA's limits.</p>

<p><strong>Three.</strong> <a href="https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=HSC&amp;sectionNum=108980">Since January 1, 2025, California law bans formaldehyde and methylene glycol in cosmetics.</a> The company now sells a line it says is made without them.</p>

<p>So why does it still sit wrong with me?</p>

<p>Because the words on the front are what failed before. NIOSH tested a bottle that said formaldehyde free, and it held 11 percent. The stylist using it breathed air over NIOSH's own, stricter limit at almost every step.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.cnn.com/2026/01/05/health/hair-straightening-formaldehyde-fda-deadline">There is still no federal ban. The plan for one missed its latest target date in December 2025, CNN said.</a> <a href="https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/BILLS-119hr7685ih">In February 2026, members of Congress put forward a bill for one, keratin treatments included.</a> It is still sitting in committee.</p>

<p>And you were never the only one in that chair with watering eyes. Stylists and their clients took their questions to the state.</p>

<h2 id="what-do-i-do">What do I do about the frizz instead?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: keep the salon blowout and skip the smoothing solution.</p>

<p>Start with your next salon visit, the one already on the books.</p>

<p>Call and ask for a plain blowout. A round brush and a dryer, with no smoothing solution on your hair.</p>

<p>Here is what that will not do. It will not keep your hair smooth for months. It lasts until your next wash, and I am not going to pretend it does more.</p>

<p>At home, the swap is your shampoo and conditioner. Pick a gentle, cleaner pair. Let your hair air dry part of the way before you pick up the dryer.</p>

<p>That is what Switch to America is for. We are a private membership store and the manufacturer. No middlemen, no markup.</p>

<p>Do the small thing first. Before your next visit, call the salon and change it to a plain blowout.</p>

<h2 id="faq">What else do people ask?</h2>

<p><strong>Is a formaldehyde free keratin treatment safe?</strong></p>
<p>Nobody can promise that from the words on the front. NIOSH tested a Brazilian Blowout bottle with that label. It held 11 percent formaldehyde.</p>

<p><strong>Is formaldehyde banned in California?</strong></p>
<p>Yes, since January 1, 2025. The law there bans it in beauty products, along with methylene glycol. There is no federal ban yet.</p>

<p><strong>Is it worse for my stylist than for me?</strong></p>
<p>Over a year, yes. She breathes salon air all day. OSHA's limits were written for workers like her. You get it one visit at a time.</p>

<p><strong>Can I still get smooth hair from the salon?</strong></p>
<p>Yes, for a few days. Ask for a plain blowout with a round brush and a dryer, and no smoothing solution. It lasts until your next wash.</p>
