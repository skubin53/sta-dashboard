<h2 id="the-short-answer">The short answer</h2>
<p>Do not sign it this week. Do not spend it this month.</p>
<p>If you are 40 or over, the law gives you at least 21 days to think. It is 45 days if others were let go with you. Then you get 7 more days to take your signature back.</p>
<p>That is the <strong><a href="https://www.eeoc.gov/laws/guidance/qa-understanding-waivers-discrimination-claims-employee-severance-agreements">EEOC's own guide</a></strong>: "If you are being rushed, ask for more time."</p>
<p>The paper is written to be signed fast. The law is written so you can take your time.</p>

<h2 id="ibm-paper">What did IBM hand a 52 year old on his way out?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: one month's pay, and a deadline tucked into the attachment.</p>

<p>You read it twice at the kitchen table. The envelope has your name typed on it. The only line you really took in was the date they want it back.</p>

<p>Look at what one company put in that envelope.</p>

<p>In March 2016, IBM let Michael Rumsey go in a group layoff. <a href="https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.mad.274397/gov.uscourts.mad.274397.47.0.pdf">He was 52, the court order says.</a> His packet held a "Resource Action Separation Agreement."</p>

<p>He got one month's salary and three months of health and life insurance. For that, he signed away his other claims. His age claim could only go to arbitration, on his own.</p>

<p>The attachment said this, in IBM's own words. "If the demand for arbitration is not timely submitted, the claim shall be deemed waived."</p>

<p>That July, he filed an age charge with the EEOC.</p>

<p>In 2020 the EEOC finished looking at IBM's mass layoffs from 2013 to 2018. It found "reasonable cause" to believe IBM broke the age law. Older workers made up 85.85% of the pool IBM looked at for cuts. That is the court's summary of the agency's finding.</p>

<p>Then IBM said he was too late. By its reading, the paper made him file for arbitration by December 2016. That was 300 days after he left. His right to sue letter from the EEOC came more than four years after that.</p>

<p>On September 30, 2025, a federal judge in Boston said no. His paper could not cut short the time the age law gives him. <a href="https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.mad.274397/gov.uscourts.mad.274397.58.0.pdf">On May 19, 2026, the court made it final</a>. IBM may not hold him to that deadline.</p>

<p>Federal courts in New York have upheld IBM's deadline in other cases. So where you live matters. A date printed on your paper is not always the date the law gives you.</p>

<h2 id="the-list">Why was the list of who else got cut missing from IBM's envelope?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: IBM stopped handing it out in 2014. It shows who else was let go, and how old they were.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.eeoc.gov/laws/guidance/qa-understanding-waivers-discrimination-claims-employee-severance-agreements">Since 1990 the law has asked for it.</a> In a group layoff, they owe you the job titles and ages of everybody picked. And the ages of everybody kept.</p>

<p><a href="https://features.propublica.org/ibm/ibm-age-discrimination-american-workers/">ProPublica explained why in March 2018.</a> Employers had begun making severance "contingent on a laid-off worker signing away the right to sue the company."</p>

<p>Until 2014, IBM handed out two lists. One showed the positions and ages of everybody laid off from the unit. The other showed everybody staying on. Then, ProPublica reported, IBM stopped.</p>

<p>It took "age" off the list of claims workers gave up. Age claims went to private arbitration instead, kept confidential, one person at a time. IBM's lawyers told the EEOC the list rule no longer applied, ProPublica reported.</p>

<p>And so the one sheet of paper that could show you a pattern never came.</p>

<h2 id="what-you-are-owed">What does the law say you are owed before you sign?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: at least three weeks to think, and one more week to take it back.</p>

<h3>Twenty one days, or forty five</h3>

<p>The EEOC puts it plainly. "A waiver must provide the employee with at least 21 days to consider the offer." In a group layoff it is 45. If they make a real change to the final offer, the clock starts over.</p>

<h3>Seven days to change your mind</h3>

<p>After you sign, you get seven days to take it back. "The seven-day revocation period cannot be changed or waived by either party for any reason."</p>

<h3>Plain words, and a lawyer</h3>

<p>Your paper has to be in plain words. It has to tell you in writing to see a lawyer. The EEOC even points to a waiver a court threw out as too hard to understand. The case is Thomforde v. IBM.</p>

<h2 id="monday">What would I do first on Monday?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: put the dates on paper, then write down the people.</p>

<h3>Circle three dates</h3>

<p>The day you got the packet. Day 21, or day 45. Then seven days after whenever you sign. Put all three on the fridge calendar.</p>

<h3>Ask for the list</h3>

<p>Did others go out the door with you? Email HR and ask for their job titles and ages. Keep your own copy somewhere that is not their laptop.</p>

<h3>Let a lawyer read it before day 21</h3>

<p>The paper should already tell you to. Do it while the clock still has days on it.</p>

<h3>Write down the people, not the job title</h3>

<p>Every name that would still take your call. The one you trained, the client who asked for you by name. Those are your references. No layoff list can take them from you.</p>

<p>Then ask five questions of whatever comes next, me included. What does it cost to start? Is there a monthly requirement? What happens to my customers if I stop? Where is the income disclosure? Who gets paid when I start?</p>

<h2 id="faq">What else are women asking me about this?</h2>

<p><strong>Do I have to sign my severance agreement right away?</strong></p>
<p>No. If you are 40 or over, the law gives you at least 21 days. In a group layoff, it is 45. After you sign, you get 7 more days to take it back. Taking that time is not being difficult.</p>

<p><strong>Is my severance taxed?</strong></p>
<p>Yes. <a href="https://www.irs.gov/publications/p525">The IRS says you must count severance pay as income.</a> So the number in the letter is not the number that lands in your account.</p>

<p><strong>Should I put my severance into a business?</strong></p>
<p>Not in the first month, and not into mine. If anybody wants your lump sum to get you started, walk away.</p>
