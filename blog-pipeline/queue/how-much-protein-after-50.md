<h2 id="the-short-answer">The short answer</h2>
<p>You stand at the stove and wonder if you eat enough protein. And if you cook for your mom or dad, you wonder about them too.</p>
<p><strong><a href="https://www.health.harvard.edu/blog/how-much-protein-do-you-need-every-day-201506188096">The US minimum, called the RDA, is 0.8 grams of protein per kilogram. At 150 pounds, that is about 54 grams a day.</a></strong> <a href="https://cdn.realfood.gov/DGA.pdf">The new US Dietary Guidelines, from January 2026, aim for 1.2 to 1.6 grams per kilogram. That is about 82 to 109 grams.</a> <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jamda.2013.05.021">A group of experts on aging looked at people over 65. They say at least 1.0 to 1.2.</a> That is about 68 to 82 grams.</p>
<p>So the minimum is low, and the newer advice is higher. And the beef on your plate? Just four companies buy most of the young cattle it comes from.</p>

<h2 id="how-much-protein">How much protein do you need after 50?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: the US minimum is about 0.36 grams per pound you weigh. The 2026 US guidelines aim higher, at about 0.54 to 0.73 grams per pound.</p>

<p>You stood at the meat case longer than you meant to. A pack of ground beef in your hand. Is it enough protein for you? For your dad?</p>

<h3>What does 0.8 grams per kilogram mean?</h3>

<p>A kilogram is about 2.2 pounds. So the RDA works out to about 0.36 grams for each pound you weigh. At 150 pounds, that is about 54 grams a day.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.health.harvard.edu/blog/how-much-protein-do-you-need-every-day-201506188096">Harvard Health calls it "the minimum amount you need to keep from getting sick."</a> It is a floor, not a goal.</p>

<p>And the minimum does not go up with age. <a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5420553/">A 2017 review from McMaster University says the RDA has no separate number for older adults.</a></p>

<p><a href="https://cdn.realfood.gov/DGA.pdf">In January 2026, the USDA and HHS put out new Dietary Guidelines for Americans. They set a protein goal of 1.2 to 1.6 grams per kilogram a day.</a> At 150 pounds, that is about 82 to 109 grams.</p>

<p>The same guidelines say some older adults need fewer calories but the same or more protein. At 65, one group of experts on aging gives its own number.</p>

<h2 id="after-65">What changes after 65?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: a group of experts on aging says people over 65 need more than younger adults. At 150 pounds, that is at least 68 to 82 grams a day.</p>

<p>In 2013, a group of experts on aging called PROT-AGE went through the research on protein and age.</p>

<p><a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jamda.2013.05.021">They wrote that "older adults need more dietary protein than do younger adults."</a> For people over 65, they advise at least 1.0 to 1.2 grams per kilogram a day.</p>

<h3>What does 1.0 to 1.2 mean at 150 pounds?</h3>

<p>Say your mom weighs 150 pounds. That is about 68 kilograms.</p>

<p>At 1.0 gram per kilogram, she lands at about 68 grams a day. At 1.2, it is about 82 grams.</p>

<p>That is about 14 to 28 grams more than the RDA, and at or under the new US goal. It is a study group's guideline, not a prescription for her.</p>

<h3>Who should not eat more protein?</h3>

<p>PROT-AGE names one clear exception. People with severe kidney disease who are not on dialysis may need to limit protein.</p>

<p>If that is your dad, the higher number is not for him. His number comes from the doctor who treats his kidneys.</p>

<p>For everyone else at your table, the next question is where that protein comes from.</p>

<h2 id="where-beef-comes-from">Where does most store beef come from?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: four companies buy 85 of every 100 young cattle raised for beef.</p>

<p>You want good beef for your dad. Nothing wrong with wanting that.</p>

<p>The trouble starts long before the beef gets to the store.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.ers.usda.gov/amber-waves/2024/january/concentration-in-u-s-meatpacking-industry-and-how-it-affects-competition-and-cattle-prices">In January 2024, the USDA's own research service called meatpacking "a concentrated industry." It said the four largest firms handle 85 percent of all steer and heifer purchases.</a></p>

<p>Steers and heifers are the young cattle raised for your steaks and roasts. In 1980, the top four bought 36 percent of them.</p>

<p>So a rancher has few places to sell. In most of the country, USDA says, it is two to four buyers.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.nbcnews.com/business/consumer/trump-doj-meatpacker-investigation-price-fixing-beef-prices-rcna242671">News reports call them the Big Four: Tyson, Cargill, JBS and National Beef.</a></p>

<p><a href="https://join.switchtoamerica.com/post/is-store-bought-ground-beef-real-beef">What goes into store ground beef is a whole other post.</a> This one is about who stands between the ranch and your fork.</p>

<h2 id="the-other-side">The case against my own story</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: three fair points, and I will not pretend they are wrong.</p>

<p><strong>One.</strong> This is not new. <a href="https://www.ers.usda.gov/amber-waves/2024/january/concentration-in-u-s-meatpacking-industry-and-how-it-affects-competition-and-cattle-prices">USDA says the top four already bought 81 percent of steers and heifers by 1995.</a></p>

<p><strong>Two.</strong> A settlement is not a guilty verdict. The companies denied the claims they paid to settle.</p>

<p><strong>Three.</strong> Store beef still has protein. Three ounces gives your dad about the same grams either way.</p>

<p>So why does it still sit wrong with me?</p>

<p>Because four companies buy most of the cattle. And there is no one to call and ask how your dad's dinner was raised.</p>

<img src="https://scan.ismyhometoxic.com/blog-images/howmuchprot-belonging-v1.webp" alt="A woman talking with a rancher at a wooden fence line, cattle grazing on open pasture behind them in late afternoon light" loading="lazy" style="width:100%;height:auto;border-radius:10px;">

<h2 id="what-do-i-do">What do I do instead?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: find a ranch you can talk to, and get to know the rancher.</p>

<p>Find a farm or ranch near you. Go meet the rancher. Ask how the cattle are raised and where they are processed. Then listen to how the answer sounds.</p>

<p>If there is no ranch near you, there is the ranch I work with, <a href="https://RiverbendRanchSteaks.com/eatbeefwithshan">Riverbend Ranch</a>.</p>

<p>The pack in the meat case rarely tells you who raised the animal. A rancher can.</p>

<h3>What do the grams look like on a plate?</h3>

<p><a href="https://www.nal.usda.gov/sites/default/files/page-files/Protein.pdf">USDA lists 3 ounces of cooked 90 percent lean ground beef at about 22 grams of protein.</a> One large egg has about 6.</p>

<p>For a 150 pound mom, the PROT-AGE number starts at about 68 grams. One 3 ounce serving of beef covers about a third of that.</p>

<p>Do the small thing first. Tonight, write your mom's weight on a sticky note and do the math once.</p>

<h2 id="faq">What else do people ask?</h2>

<p><strong>Is the higher number for me or for my mom?</strong> PROT-AGE wrote its number for people over 65, so that one is for your mom. The 2026 US guidelines set 1.2 to 1.6 grams per kilogram for all adults. That is about 0.54 to 0.73 grams per pound. The US minimum stays at 0.8 grams per kilogram, about 0.36 per pound.</p>

<p><strong>Can my dad eat more protein if he has kidney disease?</strong> Not without his kidney doctor. PROT-AGE says people with severe kidney disease who are not on dialysis may need to limit protein.</p>

<p><strong>Is store beef unsafe to eat?</strong> No receipt in this post says so. What they show is how few companies buy the cattle, and that you never meet the people who raised them.</p>

<p><strong>How do I find a rancher near me?</strong> Ask neighbors and friends who buy beef by the half or the quarter. Then call the ranch and ask if you can come out and see it.</p>
