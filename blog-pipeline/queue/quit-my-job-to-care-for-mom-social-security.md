<h2 id="what-happens">What happens to your Social Security when you quit to care for Mom?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: nothing goes in for the years you are home, and a missing year can count as a zero.</p>

<p>You sit in your mother's kitchen at nine at night. Her pills are sorted for the week. Her soup pot is washed and upside down on the rack. Your work badge is still in your purse, from a job you left in the spring. And that thought comes back, the one you keep pushing away.</p>

<p>What happens to me later?</p>

<p>Here is the math. <a href="https://www.ssa.gov/pubs/EN-05-10070.pdf">Social Security's May 2026 booklet</a> says it uses "your highest 35 years of earnings." It lines up your best years and averages them.</p>

<p>If you have fewer than 35 paid years, the unpaid ones still sit in that line of 35. Social Security says they "count as zeroes." And so every zero pulls the average down.</p>

<p>You did not do anything wrong. Your mother needed you, and you went.</p>

<p>The formula has no box for it. Nobody puts that on the form you sign when you leave.</p>

<h2 id="every-year">Does every year at home lower your check?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: no. It depends on how many paid years you already have, and one number tells you.</p>

<h3>If you already have 35 paid years</h3>

<p>Then your home years may never enter the math. Only the top 35 count. A year at home simply sits outside them.</p>

<h3>If you have fewer than 35</h3>

<p>This is where it stings. Social Security says that without 35 years, "your benefit amount will be lower than it would be if you worked 35 years."</p>

<p>Maybe you stayed home when your kids were small. Maybe you started late. Those years are already short. Now Mom's years land on the same pile.</p>

<h3>If you are still under 10 years</h3>

<p>You are probably past this one. But the same sheet says you "need at least 10 years of work, or 40 credits," to qualify at all.</p>

<p>So the number to find is simple. How many years have you been paid? That one count decides which of these three you are in.</p>

<h2 id="the-bill">Isn't there a bill to count caregiving?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: yes, two matching bills. Both were introduced in April 2026, and neither has passed.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/BILLS-119s4396is">Senate bill S. 4396</a> was introduced on April 27, 2026. <a href="https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/BILLS-119hr8490ih">House bill H.R. 8490</a> came on April 23.</p>

<p>The bill text would credit caregivers "for up to five years of such service." A month counts if you gave "not less than 80 hours" of unpaid care. Your mother counts if she needs help every day with at least two daily tasks. Bathing, dressing and eating are on the list.</p>

<p>Both bills sit in committee. A bill that is introduced is a proposal. It changes nothing on your record today.</p>

<p>And so, for now, your years at home are still empty years. Unless something else fills them.</p>

<p>Here is the trade side by side.</p>

<h2 id="this-week">What would I do first this week?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: count your paid years, check your record, and write down your care.</p>

<h3>Open your own earnings record</h3>

<p>Make a free my Social Security account at ssa.gov/myaccount. Social Security says you can "review your earnings record" there. Count the years with pay. Write the number down.</p>

<h3>If you have not left yet, ask about leave first</h3>

<p>The <a href="https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/fmla">Labor Department's leave page</a> covers this. Eligible workers can take up to 12 workweeks in a year "to care for the employee's spouse, child, or parent who has a serious health condition." It is unpaid, but your job is held. Ask your boss before you sign anything.</p>

<h3>Keep a notebook on her counter</h3>

<p>Write the dates and the hours. The baths, the meals, the rides to the doctor. If the bill ever passes, your hours are already written down.</p>

<h2 id="faq">What else are women asking me about this?</h2>

<p><strong>Does caring for my mom count toward Social Security?</strong></p>
<p>Not today. Unpaid care puts nothing on your record. Two bills from April 2026 would credit up to five years of care, but neither is law.</p>

<p><strong>Will a few years at home lower my check?</strong></p>
<p>Only if you end up with fewer than 35 paid years. Social Security uses your top 35, and missing years count as zeros. Count your paid years in your earnings record to know.</p>

<p><strong>Can I get unemployment if I quit to care for her?</strong></p>
<p>It depends on your state. Each state sets its own rules for quitting. Call your state's unemployment office and ask before you count on it.</p>

<p><strong>Should I quit or take leave first?</strong></p>
<p>If you qualify, ask about Family and Medical Leave first. It can hold your job for up to 12 workweeks while you care for a parent. The leave is unpaid.</p>

<p><strong>Does work of my own count toward Social Security?</strong></p>
<p>Yes, if you pay Social Security tax on it. Social Security counts all the pay you pay that tax on, not just a paycheck from a boss. Report all of it so it lands on your record.</p>
