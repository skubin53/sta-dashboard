<h2 id="what-happens">What happens to your money after a divorce at 50?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: it drops by close to half, and it drops harder for you than for him.</p>

<p>The money was his. Or it was theirs. The card was in his name. The retirement plan came from his job.</p>

<p>And so now you sit at the kitchen table with a pen, and you feel small.</p>

<p>Two researchers at Bowling Green State University measured this. I-Fen Lin and Susan Brown followed 590 people who divorced at 50 or older. <a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8599059/">Their paper, in a journal on aging, found women's standard of living fell 45%.</a> Men's fell 21%.</p>

<p>Standard of living means your income compared with what your home needs. Rent, food, the light bill.</p>

<p>Savings took a hit for both of them. The same paper found wealth fell by about half, for women and for men.</p>

<p>The government saw it too. <a href="https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-12-699">A 2012 government report</a> found women's household income fell 41% on average with divorce. That was for women near or in retirement.</p>

<h2 id="rigged">Why can the job hunt feel rigged after 55?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: the EEOC sued one company over it. The agency alleged its software turned away women 55 and older.</p>

<p>So you go looking for work you can do from home. You fill in the form. You type your real birthday.</p>

<p>Here is what one company did with that, by the government's account.</p>

<h3>The company: iTutorGroup</h3>

<p>iTutorGroup hired tutors in the United States to teach English online, from home, to students in China. In May 2022 the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission sued it in federal court in New York.</p>

<h3>The product: its hiring software</h3>

<p><a href="https://www.eeoc.gov/newsroom/itutorgroup-pay-365000-settle-eeoc-discriminatory-hiring-suit">The EEOC alleged the software was set to "automatically reject female applicants aged 55 or older."</a> For men the cutoff was 60. The agency says more than 200 qualified applicants in the United States were turned away for their age.</p>

<p><a href="https://clearinghouse.net/case/44258/">The Civil Rights Litigation Clearinghouse sums up the complaint.</a> By the complaint's account, it started with one applicant over 55. She applied with her real birthday and was rejected on the spot. She applied again with a later birthday and got an interview, the complaint says.</p>

<h3>The settlement</h3>

<p>The company paid $365,000 in 2023 to settle. That money went to the applicants the EEOC says were turned away. <a href="https://www.sullcrom.com/insights/blogs/2023/August/EEOC-Settles-First-AI-Discrimination-Lawsuit">It did not admit to any wrongdoing.</a></p>

<p>Read that again. You did nothing wrong. By the EEOC's account, a box on a screen decided before a person ever saw your name.</p>

<p>And so a job is a door somebody else holds. A husband was too.</p>

<h2 id="what-brought-it-back">What brought the money back for women?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: a new partner. For women who stayed single, the drop held at about 48%.</p>

<p>This is the part that stings. Lin and Brown found women's losses <a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8599059/">"only reversed for women following repartnering."</a> Women who did not find a new partner stayed down about 48%.</p>

<p>And most did not. <a href="https://www.cnbc.com/2024/03/23/why-gray-divorce-is-a-significant-financial-risk-for-women.html">CNBC reported in 2024 that only 22% of women found a new partner within ten years.</a> For men it was 37%. The same report says divorce after 50 doubled from 1990 to 2019.</p>

<p>So the research leans on a husband. Or a boss. Both can leave.</p>

<p>Does that sound like a plan to you? It does not to me.</p>

<h2 id="this-week">What would I do first this week?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: write down what has your name on it, then write down what you already do well.</p>

<h3>List what is in your name</h3>

<p>Get a sheet of paper. Write down every account, card and plan with your name on it. Just yours. If the list is short, that is not a failure. It is a starting line. Put it somewhere you can see it. The fear gets smaller once it lives on paper and not in your stomach.</p>

<h3>Write down what you already do well</h3>

<h2 id="faq">What else are women asking me about this?</h2>

<p><strong>Is it normal to feel this scared about money after a divorce at 50?</strong></p>
<p>Yes. The fear has a number on it. One study found women's standard of living fell 45% after a divorce at 50 or older, against 21% for men. You are not imagining the drop.</p>

<p><strong>Do I have to remarry to get back on my feet?</strong></p>
<p>No. The research found a new partner was the main thing that brought women's money back. But only 22% of women found one within ten years. You do not need a husband to start. You need something in your own name.</p>

<p><strong>Can a company turn me down because of my age?</strong></p>
<p>Federal law protects job applicants 40 and older. The EEOC used that law against iTutorGroup, alleging its software rejected women 55 and older. The company settled for $365,000 and did not admit wrongdoing.</p>

<p><strong>Is 52 too late to start something of my own?</strong></p>
<p>No. You already know how to run a house, keep a budget and look after people. Those are the hard parts. What is new is putting your own name on it.</p>
