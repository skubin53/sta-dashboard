<h2 id="what-cpsc-said">What did the CPSC say about the KH158?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: stop using it now. The agency counts 91 reports of these detectors staying quiet during a leak.</p>

<p>The furnace kicked on last month. Tonight the gas fireplace is going. That little white box by the stove is what you count on to speak up.</p>

<p>You stood at the counter longer than you meant to, waiting for its green light.</p>

<p>No shame in trusting it. It sold on Amazon for two years, under 18 names.</p>

<p>You wanted a warning before the air turned on you. That is a fair thing to want. The trouble, the CPSC says, is what the company sold you.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.cpsc.gov/Warnings/2026/CPSC-Warns-Consumers-to-Stop-Using-4-in-1-Plug-In-Natural-Gas-and-Carbon-Monoxide-Detectors-Immediately-Due-to-Failure-to-Alert-Consumers-to-Deadly-Carbon-Monoxide">The US Consumer Product Safety Commission posted warning 26-706 on August 20, 2026.</a> It says to "stop using and dispose of these products immediately."</p>

<p>The notice counts "91 reports of the detectors failing to alarm" during gas or carbon monoxide leaks.</p>

<p>The company is Shenzhen Kanghua Shengshi Industrial Co., LTD, which does business as KH Alert. The CPSC says it "has not agreed to recall these detectors."</p>

<p>And so the notice offers no refund. It covers 376,974 of them, sold for $13 to $140.</p>

<h2 id="check-yours">How do I know if mine is a KH158?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: one of 18 names on the box. On the front, a SELF-TEST button with three small lights to its left.</p>

<p>The CPSC says the KH158 is white, gray or black plastic with a digital display. A button marked SELF-TEST sits on the front.</p>

<p>Three lights sit to its left: green for POWER, yellow for FAULT and red for ALARM.</p>

<p>Then match the name on yours to the 18 below.</p>

<h2 id="plug-ins">Are plug-in carbon monoxide detectors reliable at all?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: not without a battery inside. The CPSC wants a battery or a battery backup "in case of a power outage."</p>

<p>A plug-in runs on the outlet. When an ice storm knocks the power out, one with no battery goes dark.</p>

<p>That can be the night you light the gas fireplace for heat.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.cpsc.gov/Newsroom/News-Releases/2025/Theres-a-Chill-in-the-Air-Stay-Warm-Safely-Be-Cautious-When-Using-Generators-Furnaces-and-Space-Heaters">A power outage is the reason the CPSC gives in its January 2025 winter notice.</a> It also names "furnaces and fireplaces" as "a potential source of CO."</p>

<p><a href="https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/carbon-monoxides-impact-indoor-air-quality">The EPA lists gas stoves as a source too.</a> It warns of "flu-like symptoms that clear up after leaving home." That is a headache at home that lifts once you step outside.</p>

<p>And your nose cannot warn you. <a href="https://www.cdc.gov/carbon-monoxide/about/index.html">The CDC calls carbon monoxide "an odorless, colorless gas that kills without warning."</a> It says more than 400 Americans die from it by accident each year, not counting fires.</p>

<p>And so the battery is the whole point. An alarm that keeps going in a blackout is the one that can wake you.</p>

<h3 id="ul-2034">Does any rule make a seller test it?</h3>

<p>The CPSC's warning says to "only buy carbon monoxide detectors that meet the UL 2034 safety standard."</p>

<p><a href="https://www.cpsc.gov/Regulations-Laws--Standards/Voluntary-Standards/Carbon-Monoxide-CO-Alarms">But the CPSC files UL 2034 under voluntary standards.</a> On that page, the line for federal rules says "N/A." So no federal rule makes the seller meet it.</p>

<h2 id="the-other-side">Is there a case for keeping your plug-in?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: two fair points, and neither one covers a KH158.</p>

<p><strong>First,</strong> the notice counts 91 reports against 376,974 sold. It lists no deaths and no injuries. That is the number, and it is a small one.</p>

<p><strong>Second,</strong> a plug-in with a battery backup keeps going in a blackout.</p>

<p>So why would I still tell you to unplug it today?</p>

<p>Because the CPSC said "immediately," and because you would never know it stayed quiet. Your nose gets nothing to go on.</p>

<p>You are far from the only one who trusted it. If your mother or your neighbor bought one too, check hers the same day.</p>

<h2 id="what-instead">What goes on the wall instead?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: a plain alarm with a battery inside. Put one on every level and outside the bedrooms.</p>

<p>Unplug the KH158 today. Then put up an alarm that runs on a battery or has a battery backup.</p>

<p>The CPSC says to place alarms "on every level of the home and outside sleeping areas." Linked alarms are best, it says, because "when one sounds, they all sound."</p>

<h2 id="faq">What else do people ask?</h2>

<p><strong>Are plug-in carbon monoxide detectors any good?</strong></p>
<p>Not a KH158, not one more night. Any other plug-in needs a battery backup, the CPSC says, in case of a power outage. If yours has none, add a battery alarm on that floor.</p>

<p><strong>My detector came from Amazon. Is it on the warning?</strong></p>
<p>Match the brand on your box or your order to the 18 names above. Then look for a SELF-TEST button with green, yellow and red lights to its left.</p>

<p><strong>Can I get my money back?</strong></p>
<p>The notice offers no refund, because the company has not agreed to a recall. Stop using it anyway. The CPSC takes defect reports at SaferProducts.gov.</p>
