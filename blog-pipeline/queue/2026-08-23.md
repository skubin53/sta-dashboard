<h2 id="the-short-answer">The short answer</h2>
<p>The FDA ran a year long sampling assignment on talc cosmetics and reported the outcome in plain arithmetic. <a href="https://www.fda.gov/food/cfsan-constituent-updates/fda-releases-data-agencys-year-long-sampling-assignment-test-talc-containing-cosmetic-products"><strong>Of 52 samples, 43 came back negative and 9 came back positive for asbestos.</strong></a></p>
<p>Congress responded by ordering the FDA to set a required, standardised test for asbestos in talc makeup.</p>
<p><a href="https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/11/28/2025-21407/testing-methods-for-detecting-and-identifying-asbestos-in-talc-containing-cosmetic-products"><strong>On November 28, 2025 the FDA withdrew that rule.</strong></a> There is still no required test.</p>

<h2 id="what-did-the-fda-find">What did the FDA actually find?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: nine positives out of fifty two, and the recalls followed within weeks.</p>

<p>Think about the compact in your bag. Not a theory, the actual one. The lid that does not shut right any more, the little sponge you have been meaning to replace, the pressed powder worn down in a crescent where your brush goes.</p>

<p>You have owned it for a while. It goes on your cheekbones every morning, and some of it goes into the air about eight inches from your face.</p>

<p>In September 2018 the FDA hired an accredited asbestos lab, bought talc cosmetics off ordinary shelves, and sent them in blind. <a href="https://www.fda.gov/food/cfsan-constituent-updates/fda-releases-data-agencys-year-long-sampling-assignment-test-talc-containing-cosmetic-products">The final report, released March 9, 2020, states it flatly: "The results showed that 43 samples were negative while 9 were positive."</a></p>

<p><strong>Nine products already sitting in shops, already in handbags, already on faces.</strong></p>

<p>Two microscopes were used, polarised light and transmission electron. Transmission electron microscopy is the most sensitive method there is for finding asbestos, which matters for a reason we will come back to further down.</p>

<h2 id="which-makeup-got-recalled">Which makeup got recalled?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: eye shadows, blushes, bronzers and palettes, across 2019, by name.</p>

<p>This is not a rumour with a company lawyer attached to it. <a href="https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetics-recalls-alerts/fda-advises-consumers-stop-using-certain-cosmetic-products">The FDA published the list itself and told people to stop using the products.</a></p>

<p>On March 5, 2019 the agency advised consumers not to use certain Claire's items after samples of the eye shadows, the compact powder and the contour palette tested positive for tremolite asbestos. In late May, Claire's and Beauty Plus Global both undertook voluntary recalls, covering the Beauty Plus Contour Effects Palette 2 and a Claire's JoJo Siwa makeup set.</p>

<p>Then on September 6, 2019 Beauty Plus Global voluntarily recalled four more: a City Color matte blush in Fuchsia, the City Color Timeless Beauty Palette, a City Color bronzer in Sunset, and a City Color shimmer bronzer in Caramel.</p>

<p>Every one of those is a product you press into your skin with a brush and then breathe near for the rest of the day. Not a niche item off the internet either. A blush, a bronzer, an eye shadow and a palette, sold in a shopping centre.</p>

<h2 id="why-is-it-in-there">Why would asbestos be in a face powder at all?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: because the two minerals grow in the same rock.</p>

<h3>Talc, hydrous magnesium silicate</h3>
<p><a href="https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetic-ingredients/talc">The FDA describes talc as a naturally occurring mineral mined from the earth, used in cosmetics to absorb moisture, prevent caking, make facial makeup opaque, and improve the feel of a product.</a> It is the bulk of most pressed powders. It is cheap, and it is why a powder glides.</p>

<h3>Asbestos, the neighbour in the seam</h3>
<p>The same FDA page states the problem in one line: both talc and asbestos are naturally occurring minerals that may be found in close proximity in the earth, and unlike talc, asbestos is a known carcinogen when inhaled. There is no chemical reaction here and no additive. It is geology. Mine talc from the wrong seam and asbestos comes up with it.</p>

<h3>Tremolite</h3>
<p>The specific form named in the Claire's findings. It is one of the amphibole asbestos minerals, and it is a common companion to talc deposits.</p>

<h3>Colour additives, the one part somebody checks</h3>
<p>Here is a detail almost nobody knows. <a href="https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetic-ingredients/talc">The FDA states that cosmetic products and ingredients, with the exception of color additives, do not have to undergo FDA review or approval before they go on the market.</a> The pigment that makes your bronzer bronze gets federal sign off. The mineral that makes up most of the tub does not.</p>

<h2 id="what-changed-in-2024">What changed at the cancer agency in 2024?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: talc moved up a category, and this time it was talc without any asbestos in it.</p>

<p><a href="https://monographs.iarc.who.int/news-events/volume-136-talc-and-acrylonitrile/">On 5 July 2024 the International Agency for Research on Cancer, the cancer arm of the World Health Organization, published its Volume 136 evaluation and classified talc as probably carcinogenic to humans, Group 2A.</a></p>

<p>The working group reached that on limited evidence for cancer in humans, sufficient evidence in experimental animals, and strong mechanistic evidence in human primary cells. Twenty nine experts from thirteen countries sat on it.</p>

<p><strong>Notice what that evaluation is about.</strong> It supersedes the older, narrower classifications and applies to talc itself. Talc containing asbestos was not re-evaluated at all, because it already sits in Group 1 as asbestos, the highest category the agency has.</p>

<p>So the mineral got a worse rating on its own merits, separate from the contamination question. Two problems, not one.</p>

<h2 id="why-is-there-no-test">So why is there still no required test?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: Congress ordered one, the FDA proposed one, and then the FDA pulled it.</p>

<p>This is the part that decides it for me, and it has nothing to do with mineralogy.</p>

<p>After the 2019 recalls, Congress wrote a requirement into the Modernization of Cosmetics Regulation Act of 2022. Section 3505 told the FDA to establish and require standardised testing methods for detecting asbestos in talc containing cosmetics. Not a suggestion. A statutory instruction.</p>

<p>The FDA published the proposed rule on December 27, 2024. Industry filed comments.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/11/28/2025-21407/testing-methods-for-detecting-and-identifying-asbestos-in-talc-containing-cosmetic-products">On November 28, 2025 the FDA withdrew it</a>, stating it was acting in response to comments received during the comment period that warrant further consideration and assessment.</p>

<p>Read that sequence again. A federal survey finds asbestos in nine products. Companies recall six of them by name. Congress orders a mandatory test. Four years later the rule to create that test is withdrawn after the industry writes in.</p>

<p>Nobody broke a law. That is what makes it worth your attention.</p>

<h2 id="is-my-compact-contaminated">Is my compact contaminated?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: probably not, and here is every reason to think so.</p>

<p>Now the case against my own argument, in full, because you deserve it before you throw anything away.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetics-news-events/fda-releases-data-agencys-2023-testing-talc-containing-cosmetic-products-asbestos">The FDA has kept testing every year, and asbestos was not detected in any of the 50 samples tested in 2023.</a> The same result came back for 2022 and for 2021. That is three straight years, 150 products, zero positives, all analysed with the most sensitive method available.</p>

<p>The agency was also honest about its own 2019 numbers. It said outright that products had been selected partly because third parties had reported them as possibly contaminated, and therefore the positives should not be interpreted as reflecting the overall frequency of positive samples in the marketplace. That was a targeted hunt, not a random sample.</p>

<p>And the IARC finding is a hazard classification, not a risk calculation. The human evidence it rested on was described as limited, and it came largely from studies of talc powder used in the genital area, not from pressed powder on a cheekbone.</p>

<p>All of that is true. I would rather you heard it from me than found it in a comment.</p>

<p>What none of it answers is the last four years. Congress ordered a required test, and there still is not one. Every reassuring result above came from a survey the agency runs because it chooses to, not because anything obliges it to.</p>

<h2 id="what-do-i-use-instead">What do I put on my face instead?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: powder that is not built on a mined mineral, from a company you can actually reach.</p>

<p>Talc is the filler, not the makeup. Powders exist that use other bases entirely, and they set and blur the same way. This is one of the easier swaps in the whole house, because you are not giving anything up.</p>

<p>I am not going to tell you to check the ingredient list, and the FDA's own record is why. Asbestos is a contaminant. It is never an ingredient, so it never appears in the fine print on any product that has it. The nine positives all had perfectly clean lists.</p>

<p>Which leaves you exactly one real question, and it is not about the powder. It is whether the company that mined it would tell you if something came back wrong.</p>

<p>That is why my family shops through a private membership store instead of a shelf. Not a nicer aisle. A different supply chain, with a name attached and a person who picks up the phone.</p>

<p>Start with the one you use every single day. The compact in your bag, not the drawer full of things you wear twice a year.</p>

<h2 id="faq">What else do people ask?</h2>

<p><strong>Is loose powder worse than pressed?</strong> Loose powder puts more of itself into the air around your face, so more of it is available to breathe. Pressed powder is bound into a cake and sheds less. That is about exposure, not about contamination, and a contaminated pressed powder is still contaminated.</p>

<p><strong>Does a higher price mean cleaner talc?</strong> No. The FDA has deliberately included both low end and high end products in its sampling every year, and price has never been the dividing line in the results.</p>

<p><strong>Is baby powder the same question?</strong> Related, and not identical. The litigation and most of the human research concern body powder used over years in the genital area. Face powder is a smaller quantity used differently, and the shared risk is the mineral supply, not the format.</p>

<p><strong>Was asbestos found in any recent FDA testing?</strong> No. Zero of 50 samples in 2021, zero of 50 in 2022, and zero of 50 in 2023. That record is real and this post is not going to hide it.</p>

<p><strong>If I already own a recalled product, what do I do?</strong> Those specific 2019 lots have been off shelves for years. Anything still in a drawer from that era goes in the bin rather than back on your face.</p>
