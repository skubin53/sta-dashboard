<h2 id="the-short-answer">The short answer</h2>
<p>Yes, if you work in Illinois. From January 1, 2027, the law says menopause counts.</p>
<p>Here is the short version. <strong><a href="https://www.ilga.gov/Documents/legislation/publicacts/104/104-0793.htm">Public Act 104-0793</a></strong> puts menopause into the state's civil rights law. It covers hot flashes, "sleep disruption" and "cognitive or mood changes."</p>
<p>When you ask, your boss has to make reasonable changes. Water breaks. A cooler desk. Hours that bend around a bad night's sleep. The only way out is for your boss to prove "undue hardship."</p>
<p>A law can get you a fan. It cannot give you back your own clock.</p>

<h2 id="what-changes">What does the Illinois menopause law change at work in 2027?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: menopause gets named in the state's civil rights law, and you get the right to ask for changes.</p>

<p>It is two in the afternoon. The heat climbs up your neck. Your shirt sticks to your back. And the meeting starts in five minutes.</p>

<p>The Governor signed House Bill 5284 on August 7, 2026. <a href="https://www.ilga.gov/Legislation/BillStatus?DocNum=5284&GAID=18&DocTypeID=HB&SessionID=114&GA=104">The state's own bill page shows the date.</a> The work part starts January 1, 2027.</p>

<p>The law tucks menopause inside the word "pregnancy" in that civil rights law. Every right a pregnant worker has to ask for changes, you now have too.</p>

<p>The law's own list of changes includes "breaks for increased water intake." Also a "temperature or climate-adjusted workspace." Also "flexible scheduling or modified work hours."</p>

<h2 id="who-it-covers">Which employers does the Illinois law cover?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: nearly all of them in Illinois, even a boss with one worker.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.ilga.gov/legislation/ILCS/details?ActID=2266&ChapterID=64&ChapAct=775+ILCS+5%2F&SeqStart=600000&SeqEnd=1900000">The state's Human Rights Act</a> counts "any person employing one or more employees." That is the rule for pregnancy claims. Menopause now sits inside that word. So a small shop counts.</p>

<p>Your boss can ask for a note from your doctor. The note only needs four things. The medical reason. The change advised. When it starts and how long.</p>

<p>Your boss cannot push you onto leave if another fix would work. And if your boss says no, your boss has to prove the change is too hard. The law puts that burden on the employer.</p>

<p>Your rights go on the wall poster too. From January 1, every Illinois boss must post "the right to reasonable accommodations for menopause-related conditions."</p>

<h2 id="the-firing">What happened in the 911 worker's perimenopause case?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: a federal judge said her firing was not sex discrimination. Illinois just closed that exact gap, for Illinois.</p>

<p>Alisha Coleman took 911 calls for the Bobby Dodd Institute in Georgia. She started in 2007. <a href="https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCOURTS-gamd-4_17-cv-00029/pdf/USCOURTS-gamd-4_17-cv-00029-0.pdf">The court's order sets out what her complaint alleged.</a></p>

<p>By her account, perimenopause brought sudden, heavy bleeding she could not predict. She kept supplies at work. She told her supervisors.</p>

<p>Her complaint says she leaked onto an office chair in August 2015 and was written up. In April 2016 it happened on the carpet. She cleaned it. Then, she says, the Institute fired her "for failing to maintain high standards of personal hygiene."</p>

<p>Almost nine years of calls. Two bad days.</p>

<p>On June 8, 2017, Chief Judge Clay Land threw her case out. His order says her bleeding "was related to pre-menopause, not pregnancy or childbirth." And so the federal law, as he read it, had no box for her.</p>

<p>She appealed. <a href="https://www.ems1.com/lawsuit/articles/dispatcher-settles-suit-after-claiming-she-was-fired-for-pre-menopause-symptoms-sjsrGwyZ08LGclvB/">EMS1 reported in November 2017</a> that she settled, with the terms kept private. The report carries no admission from the Institute.</p>

<h2 id="pushed-out">Is menopause really pushing women out of work?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: for about one in seven working women in a Mayo Clinic survey, something at work went wrong.</p>

<p>Mayo Clinic researchers surveyed women aged 45 to 60 at their clinics in 2021. 4,440 had jobs. <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.mayocp.2023.02.025">Their paper ran in Mayo Clinic Proceedings in 2023.</a></p>

<p>597 of them, 13.4%, said menopause symptoms hurt their work at least once. 480 missed work in the past year. For half of them, it was three days or more.</p>

<p>The worse the symptoms, the worse the odds. Those with the worst symptoms were far more likely to say it hurt their work.</p>

<p>Here is the weak spot. It was one survey, at one point in time. Only 16.1% of those asked wrote back. Most were White, married and college educated. So this is a strong pattern, not proof.</p>

<p>Then the money. <a href="https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2025/03/menopause-earnings-economics-study">A 2025 study led by Stanford economist Petra Persson</a> used data from Sweden and Norway. Women who saw a health provider for symptoms earned 10% less. That was four years later. Mostly they cut their hours or quit. Persson says the exact numbers may not carry over to the US.</p>

<p>You are not the only one. 597 women in one survey said it hurt their work too.</p>

<h2 id="monday">What would I do first on Monday?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: write down your worst hour, ask for the change in writing, and keep a copy.</p>

<h3>Write down your worst hour</h3>

<p>Take one week. Note when the heat hits, when you lose a word, and when sleep runs short. Then write the one change that would help most. A fan. A later start. A desk away from the window.</p>

<h3>Ask in an email after January 1</h3>

<p>The new right starts January 1, 2027. Ask in an email so there is a date on it. Name the change you want. Keep it short.</p>

<h3>Know who takes complaints</h3>

<p>Say your boss says no, and you think the law says yes. <a href="https://dhr.illinois.gov/filing-a-charge.html">The Illinois Department of Human Rights shows you how to complain.</a></p>

<p>And tell one friend who gets it. Laugh about the paper fan in your purse.</p>

<h2 id="faq">What else are women asking me about this?</h2>

<p><strong>When does the Illinois menopause law take effect?</strong></p>
<p>The work part starts January 1, 2027. The Governor signed it on August 7, 2026. A separate insurance part starts January 1, 2028.</p>

<p><strong>Does the Illinois law cover small businesses?</strong></p>
<p>Yes. The state's civil rights law reaches a boss with even one worker when the claim is about pregnancy. Menopause now sits inside that word.</p>

<p><strong>Can my boss make me take leave for menopause?</strong></p>
<p>Not if another fix would work. The law bars a boss from pushing you onto leave when a reasonable change would do.</p>

<p><strong>Does this law help me if I do not work in Illinois?</strong></p>
<p>Not directly. It covers work in Illinois. Your own state may have its own law, so look it up.</p>
