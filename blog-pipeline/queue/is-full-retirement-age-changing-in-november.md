<h2 id="the-short-answer">The short answer</h2>
<p>The age you get your full Social Security was picked in 1983, when you were a kid or not even born.</p>
<p>Here is the short version. That age does not change in November. Born on January 2, 1960 or later? It is 67. <strong><a href="https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-20/section-404.409">The federal rule's table says "1/2/1960 and later" gets "67 years."</a></strong></p>
<p>What could change is the name. On September 29, 2026, Congress passed a bill renaming it the "standard monthly benefit age." <a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/hr5284/BILLS-119hr5284enr.htm">It changes words, not ages.</a> It is not law yet.</p>
<p>A line set by law can be moved by law. What you build in your own name is yours.</p>

<h2 id="changing">Is full retirement age changing in November?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: no. The age stays where a 1983 law put it. Only the name may change, and that bill is not law yet.</p>

<p>You saw November in a headline. It said full retirement age hits 67 this November. So you did the math in your head and typed the question into your phone.</p>

<p>The Claiming Age Clarity Act passed the House in December 2025. The Senate passed it in September.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.congress.gov/119/bills/hr5284/BILLS-119hr5284enr.htm">The bill</a> swaps words. "Full retirement age" becomes the "standard monthly benefit age." Not one age moves. And so your 67 stays 67.</p>

<h2 id="your-month">When does your full age actually arrive?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: in the month you reach 67, if you were born on January 2, 1960 or later. Born in 1959 after January 1, it is 66 and 10 months.</p>

<p>Say you were born in March 1966. Add 67 years. Your month is March 2033.</p>

<p>One small trap sits in the rule. <a href="https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-20/section-404.102">You reach an age "on the day before your birthday."</a> So if you were born on the 1st, your month can be the one before. That is why the table puts January 1, 1960 in the 66 and 10 months row.</p>

<p>Born later in 1960? <a href="https://www.cbsnews.com/news/social-security-retirement-age-2026-change-66-67-increase/">CBS News put it plainly</a>: people born in 1960 "won't qualify for their full benefits until 2027, rather than 2026."</p>

<p>That 67 did not come from you. It came from a law.</p>

<h2 id="who-moved-it">Who moved the finish line from 65 to 67?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: a law signed on April 20, 1983. Its own first line says why, and the reason was money, not you.</p>

<p>Full age was 65 from the start, <a href="https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/IN/PDF/IN12630/IN12630.1.pdf">back in the 1930s</a>. Then came <a href="https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-97/pdf/STATUTE-97-Pg65.pdf">Public Law 98-21</a>, the Social Security Amendments of 1983.</p>

<p>Its first line says what it was for: "To assure the solvency of the Social Security Trust Funds." In plain words, the money was short. And so one fix was a later finish line.</p>

<p>Section 201, "Increase in retirement age," set 67 for anyone who turns 62 after December 31, 2021. That covers you. If you are 50 today, you were six or seven when it was signed.</p>

<p>It also made starting early cost more. Here is the math, from <a href="https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-20/section-404.410">the rule</a>. From 62 to 67 is 60 months. The first 36 months cost 20 percent. The next 24 cost 10 percent. Start at 62 and 30 percent is gone, for good.</p>

<p>When full age was 65, starting at 62 cost 20 percent. Same birthday, bigger cut, because a law moved the line.</p>

<h2 id="again">Could the finish line move again before you get there?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: it could. Social Security's own trustees see the money running short by 2034. One idea from Congress's budget office would make it 70 if you were born in 1981 or later.</p>

<p>In June, <a href="https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/IF/PDF/IF13256/IF13256.1.pdf">the Congressional Research Service summed up</a> the trustees' 2026 report on the money.</p>

<p>The trustees say both Social Security funds, counted together, run out in 2034. At that point, the taxes still coming in would pay about 83 percent of what was promised, and less in the years after.</p>

<p>The last time the money ran short, part of the answer was a later finish line.</p>

<p>In December 2024, the Congressional Budget Office priced moving it again. <a href="https://www.cbo.gov/budget-options/60913">Its option</a> adds two months per birth year for anyone born from 1964 to 1981. Born in 1981 or later, full age becomes 70.</p>

<p>The CBO says it "makes no recommendations," and an option is not a law. But it is plain about what a later age does. It "would reduce scheduled lifetime benefits for every affected Social Security recipient."</p>

<p>And so the year you circled is a year the next law can rewrite. Nobody has to ask you first.</p>

<h2 id="faq">What else are women asking me about this?</h2>

<p><strong>Will the new name change my check?</strong></p>
<p>No. The bill only changes words. Full retirement age becomes the standard monthly benefit age. Your age and your check stay the same.</p>

<p><strong>I was born on January 1, 1960. Is my age 67?</strong></p>
<p>No, it is 66 and 10 months. The rule says you reach each age the day before your birthday. So a January 1 birthday lands with the year before.</p>

<p><strong>Is full retirement age going up to 69 or 70?</strong></p>
<p>Not under the law today. <a href="https://www.cbo.gov/publication/60516">A September 2024 CBO report</a> looked at 69. A December 2024 CBO option would make it 70. Neither one is law.</p>
