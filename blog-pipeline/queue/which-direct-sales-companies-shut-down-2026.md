<h2 id="no-warning">Why did nobody get a warning?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: the news goes out the day it is already done.</p>

<p>Read the Sipology letter and you can feel it. The founder called it one of the hardest messages they had ever written. Then this line. "Given the company's financial position, we are unable to process commission payments."</p>

<p>Online ordering stopped that same day. No new orders. No replies to questions.</p>

<p>Modere did the same thing in April 2025. A warm note on the website, thanking everybody, after 23 years. Epicure went in January 2025, in a letter, closing at once.</p>

<p>These are not cruel messages. They are kind. And the kindness is exactly the problem, because a kind message on the morning it happens is still a message on the morning it happens.</p>

<p>You cannot plan around that. Nobody can.</p>

<h2 id="the-money">How much of it was ever real money?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: for most of the field at one company, none at all.</p>

<p>This part is on the public record. In April 2026 the Federal Trade Commission filed a complaint against Forever Living, its chief executive and its president, in federal court in Arizona.</p>

<p>The complaint alleged the company drew people in with earnings claims it could not back up. Here is what it says the company's own data showed.</p>

<p>In each of the last five years, at least 77 out of every 100 people who bought, sold or recruited got paid nothing at all.</p>

<p>After two full years in, more than 89 out of 100 had still not made back what it cost them to start.</p>

<p>Fewer than 7 out of 100 ever got a penny from the people they brought in.</p>

<p>The company settled. Under that order it now has to be able to prove any earnings claim it makes, and hand the proof over if somebody asks. Those are allegations the company resolved, not findings after a trial.</p>

<p>Sit with the first number a second. Seventy seven out of a hundred. You were not bad at this. Almost nobody was allowed to be good at it.</p>

<h2 id="checklist">How do you check the next one before you give it two years?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: six questions, asked out loud, before you sign a thing.</p>

<p>This is the part to bookmark. None of it is clever. It is just the stuff nobody hands you at the start.</p>

<h3>Who owns the customer</h3>

<p>Ask where her record lives. Not her house. Her account. If it sits in a system you cannot export, you are renting her. You could be the best in the company and still not reach her next Tuesday.</p>

<h3>Who makes what you sell</h3>

<p>Ask who owns the plant. A company that only buys and rebrands can lose its maker, its price or its formula in a week. A company that makes its own can't.</p>

<h3>How long it has been here</h3>

<p>Ask for the year it started, then ask what it did in 2008 and in 2020. Age alone is not enough, and this list proves it. But a company that traded through two bad years has shown you something.</p>

<h3>Whether the pay has changed before</h3>

<p>Ask straight out. Have you ever changed the pay plan, and when. Forever Living froze or cut several bonus programs on the way out. BODi swapped its whole plan in five weeks. A company that has done it once will do it again.</p>

<h3>Whether your money survives if you stop recruiting</h3>

<p>This is the one I care about most. If the money only comes from bringing new people in, you are on a treadmill and the treadmill never slows down. If it comes from customers reordering, you can be ill for a month.</p>

<h3>What it costs you, all in</h3>

<p>One number. In writing. Start up fee, first order, monthly minimum, event tickets, samples, the lot. If you get a range instead of a number, that is your answer.</p>

<h2 id="is-this-a-pitch">Isn't this just another pitch?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: partly, and I will tell you which parts are the same.</p>

<p>Yes, I want to talk to you. So let me be straight about what I cannot promise.</p>

<p><strong>Switch to America could close too.</strong> Any company can. I would be lying if I said otherwise. Walk away from anybody who tells you different.</p>

<p>What I will do is answer the six questions first, without being asked.</p>

<p>We are the manufacturer. Over five hundred products, made by us. No middlemen and no markup.</p>

<p>There is no monthly minimum. No month where you order things you do not need to stay active. No stock in your spare room. Nothing ships through you.</p>

<p>You pay what a brand new customer pays. Not a penny less. I have no special rate and I would not offer you one. Anybody who discounts you at the start is buying a customer, not building a partner.</p>

<p>And there is no list of ten people you know. I do not teach that and I do not have one myself.</p>

<h2 id="faq">Questions women are asking me about this</h2>

<p><strong>Is direct sales dying?</strong></p>
<p>No. Some companies are, and the weak ones are going first. What is dying is the version where you carry the stock, the company holds the customer, and the pay plan can change on a Tuesday. Read the list again. Every one of those is a structure problem, not a product problem.</p>

<p><strong>Will I get the commissions I am owed?</strong></p>
<p>I do not know, and anybody who says they do is guessing. Write down the figure and the dates. Keep every message they sent you. Then ask somebody qualified in your own state. Do not leave it unrecorded because it feels embarrassing. You earned it.</p>

<p><strong>How old should a company be before I trust it?</strong></p>
<p>Older helps and it is not enough on its own. Epicure was 32. Modere was 23. Avon is 140 and has changed owner twice in ten years. Age tells you it can survive. The six questions tell you what happens to you if it does not.</p>

<p><strong>What if I do not want to recruit anybody ever again?</strong></p>
<p>Then don't. That is a fair answer and it is a more common one than you think. Ask question five and take the answer seriously. If your money dies the day you stop recruiting, that is a job with no sick pay, not a business.</p>
