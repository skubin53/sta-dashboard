<h2 id="what-got-recalled">What exactly got recalled?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: the purple one. And the lemon one. And three more.</p>

<p>You know the bottle. Tall. Purple. Sweet smelling.</p>

<p>It has been under your sink for years.</p>

<p>In February 2023 the government put out a notice.</p>

<p>The recall covered five scents. Lavender. Lemon. Passion of Fruits. Spring Fresh. Ocean.</p>

<p>It covered every size. The small bottle. The big jug. The gallon.</p>

<p>Here is where it sold, in the notice's own words. "Dollar General, Family Dollar, The Home Depot, Sam's Club, Walmart, and other major retailers nationwide."</p>

<p>The price? "Between $1 and $11."</p>

<p>So this was not some odd brand. It is the bottle everybody buys.</p>

<h2 id="what-was-in-it">What was growing in it?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: a germ that lives in soil and water.</p>

<p>A germ. The notice gives it a long name. Say it how you like.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.cpsc.gov/Recalls/2023/Colgate-Palmolive-Recalls-Fabuloso-Multi-Purpose-Cleaners-Due-to-Risk-of-Exposure-to-Bacteria">Here is the exact wording: the bottles "can contain Pseudomonas species bacteria, including Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Pseudomonas fluorescens, which are environmental organisms found widely in soil and water."</a></p>

<p>Now the part about who it can hurt.</p>

<p>The notice says people "with weakened immune systems, external medical devices, or underlying lung conditions" face "a risk of serious infection that may require medical treatment."</p>

<p>It says the germ "can enter the body if inhaled, through the eyes, or through a break in the skin."</p>

<p>Now think about mopping. It goes in the air. It goes on your hands.</p>

<p>But here is the line I want you to keep. It is in the part that tells you what to do with the bottle.</p>

<p><strong>"Do not empty the product prior to disposal."</strong></p>

<p>Read it slowly. You could not pour your own floor cleaner down your own drain.</p>

<p>You had to bin the whole thing with the lid on.</p>

<h2 id="why-not-kill-germs">Why does a cleaner not have to kill germs?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: because cleaning and killing are two different jobs in law.</p>

<p>Nobody explains this bit, so here it is.</p>

<p>If a product says it kills germs, it has to prove it. It signs up with the EPA as a pesticide.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.epa.gov/pesticide-registration/what-are-antimicrobial-pesticides">The EPA calls those antimicrobial pesticides.</a> Its words: things "used to destroy or suppress the growth of harmful microorganisms such as bacteria, viruses, or fungi on inanimate objects and surfaces."</p>

<p>More than 4,000 of those are signed up.</p>

<p>A plain cleaner is not one of them.</p>

<p><strong>It is sold to lift dirt. Not to kill anything.</strong></p>

<p>The notice shows that split. It says: "No Fabuloso Antibacterial variants or other Fabuloso products are impacted by this recall."</p>

<p>The germ killing one was fine. The plain one was not.</p>

<p>So the purple smell was never proof. It was a smell.</p>

<h2 id="the-other-side">The case against my own story</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: three things cut against me, and they are all in the notice.</p>

<p>Here are the strongest points on their side.</p>

<p><strong>One.</strong> Nobody got hurt. Under Injuries the notice says two words. "None reported."</p>

<p><strong>Two.</strong> Most people were never at risk. The notice says it plainly: "People with healthy immune systems are usually not affected by the bacteria."</p>

<p><strong>Three.</strong> They found it and they said so. They pulled it, offered a refund, and put up a website. That is a company doing the right thing. I will not pretend it is not.</p>

<p>So why does it still sit wrong with me?</p>

<p>Because of what you were sold. You bought it to make your home clean.</p>

<p>For a few weeks in early 2023, some of those bottles were the dirtiest thing in the room.</p>

<p>And you could not have known. Nothing on the front tells you what is living inside.</p>

<h2 id="what-do-i-use">What do I keep in the house instead?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: one cleaner, from someone who will pick up the phone.</p>

<p>Start with the floor. It is the biggest surface you have.</p>

<p>You do not need six bottles. One good cleaner and a couple of cloths.</p>

<p>I am not going to send you to the shelf to read the back of a bottle.</p>

<p>The 2023 recall is why that will not save you. Nothing on that label said germs.</p>

<p>The label was right. The bottle was not.</p>

<p>One state did make them list more. <a href="https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billTextClient.xhtml?bill_id=201720180SB258">California has a law for it.</a> Makers must list what is in a cleaner, on the label and online.</p>

<p>Good law. Now read the next bit. It also lets them "protect certain chemicals from disclosure by use of a generic name."</p>

<p>So the best label rule in the country still has a door in it.</p>

<p>So the only question left is who made it, and whether they would tell you fast when something went wrong.</p>

<p>That is why my family buys through a private membership store instead. We are the manufacturer. No middleman, no markup. One name on it, and a person who picks up the phone.</p>

<h2 id="faq">What else do people ask?</h2>

<p><strong>Is Fabuloso safe to use now?</strong> The recall covered set lot codes made between December 2022 and January 2023. Those are long gone. This is a fact about how the company works, not a warning about the bottle you bought last week.</p>

<p><strong>How would I have known?</strong> By the lot code. The notice says it is on the back, above the label. Nothing on the front would tell you.</p>

<p><strong>Does a cleaner have to kill germs?</strong> No. Only one that says it kills germs has to sign up with the EPA and prove it. A plain cleaner is sold to lift dirt.</p>

<p><strong>Did anybody get sick?</strong> The notice says "None reported" under injuries. I will not claim more than that.</p>
