<h2>Does "Product of USA" mean American beef?</h2>
<p>It does now. It did not before this year. Until the compliance date of <strong>January 1, 2026</strong>, USDA policy allowed meat that was merely processed in the United States to carry a "Product of USA" claim even if the animal was born, raised and slaughtered somewhere else.</p>
<p>USDA's own survey found that <a href="https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2024-03-18/html/2024-05479.htm">only 16 percent of shoppers understood what that label meant, while about 56 percent believed the animal was at least raised and slaughtered here</a>. And the reason your beef has no country on it at all is not the packers. It was an act of Congress in December 2015.</p>

<p>Here is something you can test in ninety seconds next time you shop. Pick up a package of lamb. It tells you what country it came from, because the law requires it. Pick up chicken. Same. Now pick up a steak.</p>

<p>Nothing. There is a reason, and it is not the one you have been told.</p>

<h2 id="why">Why does your steak not say where it came from?</h2>

<p><span class="quick-answer">Quick answer:</span> beef and pork were removed from mandatory country-of-origin labeling in early 2016 and have never been put back.</p>

<p>USDA's final rule states it plainly. It <a href="https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2016-03-02/html/2016-04609.htm">"amends the Country of Origin Labeling (COOL) regulations to remove muscle cut beef and pork, and ground beef and pork from mandatory COOL requirements."</a> It took effect March 2, 2016.</p>

<h2 id="who">Who actually deleted country-of-origin labeling for beef?</h2>

<p><span class="quick-answer">Quick answer:</span> Congress did, and I am telling you that because most articles get it wrong.</p>

<p>The repeal is Section 759 of the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2016, signed December 18, 2015. USDA's rule was a conforming cleanup with no discretion in it, which is why the agency skipped the usual notice and comment. The Government Accountability Office reviewed that rule and <a href="https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-16-518r">found USDA complied with the applicable requirements</a>.</p>

<p>So if somebody tells you meatpackers quietly erased the label, they have the mechanism wrong. It was a law, passed in the open, in a spending bill.</p>

<h2 id="wto">What did the WTO have to do with your hamburger?</h2>

<p><span class="quick-answer">Quick answer:</span> Canada and Mexico won the right to hit about a billion dollars a year of American exports, and Congress folded.</p>

<p>The same USDA rule recites it: <a href="https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2016-03-02/html/2016-04609.htm">"On December 7, 2015, the World Trade Organization ('WTO') Arbitrators set the maximum permissible levels of suspension of concessions at Canadian $1.05 billion (US $781 million) annually for Canada and US $228 million annually for Mexico."</a> Authorization was granted December 21, 2015.</p>

<p>Congress repealed COOL for beef eleven days after the arbitrators ruled. Here is the part that should bother you: the WTO objected to how the American scheme treated imported livestock in practice, not to telling shoppers where food came from. Congress could have redesigned it. It deleted it instead.</p>

<h2 id="which">Which meats still have to tell you where they came from?</h2>

<p><span class="quick-answer">Quick answer:</span> nearly everything except beef and pork.</p>

<p>Under <a href="https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-7/subtitle-B/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-65/subpart-A/section-65.135">7 CFR 65.135</a>, covered commodities include lamb, chicken and goat, plus fish and shellfish, fresh produce, peanuts, pecans, macadamia nuts and ginseng. Beef and pork are simply not on the list.</p>

<p>Two limits worth knowing. Restaurants and delis were never covered. And a covered commodity stops being covered once it becomes an ingredient in a processed food.</p>

<h2 id="meant">What did "Product of USA" legally mean before 2026?</h2>

<p><span class="quick-answer">Quick answer:</span> almost nothing. Processing here was enough.</p>

<p>Under the old USDA policy, meat that was only minimally processed in the United States could carry a "Product of USA" claim, even if the animal was raised and slaughtered abroad. That was policy, not an accident.</p>

<p>Then USDA commissioned research on what shoppers thought it meant. In a survey by RTI International, cited inside <a href="https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2024-03-18/html/2024-05479.htm">USDA's own 2024 rule</a>, only 16 percent understood the claim correctly. About 56 percent believed the animal had at least been raised and slaughtered in the United States. USDA concluded the claim was misleading to a majority of consumers.</p>

<p>To be fair, an industry commenter pushed back that only about 31 percent of shoppers noticed the label at all. That is a defense which amounts to saying the misleading label did not mislead many people, because most never read it.</p>

<h2 id="changed">What changed on January 1, 2026?</h2>

<p><span class="quick-answer">Quick answer:</span> the claim finally means what you always thought it meant, and it is still optional.</p>

<p>USDA's final rule, effective May 17, 2024 with a compliance date of January 1, 2026, requires that a voluntary "Product of USA" or "Made in the USA" claim on meat be used only for animals <strong>born, raised, slaughtered and processed</strong> in the United States, with supporting documentation on file.</p>

<p>Two catches. It is voluntary, so plenty of genuinely American beef will not carry it. And packages printed before the compliance date can lawfully stay in commerce, so old labels are still out there.</p>

<h2 id="who-owns">Who actually owns the beef supply?</h2>

<p><span class="quick-answer">Quick answer:</span> four companies do most of it, and they have been sued over pricing, though none has been found liable.</p>

<p>USDA reported to Congress that the four largest packers accounted for <a href="https://www.ams.usda.gov/sites/default/files/media/PackersandStockyards2021_2022ReporttoCongress.pdf">81 percent of total US steer and heifer slaughter in 2020 and 2021</a>. In fairness, USDA calls that the lowest level in a decade, down from 85 percent in 2019.</p>

<p>The litigation is real and it is about money, not safety. In 2008, DOJ and thirteen state attorneys general sued to block JBS from buying National Beef, alleging the deal would put <a href="https://www.justice.gov/atr/case/us-and-plaintiff-states-v-jbs-sa-and-national-beef-packing-company-llc">"more than 80 percent of domestic fed cattle packing capacity in the hands of three firms."</a> No court ruled; the parties abandoned the merger. In 2022, JBS paid $52.5 million to settle price-fixing claims without admitting liability.</p>

<h3>The receipts</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Allegation, never adjudicated.</strong> <em>United States and Plaintiff States v. JBS S.A. and National Beef Packing Company</em>, filed October 2008. DOJ and thirteen states alleged the merger would concentrate more than 80 percent of fed cattle packing capacity in three firms. <a href="https://www.justice.gov/atr/case/us-and-plaintiff-states-v-jbs-sa-and-national-beef-packing-company-llc">No court ruled and the merger was abandoned.</a></li>
<li><strong>Settled, with no admission of liability.</strong> In February 2022 JBS agreed to pay $52.5 million to resolve claims that it conspired with three rivals to cut slaughter volumes and raise beef prices, as <a href="https://www.ams.usda.gov/sites/default/files/media/PackersandStockyards2021_2022ReporttoCongress.pdf">recorded in USDA's Report to Congress</a>. JBS admitted nothing and no court has found any packer fixed beef prices.</li>
<li><strong>Final rule, in force.</strong> USDA's 2024 "Product of USA" rule closed the processing loophole, effective May 17, 2024, compliance January 1, 2026. <a href="https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2024-03-18/html/2024-05479.htm">89 FR 19470.</a></li>
<li><strong>Cuts against the story.</strong> GAO reviewed USDA's COOL repeal rule and found the agency complied with applicable requirements. There is no finding of agency misconduct here, and none of these origin or antitrust records says anything at all about whether beef is safe to eat.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Shannon's Advice</h3>
<p class="advice-tag">Straight talk from a nutrition coach</p>
<p>I have bought my beef from people I can call on the phone for most of my adult life, and until I went digging for this I did not know the label had been repealed by Congress. That is the part that gets me. It was not hidden. It was in a spending bill, in public, and none of us noticed.</p>
<p>My opinion, stated as mine: a country that will delete the one line telling a mother where her family's food came from, because a trade panel made it expensive to keep, has decided something about who it works for. The <a href="https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2024-03-18/html/2024-05479.htm">16 percent number</a> is not a marketing failure. It is what it looks like when a label is written to be technically true.</p>
<p>Here is what to do instead of squinting at packages. Find a rancher inside an hour of your house and go meet him. Ask where the animals were born, what they eat, and who processes them. He will tell you, because he is proud of it. That is an answer no label can give you and no Congress can repeal.</p>

<h2 id="faq">What else do people ask?</h2>

<p><strong>Why does beef have no country-of-origin label anymore?</strong><br>
Congress repealed mandatory country-of-origin labeling for beef and pork in the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2016, signed December 18, 2015, after WTO arbitrators authorized Canada and Mexico to retaliate against roughly a billion dollars a year of American exports. USDA's rule removing beef took effect March 2, 2016.</p>

<p><strong>Does "Product of USA" mean the cow was born and raised here?</strong><br>
It does under the current rule. Before the January 1, 2026 compliance date, meat only minimally processed in the United States could carry the claim. The claim is still voluntary, so plenty of American beef will not carry it.</p>

<p><strong>Did shoppers understand the old label?</strong><br>
No. In a survey conducted for USDA, only 16 percent understood it correctly and about 56 percent believed the animal was at least raised and slaughtered here. USDA cited that when it rewrote the rule.</p>

<p><strong>Which meats still have to say where they came from?</strong><br>
Lamb, chicken and goat, plus fish and shellfish, fresh produce, peanuts, pecans, macadamia nuts and ginseng. Restaurants and delis were never covered, and covered commodities drop out once they become an ingredient in a processed food.</p>

<p><strong>Do four companies really control American beef?</strong><br>
USDA reported that the four largest packers handled 81 percent of steer and heifer slaughter in 2020 and 2021. USDA also called that a decade low, down from 85 percent in 2019. None of it is a statement about whether the beef is safe.</p>
