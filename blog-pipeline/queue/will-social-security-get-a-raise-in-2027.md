<h2 id="the-short-answer">The short answer</h2>
<p>Yes. A raise is coming in January, and nobody will ask you how big it should be.</p>
<p>A formula in the law sets it. The last piece lands on <strong>October 14 at 8:30 in the morning, Eastern time</strong>. That is when the <a href="https://www.bls.gov/schedule/news_release/cpi.htm">Bureau of Labor Statistics puts out September prices</a>.</p>
<p>So far, <a href="https://data.bls.gov/timeseries/CWUR0000SA0">July and August prices ran about 3.5 percent above last summer</a>.</p>
<p>Then Medicare's Part B comes out of the same check. The <a href="https://www.cms.gov/oact/tr/2026">2026 Medicare Trustees Report</a> estimates it at <strong>$209.50 a month in 2027</strong>, up from $202.90.</p>
<p>A raise you did not set, with a bill you did not set right behind it. That is the whole post. The rest is the proof.</p>

<h2 id="who-decides">Who decides how big your raise is?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: a formula in the Social Security Act. Not you, and not anyone you can call.</p>

<p>Every December a one-page letter comes. It shows the new amount, and what comes out of it.</p>

<p>Maybe it comes to your mailbox. Maybe it comes to your mother's, and she slides it across her kitchen table to you.</p>

<p>Nobody asked either of you what it should say. <a href="https://www.ssa.gov/oact/cola/latestCOLA.html">Social Security's own page</a> puts it plainly: "The Social Security Act specifies a formula for determining each COLA." COLA is their word for the yearly raise.</p>

<p>The formula is short. Take average prices for July, August and September. Compare them with the same three months last year. The gap, rounded, is your raise.</p>

<p>And so it can be nothing at all. The same page says so: "If there is no increase, or if the rounded increase is zero, there is no COLA for the year."</p>

<p>That has happened. <a href="https://www.ssa.gov/oact/cola/colaseries.html">Social Security's record shows a zero for 2009, 2010 and 2015.</a> Each one meant no raise the next January. In a year like that, the formula owes you nothing.</p>

<h2 id="how-big">How big will the 2027 raise be?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: about 3.4 to 3.5 percent so far, and September decides the rest.</p>

<p>Two of the three months are already in. Here is the math, from the government's own price series.</p>

<p><a href="https://data.bls.gov/timeseries/CWUR0000SA0">The index the formula uses</a> averaged 327.79 across July and August this year. The same two months last year averaged 316.83. That is about 3.5 percent higher.</p>

<p>The raise for 2026 was <a href="https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/USSSA/bulletins/3f83cb8">2.8 percent</a>. The one before was 2.5.</p>

<p>I will not guess the last decimal. September can nudge it either way, and nobody honest knows until 8:30 on the 14th. Part of it is already spoken for.</p>

<h2 id="medicare">How much of the raise does Medicare take back?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: Part B comes out of the check first, and it is estimated to go up again.</p>

<p>If you are on Medicare, the Part B premium usually comes straight out of the Social Security check. You never touch that money.</p>

<p>Look at this year. The raise was 2.8 percent. <a href="https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/fact-sheets/2026-medicare-parts-b-premiums-deductibles">Part B went from $185.00 to $202.90 a month</a>. That is $17.90 more, close to a 10 percent jump.</p>

<p>Now 2027. <a href="https://www.cms.gov/oact/tr/2026">The Medicare Trustees Report, dated June 9, 2026</a>, says it in one line: "The estimated monthly premium for 2027 is $209.50."</p>

<p>If that estimate holds, that is $6.60 more, gone before the raise ever reaches you. Medicare sets the final number later in the fall. Last year it came out November 14.</p>

<p>So you get a raise, and a bill moves in right behind it. Nobody asks you about either one, and the raise is not even measured on your shopping.</p>

<h2 id="whose-prices">Whose shopping sets your raise?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: working households in cities. Not retirees.</p>

<p>The law ties your raise to one price index. <a href="https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/USSSA/bulletins/3f83cb8">Social Security's own announcement</a> names it as the one for "Urban Wage Earners and Clerical Workers."</p>

<p>The Bureau of Labor Statistics keeps a separate index for people 62 and older. <a href="https://www.bls.gov/cpi/research-series/r-cpi-e-home.htm">Its own page says</a> official use of it has been "considered by other government agencies, but not implemented." It blames limits in how the index is built.</p>

<p>And so the raise you live on is measured on somebody else's cart.</p>

<p>A formula sizes the check. Medicare sizes the bill. Neither one asks you. That is what October 14 shows you.</p>

<h2 id="is-this-a-pitch">Isn't this just another pitch?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: partly, and here is the part I will not dress up.</p>

<p>Social Security is not a monster. <a href="https://www.ssa.gov/oact/cola/latestCOLA.html">Its own page says</a> the raise exists so benefits "keep pace with inflation."</p>

<p>The estimated Part B jump for 2027 is also much smaller than last year's. And for about 70 percent of people on Part B, <a href="https://www.cms.gov/oact/tr/2026">the law caps it</a>. Their premium cannot rise by more dollars than their check. I will say both of those plainly.</p>

<p>I am not asking you to give up a single dollar of it. It is a floor. A floor is just not a plan you get to write.</p>

<h2 id="faq">What else are women asking me about this?</h2>

<p><strong>When will I know my exact amount for 2027?</strong></p>
<p>The percentage comes out October 14. Last year, Social Security started mailing the one-page letters in early December. With an online Social Security account, you can see yours faster than the mail.</p>

<p><strong>Can Medicare take my whole raise?</strong></p>
<p>For about 70 percent of people on Part B, no. A rule in the law stops the premium from rising by more dollars than the check rises. It does not cover people new to Part B, people with high incomes, or anyone whose premium is not taken out of a Social Security check.</p>

<p><strong>Do I have to give anything up to build something?</strong></p>
<p>No. Nobody is asking you to touch your Social Security. Whatever you build sits beside it, not instead of it.</p>
