<h2 id="the-short-answer">The short answer</h2>
<p>Anne Steinemann, a professor of civil and environmental engineering at the University of Washington, put instruments on residential dryer vents and measured what came out while scented laundry products were running.</p>
<p><strong><a href="https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/08/110824091537.htm">More than 25 volatile organic compounds. Seven of them classified as hazardous air pollutants. Two of them, acetaldehyde and benzene, classified by the EPA as carcinogens.</a></strong> The work was published in the journal Air Quality, Atmosphere and Health.</p>
<p>A dryer sheet works by melting a coating onto your clothes. That is the entire mechanism. Whatever is in the sheet is designed to end up on the fabric that sits against your skin for the next sixteen hours, and on the pillowcase your face is on all night.</p>

<h2 id="how-does-a-dryer-sheet-actually-work">How does a dryer sheet actually work?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: it is a piece of polyester coated in wax and softening chemicals that melt in the heat and transfer onto everything in the drum.</p>

<p>Static happens because tumbling fabric picks up an electrical charge. A dryer sheet fixes that by coating each fibre with a thin greasy film so the charge cannot build. Softness works the same way. Nothing about the fabric changes. It is lubricated.</p>

<p>So the sheet is a delivery system. Heat melts the coating off it and redistributes it. That coating goes onto your towels, your work shirts, your daughter's leggings, your underwear and your bedding.</p>

<p><strong>This is the part people miss.</strong> With a cleaning spray, you spray, it dries, and most of it is gone. A dryer sheet is engineered to leave residue behind and to keep leaving it there. The smell three days later is not a memory of the wash. It is the coating, still on the shirt, still coming off.</p>

<p>Some of it stays on the fabric. The rest goes out the vent, which is where the researchers pointed their instruments.</p>

<h2 id="what-comes-out-of-the-vent">What did they measure coming out of the vent?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: more than 25 volatile organic compounds, seven hazardous air pollutants, and two EPA carcinogens.</p>

<p>Steinemann's team analysed the air coming out of residential dryer vents when a top selling scented detergent and a top selling scented dryer sheet were used. <a href="https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11869-011-0156-1">The findings were published in Air Quality, Atmosphere and Health in 2011</a>.</p>

<p>The two carcinogens were <strong>acetaldehyde</strong> and <strong>benzene</strong>. Neither appears on the carton, because neither is an ingredient. They are what the mixture becomes once it is heated and blown out of a metal pipe.</p>

<p>Steinemann's own summary of why this matters is the line worth keeping: <a href="https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/08/110824091537.htm">"This is an interesting source of pollution because emissions from dryer vents are essentially unregulated and unmonitored."</a></p>

<p>To give it a scale that means something, the researchers estimated that acetaldehyde coming from just that one detergent brand would account for roughly <strong>3% of all the acetaldehyde emitted by cars in the Seattle area</strong>. One brand. Against every car in a major American city.</p>

<p>Now think about where that vent is. It is a few feet off the ground on the outside wall of your house. It is often under a window, near a back door, or beside the patio where you sit.</p>

<h2 id="why-can-i-not-find-out-what-is-in-it">Why can I not find out what is in it?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: because "fragrance" is legally allowed to be one word standing in for dozens of chemicals, and that has been the arrangement for decades.</p>

<p>The single word "fragrance" can represent a mixture of many separate ingredients. <a href="https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetic-ingredients/fragrances-cosmetics">The FDA's own page explains that fragrance and flavour ingredients can be listed simply as "fragrance" or "flavor" because those combinations are treated as trade secrets</a>.</p>

<p>Not a loophole somebody found. A protection, written down, and it works exactly as designed.</p>

<p>California moved on this in 2017 with <a href="https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billTextClient.xhtml?bill_id=201720180SB258">the Cleaning Product Right to Know Act, Senate Bill 258</a>, which forces manufacturers of cleaning products to disclose ingredients on the label and online. The fact that a state had to pass a law to make a company tell you what is in the box tells you what the default was.</p>

<p>And I am not going to tell you to read the label. That is the standard advice and it is useless here. The information you would need is not printed on it, by law, and no amount of squinting at the carton will produce it.</p>

<h2 id="does-that-mean-it-is-hurting-me">Does that mean it is hurting me? The honest answer</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: that study measured what comes out of the vent, not what happens inside a person, and I am going to say so plainly rather than stretch it.</p>

<p>Steinemann counted compounds in dryer exhaust. That is an emissions study. It did not follow anybody for twenty years, and it did not establish that any particular person got ill from their laundry. Detecting a chemical is not the same as measuring a dose, and a dose is not the same as an effect.</p>

<p>Anybody who shows you that study and tells you dryer sheets gave someone cancer has gone further than the evidence, and I will not do that.</p>

<p>Here is what the study does establish, and it is enough on its own.</p>

<p>You were sold a product on the promise that it makes your family's clothes clean and fresh. Nobody told you it was putting benzene into the air outside your kitchen window. Nobody had to tell you, because dryer vents are unregulated and unmonitored, and the word "fragrance" is legally allowed to cover the rest.</p>

<p><strong>You were never given the information to make the decision.</strong> That is the finding. What you do with the box now is a decision you get to make with your eyes open, which is more than you had last week.</p>

<p>And the company standing behind Bounce is Procter and Gamble, which is not a stranger in this house. It makes <a href="https://join.switchtoamerica.com/post/is-tide-laundry-detergent-toxic">Tide</a> and <a href="https://join.switchtoamerica.com/post/is-febreze-safe-to-breathe">Febreze</a>, and in 2021 it recalled aerosol products over benzene contamination, which we went through in <a href="https://join.switchtoamerica.com/post/is-spray-deodorant-safe-benzene-recall">the spray deodorant recall</a>. Same chemical. Same company. Different aisle.</p>

<h2 id="why-this-one-matters-for-women-over-forty">Why does this one matter for women over forty?</h2>

<p>You are waking at three in the morning. You throw the covers off, then you are cold, then you are awake for an hour. Your skin has turned on you in the last few years and you cannot work out why. Somebody has told you it is just your age.</p>

<p>Your face is on that pillowcase for seven hours a night, every night, and the coating is still on it. Your dressing gown, your bra, the towel you dry your face with. That is not an occasional exposure. It is the most reliably repeated thing in your entire week.</p>

<p>Here is the part that is not fair. That smell is the smell of a job done. You trained on it. Your mother used it, you used it while you were raising your daughter, and it means the house is under control.</p>

<p>Nobody sold you a chemical. They sold you the feeling of being a woman who takes care of her family, then attached it to a box that has been coming out of a vent unmonitored for forty years.</p>

<p>You did not fail at anything. You were loyal to a smell.</p>

<h2 id="what-do-i-use-instead">What do I use instead?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: wool dryer balls, and nothing else. They are the whole solution and they last for years.</p>

<p>Three or four wool balls in the drum do the same two jobs. They separate the load so the air moves through it, which cuts drying time, and they knock the static down mechanically instead of chemically. No coating. Nothing transfers. Nothing goes out the vent that did not go in on the clothes.</p>

<p>Four things, in order:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Finish the box or bin it, then put wool balls in the drum tonight.</strong> Not next month. This is the single easiest switch in the whole house, and it is done in one minute.</li>
<li><strong>Wash the bedding first.</strong> Pillowcases and sheets, before anything else, because that is the seven hour exposure. You will notice this one within a week.</li>
<li><strong>Expect it to smell like nothing.</strong> This is the part that catches women out. Clean has no smell. The first few loads will feel wrong and then your nose recalibrates and you start noticing the smell in other people's houses.</li>
<li><strong>Do not go hunting for a "natural" scented sheet.</strong> That is the same product with a different front of box. If you want the fabric to smell of something, that something has to be coming off on you.</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="faq">What else do people ask?</h2>

<p><strong>Is liquid fabric softener any better?</strong> It works the same way, by depositing a coating on the fibres, and it goes on in the wash rather than the dryer. The wool balls replace both.</p>

<p><strong>Do wool dryer balls actually stop static?</strong> They cut it substantially by keeping the load separated so it tumbles properly. The static that is left is usually from over drying, so take the load out slightly damp and hang it for ten minutes.</p>

<p><strong>Does the study prove dryer sheets cause cancer?</strong> No, and this post does not claim it. It measured what comes out of the vent and found two compounds the EPA classifies as carcinogens. It did not measure what happens to a person. That distinction matters and I am not going to blur it to make a stronger headline.</p>
