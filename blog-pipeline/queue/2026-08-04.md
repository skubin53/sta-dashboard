<h2>So are air fresheners toxic?</h2>
<p>Yes, in the sense that matters to you. The EPA names air fresheners as a household source of volatile organic compounds and reports that <a href="https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/volatile-organic-compounds-impact-indoor-air-quality">concentrations of many VOCs are consistently higher indoors, up to ten times higher, than outdoors</a>. Federal toxicology data confirms that <a href="https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP/ToxFAQs/ToxFAQsDetails.aspx?faqid=703&amp;toxid=126">1,4-dichlorobenzene is used in air fresheners and toilet deodorizer blocks</a>, and that the Department of Health and Human Services has determined it "may reasonably be anticipated to be a carcinogen."</p>
<p>The part that should make you angry: when researchers tested 25 scented consumer products they found 133 different chemicals coming out of them, and <a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3018511/">only one of those 133 appeared on any label</a>.</p>

<p>You walk in the front door and the house smells like linen. Not like anything real. Like the word linen. For about four seconds you feel like you have it together, because a house that smells clean is a house that is fine.</p>

<p>Then your kid coughs in the night again. You end up in the hallway at 2 a.m. doing the math on what changed, and the little glowing thing in the outlet by the door is the one thing you never thought about, because it never occurred to you it was doing anything except smelling nice.</p>

<h2 id="what-is-in-it">What is actually inside an air freshener?</h2>

<p><span class="quick-answer">Quick answer:</span> a solvent, a synthetic fragrance blend, and something to hold the scent in the air. Almost none of it is required to be printed on the box.</p>

<p>The word "fragrance" on a label is not one ingredient. It is a category, legally allowed to stand in for dozens of chemicals the manufacturer calls a trade secret. That is not a conspiracy theory, it is how the labeling rule works.</p>

<p>In 2011, <em>Environmental Health Perspectives</em> reported on a study of 25 common scented products, air fresheners among them. Researchers detected <a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3018511/">133 different volatile organic compounds</a>, and 44 percent of the products emitted at least one of 24 hazardous air pollutants classified as carcinogenic, including acetaldehyde, 1,4-dioxane and formaldehyde.</p>

<p>Of all 133 chemicals detected, exactly one, ethanol, was listed on any product label.</p>

<h2 id="chemicals">Which chemicals are in air fresheners?</h2>

<p><span class="quick-answer">Quick answer:</span> learn these four and you will spot the same pattern in half your house.</p>

<h3>1,4-Dichlorobenzene</h3>
<p>The one in solid air fresheners, mothballs and toilet deodorizer blocks. The federal ToxFAQ is unusually plain: <a href="https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP/ToxFAQs/ToxFAQsDetails.aspx?faqid=703&amp;toxid=126">"You may be exposed to 1,4-dichlorobenzene by breathing vapors from products used in the home or in buildings, such as air fresheners, mothballs, and toilet-deodorizer blocks."</a> It adds that Health and Human Services has determined it "may reasonably be anticipated to be a carcinogen."</p>

<p>In fairness, the same document states that IARC and the EPA concluded the 1,2 and 1,3 forms are "not classifiable as to human carcinogenicity." The concern is specific to the 1,4 form, which is the one in the air freshener.</p>

<h3>Formaldehyde</h3>
<p>Formaldehyde is both an ingredient in some products and a byproduct that forms indoors when fragrance chemicals meet ozone. The National Cancer Institute states that <a href="https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/causes-prevention/risk/substances/formaldehyde">formaldehyde is classified as a known human carcinogen</a>.</p>

<h3>Phthalates</h3>
<p>Phthalates make a scent linger instead of fading in twenty minutes. Several are on California's <a href="https://oehha.ca.gov/proposition-65/proposition-65-list">Proposition 65 list</a> of chemicals known to the state to cause cancer or reproductive toxicity. They almost never appear by name, because they fall under "fragrance."</p>

<h3>Fine particulate matter</h3>
<p>Anything that puts a mist or a burn into the air adds particles to it. The EPA notes fine particles are small enough to <a href="https://www.epa.gov/pm-pollution/health-and-environmental-effects-particulate-matter-pm">get deep into the lungs, and some may get into the bloodstream</a>. Same mechanism as <a href="https://join.switchtoamerica.com/post/are-scentsy-wax-melts-toxic">warmed wax melts</a>, which people switch to believing they are safer.</p>

<h2 id="room-by-room">Which room is the worst one in your house?</h2>

<p><span class="quick-answer">Quick answer:</span> the smallest room with the least airflow and the longest occupancy. Usually a bedroom, not the bathroom everyone worries about.</p>

<p>The bathroom gets blamed because that is where the deodorizer block lives. But you are in there eleven minutes and the fan runs. A plug-in in a bedroom runs all night, in a closed room, every night of the year.</p>

<h2 id="kids">Are air fresheners worse for kids and pets?</h2>

<p><span class="quick-answer">Quick answer:</span> same air, smaller bodies, more breaths per minute, closer to the floor.</p>

<p>A four year old breathes faster than you do relative to body weight, and spends the day close to the ground where heavier vapors settle. Your dog lives at that height. The math is different for a forty pound body, and the product was never designed with that in mind.</p>

<h2 id="natural">Does "natural" or "essential oil" on the front change anything?</h2>

<p><span class="quick-answer">Quick answer:</span> the research says no, and this is the finding that should change how you shop.</p>

<p>The same Environmental Health Perspectives study tested products marketed as green and natural alongside conventional ones. Its conclusion was blunt: <a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3018511/">"Even products advertised as 'green,' 'natural,' or 'organic' emitted as many hazardous chemicals as standard ones."</a></p>

<p>This is why I will not tell you to read the label. You cannot read your way out of this. The label is the marketing, and "fragrance", the one word that would tell you the most, is the one word legally allowed to mean anything. The question is never what the bottle says. It is whether the company would still make the product this way if it had to print the whole list.</p>

<h2 id="swap">What do you use instead?</h2>

<p><span class="quick-answer">Quick answer:</span> take out the continuous source, put in real airflow, and if you want a scent, buy it from a company that tells you what is in it.</p>

<p>Four steps. You can do the first one before you finish this page.</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>Unplug the one in the room where somebody sleeps.</strong> Not all of them. One. The bedroom.</li>
<li><strong>Open two windows on opposite sides of the house for ten minutes a day.</strong> A cross-breeze does more for indoor air than any product ever sold.</li>
<li><strong>Find the source of the smell you were covering.</strong> The trash, the drain, the dog bed, the laundry that sat wet. Covering a smell with a chemical is a strange thing to do once you say it out loud.</li>
<li><strong>If you still want your house to smell like something, buy it from a company that publishes its ingredients</strong> instead of hiding sixty behind one word. That is the whole standard. Not complicated, just rare.</li>
</ol>

<h2 id="faq">What else do people ask about air fresheners?</h2>

<p><strong>Are diffusers safer than plug-ins?</strong><br>
A different exposure, not automatically a safe one. A diffuser still puts a fine mist into a closed room, and concentrated oils can irritate airways, particularly in small children and in cats. Use one in an open room, briefly, not overnight in a bedroom.</p>

<p><strong>Is it the scent itself, or the delivery?</strong><br>
Mostly the delivery. A cut lemon on the counter is not the same as a device releasing an undisclosed blend continuously for 50 days. The issue is constant, enclosed, unmeasured exposure.</p>

<p><strong>I have used these for fifteen years. Have I already done the damage?</strong><br>
Nobody can answer that honestly, and be suspicious of anyone who tries. What is knowable is that exposure stops when the source stops, and that is within your control today.</p>

<p><strong>What about the car?</strong><br>
A car is the smallest, least ventilated room you own, and it sits in the sun. If you remove one vent clip or hanging tree, that is the one.</p>

<p><strong>Do I need to throw everything out at once?</strong><br>
No. Doing it all in a weekend is how people quit. One room, the one where somebody sleeps.</p>
