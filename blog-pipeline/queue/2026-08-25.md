<h2 id="the-short-answer">The short answer</h2>
<p>One ingredient made Head and Shoulders famous. It is called zinc pyrithione. Europe banned it from shampoo on 1 March 2022.</p>
<p><strong><a href="https://www.cirs-group.com/en/cosmetics/breaking-news-the-ban-on-the-cosmetic-anti-dandruff-agent-zinc-pyrithione-zpt-in-the-eu-has-come-into-force-on-march-1-2022">Europe banned it as toxic to having children.</a></strong> Brands sold there had to change the recipe. They did it.</p>
<p>It is still legal here. It is the same company. It is the same shampoo. The answer changes at the border.</p>

<h2 id="what-did-europe-actually-do">What did Europe actually do, and when?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: Europe banned it in shampoo. That started 1 March 2022.</p>

<p>The rule was called Regulation 2021/1902. It moved a group of chemicals onto the banned list. <a href="https://www.obelis.net/news/lilial-and-zinc-pyrithione-banned-in-the-eu/">Zinc pyrithione was one of them. So was Lilial</a>, a scent chemical. Both went on the same day.</p>

<p>The reason was harm to having children. Europe files that under category 1B. Over there, that is not a warning label. It is a door closing.</p>

<p><strong>Changing the recipe was not optional.</strong> Every dandruff shampoo in Europe had to drop it. The companies did it. They did it on time, and quietly.</p>

<h2 id="why-is-it-still-legal-here">Why is it still legal here?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: the two countries ask different questions. They ask them before a product reaches the shelf.</p>

<p>Europe looks at what a chemical can do. A bad rating gets it pulled from shampoo. Then the company has to argue to keep it.</p>

<p>America works the other way. A chemical stays until someone proves it hurt people. Finding that proof is somebody else's job. Here, zinc pyrithione is not even a cosmetic. It is a dandruff drug you can buy off the shelf. The FDA has not moved against it.</p>

<p>Nothing was hidden from you. It was all public. It sat in a European rule book. It was written in code words about hazard. Nobody expects you to read that on a Tuesday.</p>

<p><strong>Here is what I keep coming back to.</strong> They changed it for one country and not the other. So they already know how. They know how to make it without that ingredient. They are just not made to here. So they do not.</p>

<h2 id="what-is-in-the-bottle">What is in the bottle?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: a germ killer, a soap, a silicone coat. And a scent that is allowed to stay secret.</p>

<h3>Zinc pyrithione</h3>
<p>The classic one. It kills the fungus, and it works. Europe banned it from shampoo in March 2022. Here it is still allowed, as a drug.</p>

<h3>Selenium sulfide</h3>
<p>This one is in the Clinical Strength bottles. Not the everyday ones. <a href="https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=3272a482-3813-63e4-e063-6394a90a43eb">The drug label for Clinical Strength</a> lists it. It carries the usual drug warnings. One of them is to keep it away from children.</p>

<h3>Fragrance</h3>
<p>One word on a label. It can stand for a whole list of chemicals. <a href="https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetic-ingredients/fragrances-cosmetics">The FDA lets a company print just that one word</a>, because the mix counts as a trade secret. Whatever is in there goes on your scalp. Then it runs down your back in hot water.</p>

<h3>Sodium laureth sulfate</h3>
<p>The soap that makes the suds. The worry is how it gets made. That can leave a trace of 1,4-dioxane behind. It is the same kind of problem as the benzene in the 2021 spray recalls. It is not an ingredient. It is a leftover.</p>

<h3>Dimethicone</h3>
<p>A silicone. It coats the hair so it feels smooth. It is why the first wash after you switch feels wrong. You are feeling your own hair.</p>

<h2 id="does-that-mean-it-is-hurting-me">Does that mean it is hurting me?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: the ban is a fact. What it did to you is not.</p>

<p>Here is the honest version. Europe banned it on a rating. Not on a study of women washing their hair. A rating says what a chemical can do. It does not say what happens in your shower.</p>

<p>The FDA has not banned zinc pyrithione. Procter and Gamble says their products are safe. That is their side, and it belongs here.</p>

<p>What is left is still worth your time. One of the strictest bodies in the world looked at this. It decided the stuff does not belong in shampoo. The company obeyed there. It kept selling here. Both are true at once.</p>

<h2 id="why-this-matters-after-forty">Why does this one land differently after forty?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: you use this for decades. Not once.</p>

<p>Nobody buys dandruff shampoo for one week. You buy it because your scalp itches. It works, so it stays in the shower. Twenty years is normal. Thirty is not unusual.</p>

<p>The women I talk to are in a season of change. Hair is thinner than it was. The scalp is drier. It all got blamed on hormones and age. Then it got left there. Nobody went back to the cupboard. Nobody checked what had been in the shower the whole time.</p>

<h2 id="faq">What else do people ask?</h2>

<p><strong>Did Head and Shoulders change its formula in Europe?</strong> Dandruff shampoos sold in Europe had to drop zinc pyrithione by 1 March 2022. So the recipe changed there. The American bottles did not have to change.</p>

<p><strong>Is selenium sulfide the safer one then?</strong> It is a different drug. It has its own label and its own warnings. It is not an upgrade. It replaced zinc pyrithione in some lines. It was not picked for being gentler.</p>

<p><strong>Will my dandruff come back if I switch?</strong> It might, for about two weeks, while your scalp settles. That is where most people give up. Two weeks is the honest window.</p>

<p><strong>Does the EU ban prove the shampoo harmed anyone?</strong> No, and this post does not claim it. The ban followed a rating, not a study of shampoo users. I will not blur that for a stronger headline.</p>
