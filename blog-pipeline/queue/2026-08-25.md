<h2 id="the-short-answer">The short answer</h2>
<p>The active ingredient that made Head and Shoulders famous is zinc pyrithione. On 1 March 2022 it became illegal to sell in a cosmetic anywhere in the European Union.</p>
<p><strong><a href="https://www.cirs-group.com/en/cosmetics/breaking-news-the-ban-on-the-cosmetic-anti-dandruff-agent-zinc-pyrithione-zpt-in-the-eu-has-come-into-force-on-march-1-2022">It was banned after being classified as toxic for reproduction, category 1B.</a></strong> Brands sold across Europe had to reformulate.</p>
<p>It is still legal here. The same company, the same shampoo, two different answers depending on which country's shelf it is sitting on.</p>

<h2 id="what-did-europe-actually-do">What did Europe actually do, and when?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: the EU added zinc pyrithione to its list of substances prohibited in cosmetic products, effective 1 March 2022.</p>

<p>It came in through Commission Regulation (EU) 2021/1902, which moved a batch of substances onto the prohibited list because of their harmonised hazard classifications. <a href="https://www.obelis.net/news/lilial-and-zinc-pyrithione-banned-in-the-eu/">Zinc pyrithione went on that list alongside Lilial</a>, a fragrance ingredient that went the same way on the same date.</p>

<p>The classification that triggered it was reproductive toxicity, category 1B. In the European system that category is not a warning label. It is a door closing.</p>

<p><strong>Reformulation was not optional.</strong> Every anti-dandruff shampoo sold in Europe that relied on it had to be rebuilt around something else, and the companies did exactly that, on schedule, without much noise.</p>

<h2 id="why-is-it-still-legal-here">Why is it still legal here?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: because the two systems ask completely different questions before something is allowed on a shelf.</p>

<p>Europe works on hazard. If a substance carries a certain classification, it comes out of cosmetics, and the burden sits with the company to prove a case for keeping it.</p>

<p>The United States works the other way around. A substance stays until there is enough evidence of harm to justify removing it, and gathering that evidence is somebody else's job. In this country zinc pyrithione is not even treated as a cosmetic ingredient. It is an over the counter drug active for dandruff, sitting in a monograph, and the FDA has not moved against it.</p>

<p>So nothing was hidden from you. The information was public, in a European regulation, in a language of hazard categories that nobody expects a woman buying shampoo on a Tuesday to go and read.</p>

<p><strong>Here is what I keep coming back to.</strong> A company that reformulates for one market and not another has already done the work. It knows how to make the product without that ingredient. It simply is not required to here, so it does not.</p>

<h2 id="what-is-in-the-bottle">What is in the bottle?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: an antifungal active, a detergent system, silicones, and a fragrance that is legally allowed to stay a secret.</p>

<h3>Zinc pyrithione</h3>
<p>The classic active. Antifungal, and effective at what it does. Prohibited in EU cosmetics since March 2022 on a reproductive toxicity 1B classification. Still a permitted over the counter drug active in the United States.</p>

<h3>Selenium sulfide</h3>
<p>The active in the Clinical Strength line rather than the everyday bottles. <a href="https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=3272a482-3813-63e4-e063-6394a90a43eb">The DailyMed drug label for Head and Shoulders Clinical Strength</a> lists it and carries the standard drug warnings, including keeping it out of reach of children.</p>

<h3>Fragrance</h3>
<p>One word on a label that can stand for a great many separate chemicals. <a href="https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetic-ingredients/fragrances-cosmetics">The FDA allows fragrance and flavour combinations to be listed as a single word</a> because they are treated as trade secrets. Whatever is in there, it goes on your scalp and down your back in hot water.</p>

<h3>Sodium laureth sulfate</h3>
<p>The detergent that makes the lather. The concern attached to it is the manufacturing process, which can leave 1,4-dioxane behind as a contaminant, the same category of problem as the benzene in the 2021 aerosol recalls. Not an ingredient. A residue.</p>

<h3>Dimethicone</h3>
<p>A silicone that coats the hair so it feels smooth. It is the reason the first wash after switching often feels disappointing. You are feeling your actual hair.</p>

<h2 id="does-that-mean-it-is-hurting-me">Does that mean it is hurting me?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: the ban is a fact. What a bottle of shampoo did to any one person is not.</p>

<p>The honest version has to include this: the European ban followed a hazard classification, not a study of women washing their hair. A classification describes what a substance can do at some level of exposure. It does not measure what happens in a shower.</p>

<p>The FDA has not restricted zinc pyrithione, and Procter and Gamble has consistently said their products are safe. That is their position and it belongs on the page.</p>

<p>What is left after all the caveats is still worth your attention. One of the most regulated bodies in the world looked at this substance and decided it should not be in a cosmetic at all. The company complied there and kept selling here. Both of those things are true at once.</p>

<h2 id="why-this-matters-after-forty">Why does this one land differently after forty?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: because this is a product you use for decades, not once.</p>

<p>Nobody buys anti-dandruff shampoo for a week. It gets bought because something is irritated and it keeps working, so it stays in the shower. Twenty years is normal. Thirty is not unusual.</p>

<p>The women I talk to are in a season where a lot is already shifting. Hair is thinner than it was. The scalp is drier. Everything got blamed on hormones and age and left there, and nobody ever went back through the cupboard to check what had been sitting in the shower the whole time.</p>

<p>The first fortnight is the part nobody warns you about. Coming off silicone, hair feels different before it feels better, because you are feeling hair rather than coating. Give it two weeks before you decide.</p>

<h2 id="faq">What else do people ask?</h2>

<p><strong>Did Head and Shoulders change its formula in Europe?</strong> Anti-dandruff shampoos sold in the EU had to stop using zinc pyrithione by 1 March 2022, so products relying on it were reformulated for that market. The American versions were under no such requirement.</p>

<p><strong>Is selenium sulfide the safer one then?</strong> It is a different active with its own drug label and its own warnings, not an automatic upgrade. It replaced zinc pyrithione in some lines rather than being chosen as gentler.</p>

<p><strong>Will my dandruff come back if I switch?</strong> It might for a fortnight while your scalp recalibrates, and that is the point most people give up. Two weeks is the honest window.</p>

<p><strong>Does the EU ban prove the shampoo harmed anyone?</strong> No, and this post does not claim it. The ban followed a hazard classification, not a study of shampoo users. I am not going to blur that to make a stronger headline.</p>
