<h2 id="the-short-answer">The short answer</h2>
<p>There is no number you owe. The ones you have been shown were written by people selling something.</p>
<p>The Federal Reserve asks the country directly, every year. In its 2024 survey, <a href="https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/files/2024-report-economic-well-being-us-households-202505.pdf">thirty five percent of people who have not retired thought their retirement savings were on track.</a></p>
<p><strong>Which means sixty five percent did not.</strong></p>
<p>You are not behind the room. You are the room.</p>

<h2 id="the-number">Where does that number you keep seeing come from?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: a company that manages money.</p>

<p>You know the one. Six times your salary by fifty. Eight times by sixty.</p>

<p>Ask where it came from. It came from firms that hold retirement accounts.</p>

<p>That is not a scandal. It is a rule of thumb and it is roughly the right shape.</p>

<p>But a rule of thumb is not a debt. Nobody sent you a bill.</p>

<p>And it does one thing badly. It assumes a straight line of full time years at rising pay.</p>

<p>Now think about your own twenties, thirties and forties.</p>

<p>The years at home. The part time stretch. The move for his job. The gap when your mother got ill.</p>

<p><strong>The benchmark was built for a working life you were never offered.</strong></p>

<h2 id="what-they-found">What did the Federal Reserve actually find?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: you are not on track, and neither is almost anyone else.</p>

<p>Two surveys matter here and both are the government's own.</p>

<p>The first asks people how they feel about it. Thirty five percent said their plan was on track in 2024.</p>

<p>That was up a little. It was also down from forty percent in 2021.</p>

<p>So on the country's best year in that run, six in ten still said no.</p>

<p>The second survey counts what is actually in the accounts.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/october-2023-changes-in-us-family-finances-from-2019-to-2022.htm">It looks at people aged 35 to 64 who have a retirement account at all.</a></p>

<p>Read that line twice. Everybody with nothing is already excluded.</p>

<p>Among that group, in the bottom half of earners, the average held was $54,700.</p>

<p>In the top tenth of earners it was $913,300.</p>

<h2 id="went-backwards">Who went backwards?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: the half that could least afford to.</p>

<p>Here is the part I could not stop thinking about.</p>

<p>The Fed ran the same measure in 2019 and again in 2022.</p>

<p>For the bottom half of earners it went from $66,600 to $54,700.</p>

<p>Down. Not up slowly. Down.</p>

<p>For the top tenth over those same three years it went from $803,100 to $913,300.</p>

<p><strong>Same country. Same three years. One half lost ground and the other gained.</strong></p>

<p>And remember who is in that first number. Only people who already had an account.</p>

<p>So when you sit at your kitchen table at eleven at night and feel behind, you are not reading yourself wrong.</p>

<p>You are reading the country right.</p>

<h2 id="am-i-behind">So am I behind, or is this normal?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: both, and I will not tell you only the comfortable half.</p>

<p>Let me argue against myself, because you deserve that.</p>

<p>Common does not mean fine. Sixty five percent of people not feeling on track is not permission. It is a description.</p>

<p>These are averages, not medians, so a few large accounts pull them up. The middle of that bottom half is lower still.</p>

<p>They are also two different groups of people compared three years apart, not the same families followed through.</p>

<p>And the Fed is measuring accounts. It is not measuring your house, your pension from the job you left, or the man you may or may not still be married to.</p>

<p>So I cannot tell you that you are fine.</p>

<p>What I can tell you is that the feeling of being uniquely stupid about this is wrong, and it is doing you real harm.</p>

<p>Shame keeps you from opening the statement. Not opening the statement is the only part of this that is actually your fault.</p>

<h2 id="the-two-levers">What can you still change at fifty?</h2>

<p class="quick-answer">Quick answer: there were three. You have one and a half left.</p>

<p>Every plan rests on three things. How long the money grows, what it earns, and how much goes in.</p>

<p>Time you cannot buy back. That one is spent.</p>

<p>The return is not yours to set. Anyone promising you a number there is telling you a story.</p>

<p>What is left is the third one. How much goes in.</p>

<p>At twenty five, time does the heavy lifting and the amount barely matters.</p>

<p>At fifty it reverses. The amount is nearly all of it.</p>

<p>So the question stops being what you should have saved by now.</p>

<p><strong>It becomes what you can add from here, and for how long.</strong></p>

<p>And that one is not about being clever with money.</p>

<p>It is about whether anything comes in beyond the wage you already work for.</p>

<p>That is the part I am any use on.</p>

<p>I will not put a figure on it here.</p>

<p>Anybody who does that on a website is selling you something. You have been sold enough numbers.</p>

<p>What I will do is sit with you for twenty minutes. Then you see the arithmetic from where you actually stand.</p>

<p>That is a smaller, kinder question, and unlike the other one it has an answer you can actually work on.</p>

<h2 id="faq">Questions women are asking me</h2>

<p><strong>Is fifty too late to fix this?</strong></p>
<p>No, but the shape of the fix changes. Before forty the answer is usually time. After fifty it is usually the amount going in. Different question, same person.</p>

<p><strong>Should I be putting it in the market instead?</strong></p>
<p>I am not licensed to tell you where to put money and I will not pretend otherwise. What I talk about is where more of it comes from in the first place.</p>

<p><strong>What if I have nothing at all?</strong></p>
<p>Then you are outside those Federal Reserve figures entirely, because they only counted people who already had an account. That is more common than the numbers make it look.</p>

<p><strong>My husband handles all of this. Is that a problem?</strong></p>
<p>It is only a problem if you could not describe it out loud. Ask to see it once. Not to take it over, just so it is not a room in your own house you have never been in.</p>
