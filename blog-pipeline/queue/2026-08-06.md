<h2>Is Colgate toothpaste safe?</h2>
<p>Start with the thing already printed on the tube. Federal law requires every fluoride toothpaste sold in America to carry this exact warning: <a href="https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/section-355.50">"Keep out of reach of children under 6 years of age. If more than used for brushing is accidentally swallowed, get medical help or contact a Poison Control Center right away."</a> That is 21 CFR 355.50, and Colgate did not choose to put it there. The FDA made them.</p>
<p>Then, in August 2024, the National Toxicology Program concluded with moderate confidence that <a href="https://ntp.niehs.nih.gov/whatwestudy/assessments/noncancer/completed/fluoride">higher fluoride exposure, such as drinking water above 1.5 mg/L, is associated with lower IQ in children</a>. The same report states there were insufficient data to say whether the 0.7 mg/L used in US water supplies has that effect. Both of those sentences are true, and you deserve both.</p>

<p>It is 7:40 on a Tuesday and your four year old is on the step stool with foam on his chin, and he swallows. He always swallows. He is four. You say the thing you always say, spit it out buddy, and he grins at you with blue teeth.</p>

<p>And on the back of the tube in your hand, in type smaller than the flavor name, is the word poison.</p>

<h2 id="warning">Why is there a poison warning on toothpaste?</h2>

<p><span class="quick-answer">Quick answer:</span> because fluoride toothpaste is regulated as an over the counter drug, and the FDA decided parents needed to be told.</p>

<p>This is the part that stops people. Not a study, not a headline. The label the company is legally required to print and would almost certainly rather not.</p>

<p>The rule is specific. For <a href="https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/section-355.50">all fluoride dentifrice products</a> the labeling must carry, in bold, "Keep out of reach of children under 6 years of age," then the Poison Control instruction. The directions must also say "Instruct children under 6 years of age in good brushing and rinsing habits (to minimize swallowing)" and "Supervise children as necessary until capable of using without supervision."</p>

<p>To minimize swallowing. Read that again and then picture the bathroom at 7:40 on a Tuesday.</p>

<h2 id="ntp">What did the 2024 fluoride report actually find?</h2>

<p><span class="quick-answer">Quick answer:</span> a link between higher fluoride exposure and lower IQ in children, at levels above what US water supplies use, with the low end left explicitly unanswered.</p>

<p>The National Toxicology Program is part of the federal government, not an advocacy group. Its August 2024 monograph concluded with "moderate confidence" that <a href="https://ntp.niehs.nih.gov/whatwestudy/assessments/noncancer/completed/fluoride">higher levels of fluoride exposure, such as drinking water containing more than 1.5 milligrams per liter, are associated with lower IQ in children</a>.</p>

<p>Now the honest half, which most articles on both sides leave out. NTP states plainly: "there were insufficient data to determine if the low fluoride level of 0.7 mg/L currently recommended for U.S. community water supplies has a negative effect on children's IQ." It adds that the review "was designed to evaluate total fluoride exposure from all sources."</p>

<p>All sources. Water, plus toothpaste twice a day, plus whatever a four year old swallows because he is four.</p>

<h3>What fluoride does well</h3>
<p>It would be dishonest to skip this. The National Institute of Dental and Craniofacial Research states fluoride works by <a href="https://www.nidcr.nih.gov/health-info/fluoride">"strengthening the tooth's hard outer surface, called enamel"</a> and helps reverse early decay. Cavities in small children mean pain, infection and sometimes general anesthesia. Anyone telling you fluoride does nothing is selling you something too.</p>

<h2 id="ingredients">What else is in the tube?</h2>

<p><span class="quick-answer">Quick answer:</span> fluoride is the active drug. The rest is abrasives, foaming agents, sweeteners and dye, and the history there is not spotless.</p>

<h3>Triclosan</h3>
<p>For years the flagship Colgate Total contained triclosan, an antibacterial compound. In 2016 the FDA banned triclosan from over the counter antiseptic washes, and in 2019 the company reformulated Colgate Total to stannous fluoride. The point is not that triclosan is in your tube today. It is not. The point is that it was, for years, while people asked about it.</p>

<h3>Sodium lauryl sulfate</h3>
<p>SLS is the foaming agent, the reason toothpaste behaves like toothpaste. It is also a known irritant for some people. If you get recurring canker sores, it is the first thing worth removing.</p>

<h3>Heavy metal contamination</h3>
<p>An allegation, not a finding, so it belongs in the receipts below rather than stated as fact. But it is being litigated right now, and it concerns children's toothpaste.</p>

<h2 id="kids">How much should a four year old actually use?</h2>

<p><span class="quick-answer">Quick answer:</span> far less than the ribbon shown on the box, and the box is doing that on purpose according to a pending lawsuit.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.nidcr.nih.gov/health-info/fluoride">NIDCR</a> states fluoride toothpaste should not be used for children under 2 unless a dentist or doctor advises it. For older children the amount is small, and supervision is required by the labeling rule itself.</p>

<p>Look at the front of a children's toothpaste box sometime, at how much paste is on the brush in that picture, then at what a dentist tells you to use. A class action against Colgate and Tom's of Maine <a href="https://www.classaction.org/news/colgate-toms-of-maine-lawsuit-claims-certain-kids-fluoride-toothpastes-are-deceptively-marketed-to-encourage-overuse">alleges the packaging is designed to encourage overuse</a>. That is an allegation, and the companies dispute it.</p>

<h2 id="fluoride-free">Is fluoride-free toothpaste the answer?</h2>

<p><span class="quick-answer">Quick answer:</span> not automatically, and this is where a lot of well-meaning parents get it wrong.</p>

<p>Taking the fluoride out does not make a tube clean. A children's toothpaste can be fluoride-free and still be a sweetened, dyed, foaming product from a company that will not say where its raw materials come from. Some heavy-metal allegations in the litigation below involve fluoride-free kids' products specifically.</p>

<p>Which is why I will not tell you to read the label. You cannot read your way out of this one. The things being alleged, lead and mercury, would never appear on an ingredient list, because nobody adds them on purpose. They arrive through raw materials and equipment.</p>

<p>The question is not what the tube says. It is whether the company would still make it this way if it had to publish where every input came from.</p>

<h2 id="swap">So what do you do at bedtime tonight?</h2>

<p><span class="quick-answer">Quick answer:</span> four things, and none of them require you to panic or to throw out your kid's toothbrush.</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>Cut the amount right now.</strong> A smear for the little one, a pea for the older one. This is free and it happens tonight.</li>
<li><strong>Stand there for the spit.</strong> The labeling rule literally says to supervise until they can do it alone. That is the FDA's instruction, not mine.</li>
<li><strong>Ask your dentist what your child's actual risk is.</strong> A kid with no cavities and good water is a different case than a kid with four fillings. Fluoride is a real tool for a real problem; the question is dose, not villain.</li>
<li><strong>When the tube runs out, buy the next one from a company that publishes what is in it and where it comes from.</strong> That is the standard. It is not extreme, it is just uncommon.</li>
</ol>

<h2 id="faq">What else do people ask?</h2>

<p><strong>Should I stop using fluoride toothpaste entirely?</strong><br>
That is a conversation for you and your dentist, because it depends on your child's cavity risk and your water. Cutting the amount and supervising the spit is a change you can make tonight without giving anything up.</p>

<p><strong>What are the white spots on my kid's front teeth?</strong><br>
That can be dental fluorosis, which comes from swallowing too much fluoride while permanent teeth are still forming under the gums. It is cosmetic, not painful. It is also a signal about total dose, so ask your dentist.</p>

<p><strong>Is triclosan still in Colgate Total?</strong><br>
No. The product was reformulated to stannous fluoride in 2019. Old articles claiming otherwise are out of date.</p>

<p><strong>Is charcoal toothpaste a safer swap?</strong><br>
Not necessarily. Charcoal is abrasive, and there is a pending class action alleging its safety was misrepresented. Different marketing is not the same as a different standard.</p>

<p><strong>Does my whole family need to change at once?</strong><br>
No. Start with the youngest person in the house, because they swallow the most and their body weight is the smallest. One person, one tube.</p>

<h2>Who you become</h2>

<p>A year from now you are not the mom who panicked and threw out the bathroom. You are the one who read the warning that was on the tube the whole time, cut the dose in half, stood there for the spit, and asked her dentist a better question.</p>

<p>Your kids will not remember any of it. They will remember that you stayed in the doorway at bedtime. That is the whole thing.</p>
