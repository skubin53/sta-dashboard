## NON-NEGOTIABLE

### THE CRAFT (Halbert, Kern, Sugarman)

**Never position the reader as behind, asleep, or naive for not already knowing this**.

Specifically banned: the genre-default hook and its variants, "you've been using this for years
and never knew," "most women have no idea what's under their sink," "if you're still buying X,
you're getting played " She is not a woman who missed something obvious, she is a woman who was
lied to by a company that had the data and sold it anyway  The ignorance in the post belongs to
the villain's disclosure, never to her judgment  Applies to the hook, every problem-agitation
line, and any "here's what they don't tell you" bullet  Test: read the line back imagining a
52-year-old who has run her household for thirty years is reading it over your shoulder  If she
would feel caught out rather than betrayed, rewrite it so the company is the one exposed

> *"Anything that implies they're behind or stupid for not already knowing this."*
> 
> `sources/halbert-writing-system.md`  ...  changes: The hook and the problem-agitation paragraphs. Kills the standard 'you've been using this for years and never knew' sneer.

**Never let a claim sit unsupported until the receipts section**.

The sentence immediately after a bold claim carries the specific that proves it: the dollar
figure, the court and year, the recall count, the named source  The receipts list at the bottom
is the archive, not the load-bearing proof  If the next sentence cannot support the claim, the
claim is not ready to publish

> *"Never leave a claim hanging. If you write "Pamela rebuilt her business in 60 minutes," the very next sentence has to support it."*
> 
> `sources/kern-voice-rules.md`  ...  changes: Every paragraph that makes an accusation. Moves proof up out of the receipts list and into the body.

**Pre-publish sweep the whole draft for "many," "significant," "great," "amazing," "a lot of**.

" Each one becomes a number, a named person or agency, or a date from an approved source  If no
source exists, delete the claim  Never invent a figure to satisfy the sweep

> *"Fix: Replace every instance of "many," "significant," "great," or "amazing" with specific numbers, names, and verifiable facts."*
> 
> `sources/halbert-writing-system.md`  ...  changes: A find-and-replace editing pass over the whole draft before publishing.

**Final pass on every post: copy out only the H2s, their Quick answer lines, and the bolded phrases, in order, and read them as a standalone document**.

That extract alone must carry the full argument from problem to villain to what she should do
Any H2 that reads as a topic label rather than a claim gets rewritten, and any Quick answer that
only makes sense after the paragraph under it gets rewritten to stand alone

> *"They hit: headline > subheads > bold text > bullets > P.S. > price. Make sure those elements alone tell a complete, compelling story."*
> 
> `sources/halbert-writing-system.md`  ...  changes: Forces a final skim-pass edit of every H2 and Quick answer line.

Watch the three places a blog buries them: question-form H2s ("Discover how   "), the two scan
CTAs ("Don't miss   "), and the answer capsule or who-you-become close ("transform your home")
Ban the phrase "leverage your potential", not the word "leverage"  Source: kern-voice-rules md
lines 154-163, "What Bad AIM Labs Email Copy Looks Like" / "If your draft sounds like any of the

## THE WHY UNDER A RULE. The mechanism, so it can be applied to a case no rule anticipated.

### There are exactly three reasons people do not buy: they do not want it, they cannot afford it, or they do not believe you

The first two are often outside your control. The third must always be overcome. This is the load-
bearing why under every proof rule.

> *"the three reasons people don't buy: (1) they don't want it, (2) they can't afford it, (3) they don't believe you. The first two are sometimes out of your control. The third must always be overcome."*
>
> `sources/halbert-copywriter-skill.md (Core Principle 7, Proof Crushes Skepticism)`

**Use it:** Do a numbering pass on the finished draft: write 1, 2, or 3 in the margin of every section (1 =
making her want a cleaner home, 2 = price/affordability, 3 = making her believe the claim). She
arrived by typing "is Tide toxic," so slot 1 is already closed before she reads a word and slot 2
is one line, not a section. Every section marked 1 either gets cut or gets converted, and
converting means swapping the atmosphere sentence for the three things belief runs on: the named
body, the year, the exact filed figure. Worked case, "Is Tide toxic": a paragraph that opens
"picture your linen closet smelling like nothing at all, just clean cotton" is a 1 and it goes; in
its place, "In [year] the [named regulator] fined [company] $[exact amount] for [the specific
conduct]." Slot 2 gets exactly one sentence, the price-versus-big-box comparison, and never its
own H2. When a section runs long and you cannot decide what to cut, the question is not "is this
good writing," it is "which number is this," and 1 and 2 lose. Sanity check before publish: if you
strip out every sentence carrying a name, a date, or a dollar figure and the post still reads
full, you have written the wrong 1,500 words.

### Curiosity, not self-interest, is the number one reason people buy from an advertisement, confirmed by Halbert's protege Scott Haines

Curiosity creates an itch that only the product can scratch. The mechanism is an information gap:
tease, do not tell, and the gap itself is what pulls.

> *"Halbert's protege Scott Haines confirmed: Halbert believed curiosity, not self-interest, is the #1 reason people buy from an advertisement."*
>
> `sources/halbert-copywriter-skill.md (Core Principle 6, Curiosity is King)`

**Use it:** Halbert's deployment list for curiosity is subheads and open loops, which IS her H2 + Quick answer
skeleton, so run this pass on every Quick answer line in the draft. Test: after reading the two-
line Quick answer, does the reader still have a reason to read the H3 under it? If no, the section
is now optional and skimmers leave. Fix without breaking the pass-1 skeleton test: answer the
literal question completely and honestly, then name a specific thing you have not explained yet.
Weak (closed loop): "Is Fabuloso a disinfectant? Quick answer: No. It cleans surfaces but it does
not kill bacteria or viruses, and the label has never claimed it does." Strong (answered, but
reopened): "Quick answer: No. It cleans, it does not kill anything, and the label has never
claimed to — which matters most in the one room where you have probably been assuming it did."
Reader now has to read the H3 to find out which room. The reopener must be a concrete noun she can
picture (the room, the surface, the age, the number, the year), never a vague "and there is more
to it than that." Do this on all H2s, and put the concession/limit section LAST among the loops so
the post does not run out of pull before her Advice box.

### The unit of judgment is not the paragraph, it is the seam between two lines

Every element is graded on one question only: does it create a reason to read the NEXT line. This
reframes editing entirely. You do not ask "is this section good," you ask "at the end of this
sentence, is the reader finished?" Anything that lets her feel finished is the defect, even if it
is well written and true.

> *"The core principle from both Gary Halbert and Joseph Sugarman: every single element of your email has ONE job - to get the reader to read the next line."*
>
> `sources/email-copywriting-guide.md`

**Use it:** Seam audit, run once on the finished draft, five minutes. Copy the LAST sentence of every H2 and

### A damaging admission works because trust cannot be asserted, only inferred, and the reader infers it from evidence that you are not editing

Naming what the thing is NOT proves the disclosure valve is open, and once she believes nothing is
being withheld she stops applying her standing discount to everything else you said. That is the
mechanism: the admission does not buy belief in itself, it removes the discount from the claims
around it. Which means an admission that costs you nothing does nothing, and an admission placed
far from the claim it is protecting does nothing either.

> *"Honesty about what something is NOT is the fastest way to be trusted about what it IS."*
>
> `sources/kern-voice-rules.md`

**Use it:** Move the "evidence against the story" section so it ends one line above the receipts list, not
near the end of the post. Then run two checks on it. COST CHECK: the concession has to name
something that actually weakens her case, in her own mouth. "The dose in one mopping is small, and
nobody has proven one bucket of this hurt anybody. I am not going to pretend they have." Not a
fake-cost hedge like "of course, more research is needed" or "everyone's body is different" —
those cost her nothing, so they buy nothing. ADJACENCY CHECK: the sentence that follows the
concession must be the strongest claim in the post, hand-offs on contact: "Here is what the
company has actually been sued and fined over." If more than one paragraph separates the admission
from the claim it is protecting, it is protecting nothing. Practical test before publishing:
delete the concession paragraph and reread the receipts list. If it now reads like a woman with a
grudge stacking headlines, the concession was doing work and it belongs exactly where it was. If
nothing changes, the concession was free and needs rewriting until it hurts.

### The emotional rewrite is not the same claim with more adjectives

Look at what actually changed between the two versions: the logical one is a capability, the
emotional one is a timestamped private moment with an internal sentence inside it. 2 AM. Alone. A
specific worry, quoted. That is the whole conversion recipe: pick the hour, pick the room, quote
the sentence she says in her head. And it is second-order, the feeling AFTER the feeling, not the
first benefit but what the first benefit frees her from.

> *""24/7 support" = logical. "You'll never feel stuck at 2 AM wondering if you're doing this wrong" = emotional."*
>
> `sources/email-copywriting-guide.md`

**Use it:** Source phrase to hold in your head while writing the close: "Sell the feeling after the feeling."
Test any candidate closing line by asking what it is one level short of. Three tiers, and only the
third ships: 1. Capability: "You will not be breathing synthetic fragrance." Cut. 2. First
feeling: "Your headaches stop." Cut, this is where most drafts die. 3. Feeling after the feeling:
the bracing stops. Ship.  To build tier 3, fill this: [clock time] + [where in the house she is] +
[the ordinary task just finished] + [the sentence she is NOT saying in her head anymore, in quotes
or near-quotes].  Worked, for an "Is your all-purpose cleaner toxic" post close: "It is 9:40 at
night. You wiped the last counter twenty minutes ago and you are on the couch. You are not doing
the thing where you keep half an eye out to see if your head is going to start."  Same move on
sentence one, inverted into the pain knife: instead of naming the chemical, name the hour and the
private sentence. "You wipe the counter at nine at night and by the time you sit down you are
already thinking, here it comes."  This stacks with the pass-1 rule about closing on what she can
see, smell and touch. That rule tells you to make the close physical. This one tells you which
moment to make physical: the one AFTER the benefit lands, when she notices she is not waiting for
something. Physical plus second-order, both.  Reason it is worth the extra sentence of length:
line 83 records a 4x lift on the identical offer with the wrapper changed and the evidence
untouched, so this costs you nothing in the receipts section.

### Telegraphing kills a diagnostic

The fit-list on the landing page pre-coached visitors on which quiz answers scored well, so
everyone scored qualified and the quiz stopped measuring anything. The mechanism generalises to
any section whose job is to let the reader reach a verdict: if she can see which answer you want,
she stops using the section to decide and starts using it to please you, and then she does not
trust the verdict either.

> *"The fit-list (:62-66) telegraphs the quiz's "right answers" ("Move when something's right, instead of overthinking it") — it pre-coaches visitors to score qualified, defeating the quiz's sorting function."*
>
> `2026-06-11-theshannonnicole-fable5/sales-psychology.md`

**Use it:** Three 30-second tests before publish, on the three sections that exist to let her reach a verdict
herself.  1. COUNTER-EVIDENCE SECTION. Write the opposing case as its own standalone sentence with
a real named body and a real number, and no "but" inside it. Rebut in the NEXT paragraph, never
the same one. Test: read only that first sentence aloud. If a reader could not plausibly stop
there and believe it, it is not the strongest version and the section is doing no work. Shape to
fill in: "[Named body] reviewed [ingredient] in [year] and [found/ruled] [exact wording]. That is
a real finding and I am not going to pretend otherwise." Then answer with a specific checkable
fact, a date, a dollar figure, a filing, never with tone or a shrug. Verify the body and the year
on the web first — do not ship the placeholder.  2. COMPARISON TABLE. Cover the column headers. If
a stranger can still tell which column is hers from the wording of the cells, the cells are
editorialized and the table is decoration. Every cell must be a checkable fact (an ingredient
present or absent, a price, a fine, a date), not an adjective. A table that gives the villain
nothing has told her nothing.  3. FAQ + "Quick answer" lines. No question phrased so the answer is
pre-loaded ("Is it worth switching?"). Write the question in the words she actually says, then let
the Quick answer land where the evidence lands, including the one place it lands against Shannon.

> *"Most of her audience is Problem Aware → Solution Aware. They know they want cleaner products / better income. They don't know about her specifically."*

### Intent Based Branding: never argue with what the reader already intends to do

The file's own example is that Shannon does not try to convince anti-MLM people that MLM is good.
Transferred to the blog, this forbids a whole move nobody has named yet: arguing with the reader's

### The headline selection process is a four-step scored funnel, not an act of taste: write at least 20-30 variations, score each against the 4 U's (Urgency, Usefulness, Uniqueness, Ultra-specificity), read each one as the prospect scanning, and the winner is the one that creates an involuntary need to read the next line

The scoring is additive, which is the part that transfers.

> *"1. Write at least 20-30 headline variations 2. Test them against the 4 U's 3. Read each one imagining you're the prospect scanning a newspaper or inbox 4. The winner should create an almost involuntary need to read the next line ... The more U's your headline hits, the stronger it performs."*
>
> `sources/halbert-writing-system.md (section 4, Headline Mastery)`

**Use it:** Before writing any post, spend five minutes typing 20 H1 variations into a scratch list, then
score each 0-3 by ticking Usefulness / Uniqueness / Ultra-specificity. Urgency is off the board
for her, so 3 is the max and anything scoring 1 is dead. Ultra-specificity is the cheapest point
to win: it needs a named brand, a named material or mechanism, and a countable number. Worked on a
Bounce post: "Are Dryer Sheets Toxic?" = 1 (usefulness only, no brand, no number, everyone has
written it). "Are Bounce Dryer Sheets Toxic? What Women Over 40 Should Know" = 2 (usefulness, thin
uniqueness from the audience call-out; still no number, no mechanism). "Why Your Towels Still
Smell Like Bounce Three Washes Later, and What P&G Coats Them With" = 3 (usefulness, uniqueness
via a claim she has not seen elsewhere, ultra-specificity via named brand, named parent company, a
countable three washes, and a physical mechanism). Ship the 3. If nothing in the 20 scores above
2, the missing point is almost always a number, so go back to the receipts and pull the actual
figure (fine amount, recall count, wash count, year) before rewriting.

### The five-link benefit chain, run per feature, with the instruction to ask "So what?" three times until you hit the emotional payoff

The chain is: Feature -> What it does -> Why that matters -> How that makes the buyer feel -> What
that says about who they are. Most writing stops at link two; the identity link at the end is the
one that produces the close.

> *"Feature -> What it does -> Why that matters -> How that makes the buyer feel -> What that says about who they are ... Fix: For every feature, ask "So what?" three times until you reach the emotional payoff."*
>
> `sources/halbert-writing-system.md (Section B - Benefit Stack; section 16)`

**Use it:** Before drafting the closing section, run the ladder once on the swap and write all five rungs down
as literal lines, then use rung 5 as the close. Worked, for the Fabuloso post: (1) Feature: the
Switch to America floor cleaner carries no masking fragrance. (2) What it does: ten minutes after
she mops, the kitchen smells like nothing. (3) Why that matters: she can open the windows in
February and not be airing anything out. (4) How it feels: she stops bracing before she mops,
which she never noticed she was doing. (5) Identity: she is a woman who trusts her own house
again. Rungs 2 and 3 go in the H3 that follows the fragrance component; rung 4 belongs in
Shannon's Advice, in first person, because "bracing" is her read and not a fact; rung 5 IS the
closing line of the post. Enforcement check on any draft: if the last paragraph is still
describing the product, the ladder stopped at rung 2, so climb it. Keep rung 3 physical and
household ("windows open in February") rather than medical; do not write "the headache stops,"
which converts the chain into a health claim the post cannot source.

### The Section D objection map: five objections with their named counter-strategies

"It's too expensive" -> price anchoring, value comparison, ROI. "I don't have time" -> show how
little time is required. "Will this work for me?" -> social proof from similar people, guarantee.
"I've tried things like this before" -> differentiation, unique mechanism. "I can do this myself"
-> cost of DIY mistakes, time comparison. This is a complete FAQ generator, and it is built before
the draft, not after.

> *"- "I've tried things like this before" -> Differentiation, unique mechanism - "I can do this myself" -> Cost of DIY mistakes, time comparison"*
>
> `sources/halbert-writing-system.md (Section D - Objection Map)`

**Use it:** Before drafting any "Is X toxic" post, fill five FAQ slots on paper, one per Halbert objection,
each headed in her spoken words and each answered with the counter-strategy the map names. For a
Lysol Disinfecting Spray post: (1) Too expensive -> "Isn't the safer stuff way more expensive?"
Counter with price anchoring: cost per use, not shelf price, plus one concentrate that dilutes.
(2) No time -> "I don't have time to research every bottle." Counter with how little time: one
swap, one shelf, ten minutes. (3) Will it work for me -> "My mother used Lysol for forty years and
she's fine." Counter with social proof from similar people: a named woman her age whose symptom
cleared, plus the survivorship point that fine-so-far is not a safety study. (4) Tried it before
-> "I bought a green cleaner once and it didn't clean anything." Counter with unique mechanism:
name what actually does the killing in the replacement, so it is not the same category of promise.

deodorizes, it does not disinfect, so she has quietly traded a disinfectant for a smell and does
not know it. Write these five before the first paragraph, not after; slot 5 and slot 3 are the two
that never appear if you build the FAQ last.

the withheld filed fact plus the objection it kills: "P.P.P.S. Still think this is overblown? [the
one lawsuit or recall you kept out of the receipts list, with the link] That was filed [date]."
Mechanically: when you build the receipts list, mark one item RESERVED and do not cite it anywhere
in the body. The test that it worked is that the skimmer's path (headline, H2s, bold, table, P.S.
ladder) tells the whole story with the body deleted.

### The Reporter Technique, seven steps: news headline not ad headline, journalistic lead (who what where when why), story told as reported fact, quotes from subject or experts or customers, specific data throughout, build to the product as the newsworthy element, end with how to learn more

Three reasons it works are given: it does not trigger ad blindness because readers process it as
content, the editorial format lends inherent credibility, and it lets you run heavy proof and
specifics without sounding salesy.

> *"1. Write a headline that reads like a news headline (not an ad headline) ... - It doesn't trigger "ad blindness" - readers process it like content"*
>
> `sources/halbert-writing-system.md (section 7, The Reporter Technique)`

**Use it:** Steps 1, 2, 3 and 7 are already satisfied or overridden by her locked format (her posts are first-
person, so ignore step 3's third-person instruction). Only two steps change the draft. Step 4,
"Include 'quotes' from the subject, experts, or satisfied customers": she quotes the authority and
quotes herself but never quotes the accused company. In a Swiffer WetJet post, open P&G's own
product page or the SDS, lift one sentence of their own language verbatim, and set it as the line
immediately BEFORE the authority's sentence in the same H3, so the reader hits the company's claim
and then the regulator's finding back to back with no commentary in between. Their own words are
the only quote that cannot be dismissed as a critic's spin, and it costs nothing to get. Step 6,
"Build to the revelation of the product/service as the newsworthy element": the switch has to
arrive inside the story as the next fact, not as an offer after the FAQ. Concretely, name the swap
at the moment in the narrative where she found it, and let the closing CTA restate it rather than
introduce it. The why is the part to hold onto: the editorial frame is what lets the post carry a
lawsuit list, a comparison table and a product recommendation without reading as an ad, so any
sentence that breaks the reporting voice (hype adjectives, direct sell) forfeits the credibility
the format was buying.

### Trust, then Belief, then Desire, then Action, with a specific instrument assigned to each stage, and the stages cannot be reordered or skipped

This turns into a diagnostic you can run on a finished post: find each CTA, then check that all
three prior stages have fired above it. Most drafts fail at Desire, because a mechanism section
builds Belief beautifully and then the CTA fires one stage early, into a reader who understands
and does not yet want.

> *"Content builds Trust. Case studies and logic build Belief. Emotion creates Desire. Clear CTA drives Action."*
>
> `sources/marketing-business-gurus.md`

**Use it:** Run this as a 30-second audit on any finished draft. Ctrl-F both scan CTAs. Read the 150 words
directly above each one. If that text is mechanism, ingredient names, or an authority citation, it
is Belief and the CTA is firing one stage early into a reader who understands and does not yet
want. Pass-1 locks the CTA copy itself, so the only lever is the paragraph above: write three
sentences of sensory after-state there, using the same file's Kern rule 4 ("paint the 'after'
state in sensory detail"). Not benefits, not "peace of mind" — what the cabinet under her sink
looks like in six weeks, what the bathroom smells like on a Saturday morning, what she is not
doing anymore. The mid-post CTA sitting after the H3 component breakdown almost always needs this
beat; the closing CTA usually already has it, because pass-1 line 41 already forces a sensory
close. Full stage map for the audit: opening story plus her own past use of the product = Trust.
H3 components, named authorities, receipts list, and the evidence-against section = Belief. The
three-sentence beat above each CTA plus the who-she-becomes close = Desire. The two locked scan
CTAs = Action. Any CTA with no Desire beat in the 150 words above it gets one written, or the CTA
moves down to where one already exists.

### Star, Story, Solution, with the Star defined as a hero the reader identifies with

The three blanks are: [a person like her], [the struggle and the messy middle, including what she
wrongly blamed], [how the breakthrough happened]. The middle beat is the one that gets skipped,
and it is the one that does the work, because the wrong diagnosis is what the reader is currently
living inside.

> *"- Star: A hero the reader identifies with (often you, or someone like them) - Story: The struggle, the failure, the messy middle - Solution: How the breakthrough happened"*
>
> `sources/email-copywriting-guide.md`

**Use it:** Apply SSS to every per-component H3, the driest block in the post. Fill three blanks in order,
roughly 60 to 90 words total, then land the chemistry in one plain sentence at the end instead of
opening with it.  Blanks: [Star = Shannon, with her age and the room she was standing in] / [Story
= the struggle and the messy middle, the specific thing she kept doing about it] / [Solution = how
she found out, told flat].  Worked, for an H3 on methylisothiazolinone: Do not write
"Methylisothiazolinone is a preservative associated with contact dermatitis." Write: "Two winters
my knuckles split open. I blamed the furnace. I bought a thicker hand cream every October and kept
a tube by the kitchen sink. Then I switched cleaners for a reason that had nothing to do with my
hands, and by February they were fine. The preservative in that bottle, methylisothiazolinone, is
one of the most common causes of contact dermatitis on the ingredient list."  Two hard constraints
from her existing rules, both of which the formula would otherwise break: the Star is Shannon or a
documented public case, never an invented named customer and never a named private individual, so
in practice Shannon is the Star in every component H3. And the beat must be something she actually
did, not a plausible-sounding scene, so pull the specifics from her real life or ask her, do not
write the winter she never had.  Source line 73 gives the sentence-level transform to run on any
flat claim: instead of stating the fact, tell the story of the time it happened and what happened
next.

### Her native repetition device is a DOUBLET, not a triad

Pattern: [short declarative, concrete noun A]. [identical sentence, noun B swapped]. [longer third
sentence that turns and names the reader]. Two beats of drumbeat, then the turn. This is the thing
to reach for every time the rule against tricolons kills a line: she never needed three, she uses
two and then breaks the pattern.

> *""$140K is a ceiling. $300K is a ceiling. I'm looking for women who've hit theirs and know they were built for more.""*
>
> `brand-voice.md (Voice Samples, Builder / ambitious)`

**Use it:** THE PATTERN, from "$140K is a ceiling. $300K is a ceiling. I'm looking for women who've hit theirs
and know they were built for more." — three slots, not two: (1) short declarative, concrete noun
A, verb "is"; (2) the identical sentence with noun B swapped in, same length, same verb; (3) a
longer sentence that switches to "I" or "you" and stops being about the noun. Beat 3 is the job of
the line. Beats 1 and 2 only set the drum so beat 3 lands.  WHERE IT GOES: the sort-line, one line
under the answer capsule, before the first H2. One doublet per post, never two.  FILL IT IN 30
SECONDS: pick two variants of the same product that the reader believes are different (two scents,
two "sensitive skin" versions, two sizes), write the same five-word verdict about each, then break
to first person with something she did for years.  Fabuloso: "The lavender one is a fragrance. The
lemon one is a fragrance. I bought both for years and never once turned the bottle around." Dryer
sheets: "The free-and-clear box is a fragrance. The spring-meadow box is a fragrance. I ran them
through every load in this house and thought I was doing the safe one."  TWO GUARDRAILS. Beats 1
and 2 must be within a word or two of the same length, or the drum does not land, and the noun is
the ONLY thing that changes. And beat 3 has to be true: substitute a real number of years or a
real detail from her life, never an invented one, per the no-fabricated-testimony rule.

### The false-binary rule comes with its causal chain written out, and the chain is what makes it portable: both options mean the same → reads as a fake choice → people don't answer

The failure is not confusion, it is the reader detecting that the question was rhetorical, and
rhetorical questions get no engagement. The corollary is stated flatly: unclear questions get no
replies.

> *""those are the same → reads as a fake choice → people don't answer" / "Unclear questions = no replies. Clarity drives response.""*
>
> `brand-voice.md (2026-06-06 Copy rule) and knowledge/brand-voice.md (Copy Rule: No False-Binary Questions)`

**Use it:** Test every question H2 in 5 seconds: cover the second half of the question. If the first half
alone would get the same Quick answer, the H2 is a fake choice and gets rewritten. "Is Fabuloso
Toxic or Is It Safe?" fails (toxic and safe are one question asked twice, and there is no Quick
answer that faces one way). Rewrite to a question with one direction and one factual answer: "What
is actually in the purple Fabuloso?" / Quick answer: fragrance, dye and a preservative, and the
label only names one of them. When you do want a binary H2, copy the shape of Shannon's own
approved opener, where the two answers lead to different places: "Is it the fragrance or the
cleaning agent that burns your throat?" Both halves are real, both have receipts, and the Quick
answer picks one. Same move on the comparison table: read the two cells in a row side by side, and
if they say the same thing in different words ("contains fragrance" vs "has added scent"), delete
the row. Every row must show a difference she could act on that afternoon.

### The template for asking anything: [the ask] + 'not because [the flattering reason], but because [the unflattering true reason]

' The unflattering half is the whole mechanism. It is a damaging admission and a 'because' fused

> *"e.g. "Next step is a 15-minute video. It's $11 — not because the info is precious, but because free attracts watchers and I only work with builders.""*

**Use it:** TEMPLATE: [the ask], not because [the flattering reason she suspects], but because [the

Rewrite until the second clause admits something about her, her buyers, or her own results that
she would rather not say. Commas only, never an em-dash.  WHERE, exactly two slots per post, no
more: 1. The last sentence before the first free-scan CTA, replacing whatever "here's why it's
free" line is there now. Fill blank 1 with the friction the scan actually asks of her (the label
photos), blank 2 with the behavioural truth. Ready to paste, swap the count to match the live

because the woman who does one bottle puts it back and forgets, and the woman who does four goes

where it doubles as the post's required concession: "I switched the laundry first, not because
laundry is the worst offender, but because it is the thing you touch every single day and it is
the one you will actually notice."  30-SECOND CHECK before publish: read the second clause alone.
If it could appear in her own ad copy, it is flattering and the line is dead. Kill the whole
sentence rather than ship a "not because X, but because I care about you" version, which is the
failure mode this template exists to prevent.

### Her identity line has three parts and no fourth: a contraction, one concrete claim with a number in it, and zero adjectives

**Use it:** THE FORMULA (3 slots, no fourth): [contraction] + [one concrete claim containing a hard number] +

release. 3. Confirm one hard number, and confirm it is a number ALREADY ON RECORD (the 20 years;
$257/month to $17,000/month; "a couple dozen women"). Never mint a fresh number to fit the topic.

no verified number, reuse the 20 years and hang the topic off it ("in 20 years") rather than
inventing one. 4. Reject if the line has three parallel beats. The identity line is two clauses,
never three.

## MECHANICS. Small numbers, thresholds and orderings that sound trivial and are not.

### The writing block is timed at 33 minutes 33 seconds, drafted at speed with zero editing, then a walk, then a second 33:33 block, and editing is not permitted until the entire draft exists

The editing that follows is 3-4 discrete passes, each with one focus, never a single mixed sweep.

> *"4. Set a timer (33 minutes 33 seconds was his preferred interval) 5. Write fast without editing. Get the ideas down. The first draft is for capturing, not perfecting. ... He edited in 3-4 passes, each with a specific focus."*
>
> `sources/halbert-writing-system.md (section 3, Halbert's Pre-Writing Ritual)`

**Use it:** Two timed blocks, then the sweeps. BLOCK 1 (33:33, no editing, no browser): answer capsule, the
sort-line kitchen scene, and every question-form H2 with its Quick answer line under it. Every
lawsuit, fine, recall date and named authority goes in as a bracketed hole, [FDA warning letter,
date TBD], never verified mid-block. Walk. BLOCK 2 (33:33): H3 per component, comparison table,
the evidence-against section, FAQ, close on who she becomes. Shannon's Advice box is locked copy,
paste it, do not draft it. ONLY NOW open a browser and fill the brackets. Then four separate
passes, one focus each, never mixed: (1) fill and verify every bracketed receipt; (2) sweep the
vague-word and AI-tell reject list; (3) copy out just the H2s, Quick answers and bolded phrases in
order and check they read as a standalone answer; (4) read every prose paragraph out loud in her
voice at the kitchen table. The failure this kills is the real one on these posts: stopping at
word 300 to go confirm a fine amount, losing the voice, and writing the back half in a flatter
register than the opening.

### A subhead every 3 to 5 paragraphs, and each subhead is itself a mini-headline whose job is to re-hook skimmers rather than to label a section

Paragraphs are capped at 1-4 sentences. The skimmer's path is a named, ordered sequence: headline,
then subheads, then bold text, then bullets, then P.S., then price.

> *"- Subheads every 3-5 paragraphs (each one a mini-headline that re-hooks skimmers) ... They hit: headline > subheads > bold text > bullets > P.S. > price."*
>
> `sources/halbert-writing-system.md (section 13, Visual Formatting for Readability)`

**Use it:** Cadence check: no block of body copy runs more than 5 paragraphs (at her 1-2 sentence paragraphs,
about 200 words) without a new heading. Mark the draft at every 200 words; if an interval has no
heading, split it. Then the re-hook rule, applied where her format leaves room: H2s stay locked in
question form with "Quick answer:" for the FAQ schema, so the mini-headline job falls entirely on
the H3s. Never let an H3 be a label. On a Fabuloso post, "Methylisothiazolinone" becomes "The
preservative that made 63 women's hands blister"; on a Dawn post, "Fragrance" becomes "The one
word that hides 3,000 others"; on Mr. Clean, "Quaternary ammonium compounds" becomes "The
ingredient banned from hospital nurseries but not your kitchen." Each H3 has to make a skimmer
stop, and the paragraph under it has to pay off the H3's specific promise.

### The count matters more than the word

33 uses across 18 letters is about 1.8 per letter, so roughly two per piece, not one and not ten.
And the function of "anyway" is specific: it is a self-interrupt. It signals the writer wandered
off and is now walking back, and only a live person wanders. A document does not. So the word is
not folksy decoration, it is manufactured evidence that the page was spoken rather than assembled.

> *"Halbert used "anyway" 33 times in 18 letters. That's not an accident. It's conversational rhythm."*
>
> `sources/email-copywriting-guide.md`

**Use it:** DOSE: exactly two "anyway"s per post. Not one, not five. (Halbert: 33 across 18 letters = 1.8
each.)  PLACEMENT: both go on the return line out of a personal aside — the sentence where she
stops wandering and gets back to the point. Nowhere else. If you cannot name the tangent it is
walking back from, delete it.  SLOT 1, closing the kitchen scene under the answer capsule:
"Anyway. That bottle is still under my sink. I have not gotten to it."  SLOT 2, last line of the
Shannon's Advice box, coming back from the moment her belief flipped: "Anyway, that was four years
ago and I have not bought it since."  30-SECOND CHECK BEFORE SHIPPING: Ctrl+F "anyway". If the
count is not 2, fix it. For each hit, read the two sentences above it — if those sentences are
product facts, a stat, or a lawsuit, it is in the wrong place; move it or cut it. It only earns
its spot after a first-person digression.  DO NOT also load in "here's the thing" and "look" from
the same line of the source. One transitional tic used twice reads as a person. Three different
ones in one post reads as someone performing a person.

### The P

S. is read second because reading order is not document order. People sample the end before they
commit to the middle. That is the actual mechanism, and it generalises off email immediately:
whatever your reader thumbs to first is your real second element, no matter where it sits in the
file. The second half of the rule is just as mechanical: it must read like something thought of
after the writing finished, which means a P.S. that summarises the post proves it was planned and
kills its own effect.

> *"After the subject line, the PS gets the most eyeballs. People scroll to the bottom first."*
>
> `sources/email-copywriting-guide.md`

**Use it:** Treat the last two blocks on the page as first-read, not last-read, because a phone reader thumbs
to the bottom before committing to the middle. Three concrete moves: (1) Shannon's Advice box must
stand alone. It has to name the villain brand and the one action inside the box, with no pronoun
pointing back up the page. Not "so that's why I stopped buying it" but "Fabuloso hid a bleach
recall for four months. Go look under your sink tonight and read what's next to the dish soap."
(2) The FAQ answers get written as if that is the only thing she reads. First sentence of each
answer states the verdict outright, no "as mentioned above." (3) The very last line is a P.S. that
adds something the body never said, because a P.S. that recaps proves it was planned and kills its
own effect. Kill: "So remember, fragrance can hide hundreds of chemicals." Use: "P.S. I still have
half a jug of it in the garage. I use it on the outside garbage cans, which tells you exactly what
I think of it." Test before publishing: cover the whole article, read only the Advice box and the
last line, and ask whether a woman who read nothing else knows who the villain is and what to do
tonight. If not, rewrite those two, not the body.

### 75/25 is a time budget, not a slogan, and the mining image tells you the surplus is the point

You dig carloads and ship a fraction. The unused ore is what makes the shipped part sound
authoritative, because you are choosing the four best receipts out of twelve instead of stretching
the only four you found. Thin research does not read as thin research, it reads as hedged writing.

> *"**Research first** — copywriting is 75% research, 25% writing. Know your prospect's exact fears, words, and desires"*
>
> `sources/marketing-business-gurus.md`

**Use it:** Before drafting a word of an "Is X toxic" post, open a scratch file with two lists. List A: hunt
twelve verified items on the villain company (lawsuits, recalls, FDA warning letters, AG
settlements, state fines, Prop 65 notices), each with the filed figure and a live link. List B:
twenty phrasings in real women's words, pulled from her comments, Amazon 1-star reviews and Reddit
threads about that product. Then draft using only a fraction: the four strongest items become the
receipts list, the other eight set the H2 order (whichever harm shows up most across the discarded
eight becomes H2 number two) and hand you the FAQ questions; five of the twenty phrasings become
the symptom H2s and the "Quick answer" lines, verbatim. Concrete test at the end: if you wrote
"many women report" or "significant concerns," you shipped from list A of four, not twelve, and
the fix is more digging, not a better adjective.

### The second element must never restate the first, it must ADD

The worked example is exact: subject "Pamela is 77," and the forbidden preview is "How Pamela
built a business at 77," which is the same fact in more words. The test is mechanical, not
stylistic: does element two contain a fact element one does not. If not, one of the two is dead
weight occupying prime screen space.

> *"**Complement the subject. Do not repeat it.** If the subject is "Pamela is 77," the preview text should NOT be "How Pamela built a business at 77.""*
>
> `sources/kern-voice-rules.md`

**Use it:** Before publishing, list the six H2s next to their Quick answer lines. For each pair ask one
question: does the Quick answer line contain a fact the H2 does not? "Does Fabuloso contain hidden
fragrance chemicals?" + "Quick answer: Yes, the fragrance blend hides other chemicals" fails, that
is the Pamela error and it burns the two most-read lines of the section. Rewrite to "Quick answer:
Yes, and US law does not require a single one of them on the label." Rule of thumb for the fix:
spend two words on the verdict (Yes / No / Sometimes), then spend the rest of the line on a
number, a law, a year, a brand, or a body part that appears nowhere in the H2, and stop before you
explain it so the loop stays open into the H3s. Run the same test on the H1 against the answer
capsule.

### She hedges HERSELF and never the claim

"trying to clean up" is a hedge on her own progress inside a sentence that also refuses to hedge a
single fact. This is the mechanism that lets her follow the no-hedging rule and the never-assert-
your-own-credibility rule at the same time: the softening has to land on her competence, never on
the evidence.

> *""I'm Shannon, a mom, a grandma, and someone who's spent the last few years trying to clean up how I live, eat, and shop.""*
>
> `brand-voice.md (Sample 3: Soft-mom voice, join.switchtoamerica.com) and knowledge/brand-voice.md`

**Use it:** Two-place check before publishing. (1) Bio block: open with her own verb tense, still-in-progress,
never mastery. Use "I'm Shannon, a mom and a grandma, and I've spent the last few years trying to
clean up how I live, eat, and shop" and add one unfinished thing: "there's still a bottle under my
sink I haven't dealt with." Ban "expert," "mastered," "figured out," "spent years perfecting." (2)
Then Ctrl-F the draft for: reportedly, roughly, around, seems, may, might, could, possibly, some
say, in my opinion. Every hit outside the bio block and Shannon's Advice box gets deleted and
replaced with the filed number and filed date from the receipt. The single exception is
"alleged"/"allegations," which stays wherever the court record uses it. Rule of thumb: if a
sentence's subject is Shannon, a softener is allowed; if the subject is a company, a lawsuit, a
recall, or a fine, zero softeners.

### The health-claim constraint names three specific disease categories, twice, in two files

That turns a vague compliance worry into a three-word pre-publish search. And the constraint is on
EFFICACY claims, which is a narrower thing than it first reads: reporting what a regulator found
or a plaintiff alleged is not an efficacy claim, promising what the swap will do to a body is.

> *""No cancer/asthma/cardiovascular efficacy claims without substantiation files." / "No cancer, asthma, or cardiovascular efficacy claims without substantiation.""*
>
> `sources/identity.md (Role Boundaries; Compliance & Hard Constraints) and the vault CLAUDE.md FTC rules`

**Use it:** Pre-publish, Ctrl-F the draft for five words: cancer, asthma, heart/cardiovascular, hormone,
lungs. At each hit, check the VERB, not the word. Permitted verbs attach the harm to a source that
owns it: "linked to," "flagged by," "restricted in the EU," "the complaint alleges," "the recall
cited." Banned verbs attach a benefit to the swap: "causes," "treats," "prevents," "helps,"
"reduces your risk of," "clears up." Rewrite pattern, keeping the receipt and dropping the
promise: FAILS — "Switching cleaners will help your asthma." PASSES — "The 2019 complaint alleges
the company knew the propellant triggered asthma attacks and kept the formula on shelves." Second
rewrite pattern for the benefit side, straight from the vault: never claim what the product does
to her body, only what is absent from the bottle. FAILS — "improves heart health." PASSES — "free
of the propellant named in that complaint." Apply this hardest in the Shannon's Advice box, where
her blunt first-person voice is the exact place a "this is poisoning you" sentence turns into an
efficacy claim: keep it framed as her opinion about the villain's conduct, never as a prediction
about the reader's body. If a hit cannot be rewritten to a permitted verb, cut the sentence rather
than soften it.

### The follow-up formula says the second contact must reference the first, add NEW value, and make the ask EASIER, explicitly not repeat it

That transfers directly to a page carrying two CTAs for the same offer, which is otherwise just
the same ask twice.

> *""**No-response follow-up:** reference previous note + add NEW value + make the ask EASIER (not repeat).""*
>
> `sources/voice-profile.md (Email frameworks, from Vox)`

**Use it:** Write the two CTA lead-ins as a first contact and a follow-up, not as two copies. Button copy
stays locked; only the sentence above it changes. CTA 1 (mid-post) = whole-outcome ask: name what
the scan tells her about every product under her sink and what it will ask of her in return. CTA 2
(closing block, after receipts + FAQ) = all three follow-up parts in order. (1) REFERENCE: point
at what she just read, by its specific detail, not "as you saw above" — "That recall number you
just scrolled past is on a bottle in nine out of ten kitchens." (2) NEW VALUE: give her one fact
the first CTA did not have, earned from the receipts section — the scan flags that exact brand by
name, or it covers the category the lawsuit was filed in. (3) EASIER ASK: shrink the job from the
house to one object — "You don't have to do the whole house. Scan the one bottle you were holding

> *"**builder-landing:** No fake line. Weakest is "Want income that builds while you sleep, not more hours" (line 65) — a guru cliché, but it wouldn't get screenshotted."*

## LINES WORTH STEALING THE STRUCTURE OF.

### The Direct Address opening: a run of short flat second-person sentences listing what she already tried, each one a real action, closing on the thing that still is not working

"You try everything. You watch the YouTube videos. You buy the courses. You show up to the
networking events. And still, your phone isn't ringing with qualified leads..." The structural
move is that every line is an effort she made, so the failure at the end cannot be read as her
fault. There are four other openings in the same section (Story Lead, Startling Fact, Question
Hook, and the If/Then Qualifier: "If you're a [role] between [age] and [age] who's [built
something] but feels like [gap], the next [N] minutes could change everything").

> *""You try everything. You watch the YouTube videos. You buy the courses. You show up to the networking events. And still, your phone isn't ringing with qualified leads...""*
>
> `sources/halbert-writing-system.md (section 5, Opening Strategies, The Direct Address)`

**Use it:** SLOT: first paragraph of body copy, immediately under the answer capsule, before the first
question-form H2.  PATTERN (fill 4 blanks + 1): "You [effort 1]. You [effort 2]. You [effort 3,
the one that cost money]. You [effort 4, the one she did for a specific family member or room].
And [the symptom, in her own out-loud words, with a time of day on it]."  Rules for filling it:
all four efforts must be things SHE did, never things she failed to know; keep them physical and
household-scale (opening a window, changing a filter, buying the "free and clear" one), never
research-shaped ("you read the label"); make effort 3 the one she paid for, so the failure has a
receipt attached; put a clock on the closing symptom so it lands as a lived fact, not a claim.
WORKED, Glade PlugIns post: "You open the windows. You change the furnace filter. You buy the one
that says fresh linen instead of the sweet one. You unplug the one in Katie's room because that
one bothered her. And your head still hurts by four o'clock every afternoon."  WHY IT IS FOUR AND
NOT THREE: pass-1 bans three parallel beats as an AI tricolon. Four short "You…" sentences read as
inventory, not rhythm, so this is the one place in the post where parallel structure is safe.  THE
MOVE IT BUYS: every sentence before the problem is an action she already took, so there is no
grammatical slot left for the reader to hear "you should have known." That satisfies "never imply
she was careless" by construction instead of by vigilance.  DO NOT: use the If/Then Qualifier
opening on a blog post. "If you're a woman between 40 and 60 who…" is a self-selecting sales lead
and collides with pass-1's "never write a 'this is for you' line that is true of every reader" and
"never name the reader as a group." Keep If/Then in ads only. The other three (Story Lead,
Startling Fact, Question Hook) are live options: Startling Fact pairs naturally with the answer
capsule, Question Hook duplicates work the question-form H2s already do.

### The four-beat objection preempt, said before the reader consciously forms the objection: name the thought in her words, grant that it is reasonable, admit you had it yourself, then pivot to what changed your mind

"Now, I know what you might be thinking. This sounds too good to be true. I thought the same thing
when I first heard about it. But here's what convinced me..." The why is that an objection you say
first is disarmed, while the same objection formed silently by the reader is never answered.

> *""Now, I know what you might be thinking. This sounds too good to be true. I thought the same thing when I first heard about it. But here's what convinced me...""*
>
> `sources/halbert-writing-system.md (section 8, Predicting and Overcoming Objections)`

**Use it:** Use it as the FIRST paragraph of the counter-evidence H2, where the neutral "critics say" survey
would otherwise go. Fill four slots: (1) "Here's the part I know you're thinking, because I
thought it too." (2) The objection in HER exact words, never paraphrased into fair-minded
language, and it is always the same objection for a household-product post: someone she loves used
this for decades and is fine. (3) The grant, one sentence, no "but" in it. (4) The pivot: "Here's
what changed my mind anyway." Then run the real counter-evidence honestly under it. Worked for a
Bounce post: "Here's the part I know you're thinking, because I thought it. My mother dried towels
with these for thirty years and she's seventy-nine and fine. That's not nothing and I'm not going
to pretend it is. Here's what got me anyway." Then the honest limits (small studies, no long-term
human data on the fragrance load) land as candor instead of hedging, which is what earns the
Shannon's Advice box the right to be blunt two sections later. Reuse the identical four slots as
the opening of the FAQ answer to "My mom used these her whole life and she's fine, so why should I
care?" — same structure, tightened to two sentences so the FAQ still reads as an answer and not an
essay.

### Future pacing runs in present tense with sensory language, and the technique inside the technique is that the state is never named, it is proved by one small physical action

"Picture yourself waking up Monday morning. You check your phone. 12 new qualified appointments
are already on your calendar. You pour your coffee slowly because you know the business is
handling itself..." The line does not say 'you feel relaxed.' It says the coffee gets poured
slowly, and 'because' does the rest.

> *""Picture yourself waking up Monday morning. You check your phone. 12 new qualified appointments are already on your calendar. You pour your coffee slowly because you know the business is handling itself...""*
>
> `sources/halbert-writing-system.md (section 8, Future Pacing)`

**Use it:** Use it for the final "who she becomes" section. Fill this pattern: [present tense, one ordinary
errand with a date] + [one small physical action, done slowly or differently] + "because" + [the
changed fact about her house] + [one thing she no longer does]. Never write "you feel safe" or
"you have peace of mind." Worked, for a Febreze or Glade post: "It's a Tuesday in October. You
come in from the car with a grocery bag on each arm and you stop in the doorway, because the house
doesn't smell like anything. You put the bags down slowly. You don't open a window." The slow put-
down is Halbert's coffee pour; the "because" carries the state so the sentence never has to name
it. Also usable in the answer capsule's last line. Two rules to respect while filling it: no em-

Its shape is: rhetorical question naming a common behaviour, then an anticlimactic and specific
answer. The gap opens because the reader instantly checks herself against the behaviour before she
has decided to read the piece. The anticlimax is essential, a dramatic payoff would close the gap.

> *"GOOD: "You know what most people do after a 3-day bootcamp? They close their laptop and forget everything by Tuesday.""*
>
> `sources/email-copywriting-guide.md`

**Use it:** OPENER TEMPLATE, Shannon-aimed only: "You know what I did the first time [somebody told me / I
read that] [her product] was the problem? [Small, unheroic, specific thing I did anyway]. Then
[the thing that made it worse]." Never "you know what most women do" — that names the reader as a
group and paints her naive, both banned. FABULOSO: "You know what I did the first time somebody
told me Fabuloso was the problem? I finished the bottle. Then I bought the lavender one because it
was on sale." SHAMPOO: "You know what I did the first time I read the label on my shampoo? I put
it back in the shower and washed my hair with it that night." PLACEMENT: this is the first line of
the opening story beat, which sits BELOW the answer capsule, not above it. Pass-1 requires the
