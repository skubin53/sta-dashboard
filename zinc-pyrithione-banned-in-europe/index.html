Why Is Zinc Pyrithione Banned in Europe?

What Europe's rule really says, why the same ingredient is still in American dandruff shampoo, how the bottle is different over there, and what you can use instead.

By Shannon Nicole, founder of Switch to America. Seven years helping women switch to a toxin-free home, and a health coach for over 20 years. Published October 10, 2026.

Europe banned zinc pyrithione in shampoo on March 1, 2022, because it carries a hazard label: toxic for reproduction. Its own safety panel said it can be considered safe in rinse-off shampoo up to 1 percent. It was banned anyway, because no one proved it was the only good choice for dandruff. In the US it is still sold as an over-the-counter dandruff drug, at up to 2 percent in shampoo you rinse out.

Europe banned zinc pyrithione for its hazard label, not because a study found that shampoo hurt anyone. No one proved it was the only good choice for dandruff.

Why is zinc pyrithione banned in Europe?

Quick answer: Europe gave it a hazard label, and Europe's beauty law bans chemicals with that label.

Zinc pyrithione is the dandruff fighter in many dandruff shampoos.

Europe sorts some chemicals into a group called CMR. These are chemicals that can cause cancer, can change genes, or are toxic for reproduction. There are three levels: 1A, 1B and 2. The level depends on how strong the evidence is.

A 2020 rule, Delegated Regulation (EU) 2020/1182, gave zinc pyrithione its label: toxic for reproduction, level 1B.

Europe's beauty law says chemicals in the CMR group are banned from cosmetics, unless strict conditions are met. So on October 29, 2021, the European Commission adopted Regulation (EU) 2021/1902. It put zinc pyrithione on the list of substances banned in cosmetic products. The rule says, "It shall apply from 1 March 2022."

Source: Commission Regulation (EU) 2021/1902, October 29, 2021, recitals 1 to 5 and 8, Article 2 and the Annex.

Did Europe's own experts say it was unsafe in shampoo?

Quick answer: no. Its safety panel said it can be considered safe in rinse-off shampoo up to 1 percent.

Europe has a science panel for beauty products. It is called the Scientific Committee on Consumer Safety. On March 3 and 4, 2020, it concluded that zinc pyrithione "can be considered safe when used as an anti-dandruff ingredient in rinse-off hair products" up to 1 percent.

A request to keep it in rinse-off dandruff shampoo, up to 1 percent, was filed on April 11, 2019. No one asked to keep any other use.

To win that exception, the law sets several conditions. Two of them matter here. No suitable other ingredient can be available. And the panel must find it safe.

It passed the safety test. It failed the other one. In the rule's words, "it has not been established that there are no suitable alternative substances available."

So the ban is a hazard rule. Europe's rule does not say that shampoo hurt anyone.

Source: Commission Regulation (EU) 2021/1902, recitals 5, 7 and 8.

Why is zinc pyrithione still allowed in the US?

Quick answer: here it is sold as an over-the-counter dandruff drug, and the rule allows up to 2 percent.

In the US, a dandruff shampoo with zinc pyrithione is an over-the-counter drug. The FDA rule, 21 CFR 358.710, lists "Pyrithione zinc, 0.3 to 2 percent when formulated to be applied and then washed off after brief exposure." For products left on the scalp, it allows 0.1 to 0.25 percent.

This part of the rule was written in 1991. Its last change was in 1994.

That top US limit, 2 percent, is double the 1 percent Europe's panel looked at.

A rule like this tells you what a company is allowed to sell. It is not proof that the ingredient is safe.

The two sides ask different questions. Europe asks: does it carry a hazard label, and can something else do the job? The US rule asks: is it on the list, at an allowed amount?

Is the shampoo different in Europe?

Quick answer: for Head and Shoulders Classic Clean, yes. The German bottle is made without zinc pyrithione.

Head and Shoulders Classic Clean is a good example. On the brand's own German website, Classic Clean shampoo is made with piroctone olamine. Zinc pyrithione is not on its ingredient list.

In the US, the Classic Clean drug label posted on DailyMed lists "Pyrithione zinc 1%" as the active ingredient. The same US label also lists piroctone olamine, as an inactive ingredient.

Same brand. Same name. The German bottle leaves zinc pyrithione out. The US bottle keeps it. That does not prove the German formula is better. It does show the company knows how to make it without zinc pyrithione.

Here is the company's side, in its own words. On its India website, Head and Shoulders says zinc pyrithione shampoos "are generally safe for regular or maintenance use" on oily and dandruff-prone scalps.

Sources: Head and Shoulders Germany, Classic Clean Shampoo product page; DailyMed, Head and Shoulders Classic Clean (pyrithione zinc) shampoo label, The Procter and Gamble Manufacturing Company, published September 18, 2026; Head and Shoulders India, Zinc Pyrithione Shampoo: How This Ingredient Fights Dandruff.

Does the ban mean my shampoo hurt me?

Quick answer: the ban is a fact. What it did to you is not known.

Europe banned it for a hazard label. It did not ban it because of a study of women washing their hair.

Still, think about how you use it. Nobody buys dandruff shampoo for one week. It is easy to keep the same bottle in the shower for years.

Europe decided zinc pyrithione does not belong in its shampoo. You can make that same choice at home.

What should I use instead?

Quick answer: a gentle shampoo made without zinc pyrithione, from a company that will tell you what is in it.

  • Pick a gentle shampoo made without zinc pyrithione. You do not need a harsh one to have a clean scalp.
  • Buy from a company you can reach. It should tell you what is in the bottle, and why.
  • Ask your current brand one question. Do you sell this same formula in Europe? If not, ask why.
  • Give your scalp some time. The first few washes can feel different.
