What Does "Clinically Shown" Mean on a Supplement?

What the words are supposed to mean, who checks them and who does not, what a jury found about one famous memory pill, and the questions to ask before you pay.

By Shannon Nicole, founder of Switch to America. Seven years helping women switch to a toxin-free home, and a health coach for over 20 years. Published October 6, 2026.

"Clinically shown" means the company says a study in people backs up its claim. It is the company's own claim, and the FDA does not approve it before the box is sold. In 2024, a federal jury found that Prevagen's "clinically shown to improve memory" was not backed by competent and reliable science.

"Clinically shown" is the company's claim about its own proof. It is not a government stamp of approval.

What does "clinically shown" mean?

Quick answer: it means the company says studies in people prove its claim.

A clinical study is a study done in people. So "clinically shown" is a promise about proof.

The FTC puts it simply. Companies selling health products "must have at least the level of support that they claim to have." If the box says a study showed it, the study has to show it.

Pictures can make the same promise. The FTC says a brochure showing doctors in white lab coats and a stack of medical journals likely tells you a product was clinically proven, even without those words.

Source: FTC, Health Products Compliance Guidance, December 2022, section on ads that refer to a specific level of support, and Example 1.

Who checks a "clinically shown" claim before it is sold?

Quick answer: the FDA does not approve it first, and the company does not have to send the FDA its study.

Supplements fall under a 1994 law called DSHEA. Under it, the FDA says it "does not have the authority to approve dietary supplements before they are marketed."

Claims about how a supplement affects the body are called structure/function claims. The FDA says they "are not pre-approved by FDA."

The company must have proof. It must tell the FDA the words of the claim within 30 days after it starts selling the product with that claim. Under the rule, 21 CFR 101.93, it signs that it has proof. It does not have to send the study.

The government's own watchdog flagged this in 2012. The HHS Inspector General wrote that companies "do not have to submit the substantiation to FDA." It asked the FDA to seek the power to review that proof. Its tracker lists that request as "Closed Unimplemented."

What does "This statement has not been evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration" mean?

It means the FDA has not checked the claim. The law requires this note, word for word, with a structure/function claim: "This statement has not been evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration. This product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease."

So a box can say "clinically shown" in big letters and carry that small note too. The FDA says the FTC is generally in charge of supplement ads. And the FTC can take a company to court, as you will see below.

Sources: FDA, Questions and Answers on Dietary Supplements; FDA, Structure/Function Claims; 21 CFR 101.93; HHS Office of Inspector General, Dietary Supplements: Structure/Function Claims Fail To Meet Federal Requirements, October 2, 2012.

What does the FTC say "clinically shown" has to mean?

Quick answer: good studies in people, run the right way, that show a real result for the claim on the box.

The FTC's Health Products Compliance Guidance, from December 2022, says health claims need "competent and reliable scientific evidence." "As a general matter," that means "randomized, controlled human clinical testing." People are split by chance into two groups. One group gets the product. The other, the control group, does not.

Here is what the FTC looks for, in plain words:

  • A real difference. The product group must beat the control group by more than chance. If not, the FTC says the change may be just the placebo effect, time, or chance.
  • A test of what the ad promises. In one FTC example, a brain training program claimed "clinically proven to improve memory." Its study tested only one kind of memory. That does not prove the bigger promise.
  • The product, not just one ingredient. For a drink that touts a "clinically tested ingredient," the FTC says the company should check whether experts would want the drink itself tested.
  • No slicing the data afterward. Looking at "smaller subgroups" after a study "doesn't generally provide reliable evidence to substantiate a claim," the FTC says. It can be a sign of "data mining or 'p-hacking.'"
  • A second, independent study helps. Repeating the research "in an independently-conducted study adds to the weight of the evidence."
  • Published is not proven. A published study "isn't a guarantee of quality or proof that the product is effective for the advertised benefit."

Source: FTC, Health Products Compliance Guidance, December 2022, including Examples 18 and 35.

What happened when a jury looked at "clinically shown" on Prevagen?

Quick answer: a federal jury found the memory claims, including "clinically shown to improve memory," were not backed by competent and reliable science.

In January 2017, the FTC and New York's attorney general sued Quincy Bioscience, the company behind Prevagen. Their complaint alleged the following. These were allegations, not findings:

  • The company relied mainly on one study of 218 people.
  • That study did not beat a dummy pill by a statistically significant amount on any of nine computer tests of memory and thinking.
  • The researchers then ran more than 30 more analyses on smaller slices of the group. The complaint said most of those still showed no significant difference.

That is the pattern the FTC's guidance warns about.

In 2024 the case went to trial. The jury found that none of the eight memory statements challenged at trial "was supported by competent and reliable scientific evidence." One was "Prevagen is clinically shown to improve memory."

On November 18, 2024, Judge Louis L. Stanton ordered the company to "immediately remove" all eight. In December 2024 he made clear that applies nationwide. The order covers the claims, not the sale of the product.

Here is the other side, straight. The jury found only two of the eight "materially misleading." Both were about memory problems that come with aging. The judge wrote that "there was no evidence that Prevagen, or the challenged statements, had actually caused harm or economic injury." The company appealed, and three appeals judges heard the case on February 26, 2026.

What should I ask before I trust "clinically shown"?

Quick answer: ask who ran the study, how many people were in it, and whether it worked for the whole group.

  1. Who paid for the study, and who ran it? If only the company tested it, ask if anyone else got the same result.
  2. How many people, and for how long? The FTC looks at the size and length of a study. A few people for a few weeks tells you less.
  3. Did it beat a dummy pill? Without a control group, you cannot tell the product from the placebo effect, time, or chance.
  4. Did it work for the whole group, or only a slice? A win found in a small subgroup after the study ended does not generally prove the claim.
  5. Was it this product, or one ingredient? A study on one ingredient is not a test of the whole product.
  6. Does the study match the promise? A test of one kind of memory does not prove a product helps you find your keys.

Common questions about "clinically shown"

Does "clinically shown" mean the FDA approved the supplement?

No. The FDA says it does not approve dietary supplements before they are sold. Claims about how a supplement affects the body are not pre-approved by the FDA either. That is why the law requires the note "This statement has not been evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration."

Is "clinically tested" the same as "clinically shown"?

The words are different, but the FTC says they can send the same message. It says a phrase like "clinically tested ingredient" can imply that the test proved a benefit, not just that a test was done. So a company that says "clinically tested" may need proof of the benefit, too.

Is one study enough to say "clinically shown"?

The FTC does not set a number of studies. It says the quality of the research matters more than how many studies there are. It also says a second study by independent researchers adds weight. And a good result found only in small slices of the group, after the study, does not generally prove a claim.

What did the jury find about Prevagen's "clinically shown" claim?

In 2024, a federal jury found that none of the eight memory statements challenged at trial, including "clinically shown to improve memory," was supported by competent and reliable scientific evidence. The judge ordered all eight removed. He also wrote there was no evidence that Prevagen had caused harm. The company appealed.

Sources for these answers: FDA, Questions and Answers on Dietary Supplements; FDA, Structure/Function Claims; FTC Health Products Compliance Guidance (2022); Memorandum and Judgment (2024).
