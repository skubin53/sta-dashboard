Do Clorox Wipes Have Bleach in Them?

What is really in the tub on your counter, in Clorox's own words, how Clorox says to use it, and a simple swap for everyday messes.

By Shannon Nicole, founder of Switch to America. Seven years helping women switch to a toxin-free home, and a health coach for over 20 years. Published October 21, 2026.

Bleach-free is not the same as chemical-free. A Clorox wipe swaps bleach for two quats, a kind of germ killer that counts as a pesticide.

Do Clorox wipes have bleach?

Quick answer: no. Clorox answers that question on its own product page, and the answer starts with one word: "No."

The page is for Clorox Disinfecting Cleaning Wipes, Crisp Lemon Scent. Its FAQ asks, "Do Clorox Disinfecting Cleaning Wipes have bleach?" Clorox says they are "made with a bleach-free formula that's available in different scents."

The same page calls the wipes "a bleach-free, all-in-one solution."

So why do so many of us think they are bleach? The name on the tub is the same name on the bleach jug under the sink. It is an easy mix-up. But they are two very different products.

Source: Clorox, Clorox Disinfecting Cleaning Wipes, Crisp Lemon Scent, product page and FAQ, read October 21, 2026.

What is actually in a Clorox wipe?

Quick answer: water, two quats, two solvents, a grease cutter, baking soda, citric acid and fragrance, by Clorox's own list.

The Clorox product page links to a full ingredient list on SmartLabel. The list says Clorox updated it on April 8, 2025. It is for Clorox Disinfecting Wipes, Bleach Free Cleaning Wipes, Crisp Lemon, 75 count. Here is each ingredient, with what Clorox says it does.

Clorox's safety data sheet for the Crisp Lemon wipes, dated August 22, 2024, also lists two quats, at 0.184 percent each. It gives the wipes an EPA registration number, 5813-79.

If there is no bleach, what kills the germs?

Quick answer: the two quats. Clorox's ingredient pages say each one can work as "a bleach-free antimicrobial agent." Antimicrobial means germ killer.

Quats are a big family of chemicals, and many of them are used to kill germs. A California Department of Public Health fact sheet for employers, from February 2017, says the most common chemicals in wipes are quats. The same sheet says most disinfectant wipes contain asthmagens. It describes those as chemicals that can cause asthma even in people who have never had asthma before.

The National Pesticide Information Center at Oregon State University says quats "are considered pesticides." It counts "over 2,000 pesticide products containing quats." It also says antimicrobial products "should never be used on people."

Most of the research on quats is about people who clean for work, and the results are mixed. I walk through it, study by study, in Are quats in cleaners safe?

Sources: California Department of Public Health, Disinfectants and Work-Related Asthma: Information for Employers, February 2017; National Pesticide Information Center, Quaternary Ammonium Compounds (ADBAC and DDAC) Fact Sheet.

What is the difference between Clorox bleach and Clorox wipes?

Quick answer: Clorox bleach kills germs with sodium hypochlorite. Clorox Disinfecting Wipes have no bleach and use quats.

That gas warning is about the bleach jug. But the CDC says not to mix products or chemicals with each other, so keep the wipes away from bleach too. How does Clorox say to use its wipes?

Quick answer: keep the surface wet for 4 minutes to disinfect, then let it dry.

Clorox's own directions say: "Wipe surface with enough wipes for treated surface to remain visibly wet for 4 minutes for disinfecting." Then, "Let the surface dry."

Those steps are for "hard, nonporous, non-food-contact surfaces."

So a quick swipe that dries in a few seconds does not follow Clorox's own steps for disinfecting.

Clorox also says to avoid using the wipes on skin. And it says to skip "unsealed wood, untreated stone, mirrors or inside appliances."

Source: Clorox, Clorox Disinfecting Cleaning Wipes, Crisp Lemon Scent, How to use and FAQ.

What can I use instead of Clorox wipes?

Quick answer: for everyday messes, a damp cloth with warm, soapy water. Open a window, and save a disinfectant for when someone is sick.

The CDC says, "Using household cleaners that contain soap or detergent will remove germs and dirt on surfaces." It also says, "Cleaning alone removes most harmful viruses or bacteria from surfaces."

The California health fact sheet makes the same point about wipes. It says they are often used "when water with a paper towel or cloth would do, like for cleaning up a coffee spill."

Here is the simple swap:

  • Counters and sinks, every day: a washable cloth, warm water and a little plain soap. Rinse the cloth and use it again.
  • Glass and hard-water spots: plain white vinegar, on its own. Never with bleach.
  • When someone is sick: that is when the CDC says to disinfect. Use one product by itself.
  • Every time you clean: open a window or run a fan. The CDC says not to mix products.
  • When you buy a cleaner: pick one that lists every ingredient, has no quats, and skips added fragrance.

Sources: CDC, When and How to Clean and Disinfect Your Home; California Department of Public Health, Disinfectants and Work-Related Asthma, February 2017.

Common questions about Clorox wipes

Are Clorox wipes the same as bleach?

No. Clorox says its Disinfecting Wipes are made with a bleach-free formula. Clorox bleach kills germs with sodium hypochlorite. The wipes kill germs with two quats instead.

What kills germs in a Clorox wipe?

Two quats. Clorox's ingredient list names alkyl C12-14 dimethylethylbenzyl ammonium chloride and alkyl C12-18 dimethylbenzyl ammonium chloride. Clorox says each one works as a germ killer and a cleaner.

How long does a surface need to stay wet with a Clorox wipe?

Clorox says to use enough wipes so the surface stays visibly wet for 4 minutes to disinfect it, then let it dry. A quick swipe that dries in a few seconds does not follow those steps.

Can I use Clorox wipes on my hands or skin?

Clorox says to avoid using them on skin. The National Pesticide Information Center says germ-killing products made for surfaces are pesticides and should never be used on people.

Sources for these answers: Clorox product page and FAQ; Clorox ingredient list on SmartLabel; National Pesticide Information Center, Quats Fact Sheet.

What can I do this week?

Tonight, move the wipes tub off the counter and set a stack of clean cloths in its place. For one week, wipe down with warm, soapy water and open a window while you do it. Save a disinfectant for a day when someone in the house is sick.
