Is There a Limit for Arsenic in Candy?

Who draws the line for arsenic in candy, whether Florida found the dangerous kind, and what to put in the bowl instead.

By Shannon Nicole, founder of Switch to America. Seven years helping women switch to a toxin-free home, and a health coach for over 20 years. Published October 22, 2026.

No. I found no US federal limit for arsenic in candy, and the FDA's own arsenic page lists none. Florida found arsenic in 28 of the 46 candies it tested. But its posted results do not say if it was inorganic arsenic, the kind the FDA calls more dangerous.

A missing limit is not a finding that candy is safe or unsafe. It only means no federal agency has drawn the line.

Is there a federal limit for arsenic in candy?

Quick answer: no. I found no federal limit, and the FDA's arsenic page has no candy entry.

The FDA has a page called "Arsenic in Food and Dietary Supplements." It lists the arsenic rules and guidance the agency has put out. Bottled water has a limit of 10 parts per billion. The page lists action levels for two foods. One is apple juice, with an action level of 10 parts per billion for inorganic arsenic, set in June 2023. Candy is not on the page.

An action level is not a law. The FDA says it tells companies the level at which the agency may take enforcement action. In its own words, "Unlike regulations, guidance is not legally binding for the FDA or industry."

The FDA also keeps a general list of action levels for food, issued in August 2000. It has entries for lead and mercury. It has no entry for arsenic at all.

No limit does not mean the FDA cannot act. That same list says that where no action level exists, the FDA "may take legal action against the product at the minimal detectable level of the contaminant." But that is case by case. It is not a promise about the candy in your bowl.

Sources: FDA, Arsenic in Food and Dietary Supplements, content current as of March 5, 2024; FDA, Action Level for Inorganic Arsenic in Apple Juice, June 2023; FDA, Action Levels for Poisonous or Deleterious Substances in Human Food and Animal Feed, August 2000.

Who draws the line for arsenic in candy?

Quick answer: one state ran its own test, the FDA has no candy number, and the candy makers' trade group disputes the test.

Is the arsenic Florida found the dangerous kind?

Quick answer: the state does not say. Its results give one arsenic number for each candy where it found arsenic. They never say if it was inorganic.

Arsenic comes in two main forms. The FDA calls them inorganic and organic. Here, "organic" has nothing to do with organic farming. The FDA says organic arsenic compounds "contain arsenic with carbon."

The FDA says, "Current research indicates that inorganic arsenic is more dangerous than organic arsenic." It also says some studies have shown that two organic forms found in certain foods "may be a health concern." The FDA says it will keep watching both forms.

The FDA says it "tests for total arsenic levels in foods." If needed, it can test samples further to sort out the forms.

Florida's results page and its download table give one column, "Arsenic," in parts per billion. I read both, and the state's FAQ. None of them says whether the candy numbers are total arsenic or inorganic arsenic. So the honest answer is that the state has not said.

Here is what the state does say. Arsenic beyond safe limits "can increase the risk of cancers, including lung, skin, kidney, and bladder."

What does "parts per billion" mean?

It means parts of arsenic in every billion parts of candy. Florida's numbers ran from 180 to 570 parts per billion.

Sources: FDA, Arsenic in Food and Dietary Supplements; Florida Department of Health, Exposing Candy Toxins results page and candy results table; Healthy Florida First FAQ.

How did Florida decide how much candy is too much?

Quick answer: its FAQ lists drinking water, European and federal health yardsticks. It names no candy standard.

The state's FAQ lists the tools it uses to judge risk. One is "EPA enforceable Safe Drinking Water Standards." Others are health-based levels from the European Food Safety Authority and risk tools from the federal Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry. It also lists EPA exposure numbers for different ages.

For each candy where it found arsenic, the state then posted a rough "safe" amount per year. I did not find the math behind each number on its results page or in its FAQ. Here are three Hershey candies from its list, with the state's numbers for an adult:

  • Jolly Rancher Hard Candy Sour Apple: 540 parts per billion. About 15 pieces a year.
  • Twizzlers Strawberry: 500 parts per billion. Under 10 pieces a year.
  • Kit Kat: 230 parts per billion. About 6 pieces a year.

The top number on the whole list was 570 parts per billion, for Tootsie Fruit Chew Lime. These are the state's own estimates. They are not a federal rule.

Sources: Healthy Florida First FAQ; Florida Department of Health, candy results table; Office of the Governor news release, January 26, 2026.

What do the candy makers say about Florida's test?

Quick answer: they say the state used the wrong test. That is their claim.

The National Confectioners Association speaks for candy makers. In February 2026, Confectionery Production quoted a top official of the group. He said Florida's arsenic claims "are based on a testing method designed for environmental samples like soil and water." The article reports the group's claim that this pushed results up by as much as 3,800%.

Florida's FAQ says its labs are accredited and follow EPA, FAO, AOAC and ISO 17025 methods. I could not find a state document that answers the soil and water point. I found nothing that settles it.

Here is where I land. The fight is over the test. No federal limit settles it, and the bag in your hand does not either.

What should I put in the candy bowl instead?

Quick answer: treats from a farm you know, or ones you make yourself.

Find a farm stand near you that sells maple candy. Ask the farmer how they make it. A farmer who is proud of it will tell you.

Or make your own. A pan of caramel corn takes one evening. Tie each handful up in cellophane with a ribbon.

Some parents toss anything homemade from a house they do not know. So save the homemade bags for the grandkids and the neighbors who know your porch.
