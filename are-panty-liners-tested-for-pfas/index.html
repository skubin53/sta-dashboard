Is Anyone Testing Panty Liners for Forever Chemicals?

What the federal rule for pads asks before a liner is sold, what California bans, and what the Carefree lawsuit claims.

By Shannon Nicole, founder of Switch to America. Seven years helping women switch to a toxin-free home, and a health coach for over 20 years. Published November 3, 2026. Updated November 20, 2026.

Not in the way you might hope. No federal rule requires a PFAS test before a liner is sold. California is the state that bans added PFAS in period products. And the Carefree case is where a test claim is now in court.

On the shelf is not the same as tested for forever chemicals.

Does a federal rule require a PFAS test before a liner is sold?

Quick answer: no. The federal rule for pads lets most liners go on sale with no filing first.

A liner is a thin pad. So the rule that covers it is the federal rule for pads. There are two. One is 21 CFR 884.5435, for unscented pads. The other is 21 CFR 884.5425, for scented ones.

Both put pads "made of common cellulosic and synthetic material with an established safety profile" in Class I. Cellulosic means made from plant fiber. That wording is how the rule sorts pads. It does not mean a lab tested them for PFAS.

For those pads, both rules say the same thing. They are "exempt from the premarket notification procedures." That means the maker does not have to file anything before the pad goes on sale.

There are limits, set out in 21 CFR 884.9. A maker must still file if its pad is meant for a different use, or works in a different basic way, than pads already sold.

Look at what is not in those rules. None of them is a lab test for forever chemicals.

Sources: 21 CFR 884.5435; 21 CFR 884.5425; 21 CFR 884.9, read on eCFR.

Is anyone testing panty liners for forever chemicals?

Quick answer: California bans added PFAS in period products. And a lawsuit says a lab found PFOA in one brand.

What does California's law do?

California has its own law, AB 2515. Since January 1, 2025, it bans selling period products with PFAS "that a manufacturer has intentionally added." Starting January 1, 2027, it also counts PFAS found in a product at or above a set limit.

So until 2027, the ban turns on what a company added on purpose.

What does the Carefree lawsuit claim?

On October 9, 2026, hundreds of California residents sued the companies behind Carefree liners in Los Angeles County, Manufacturing Dive reported. They claim a 2024 independent lab test found PFOA in one or more of the liner's five layers. They also claim the companies kept using it after that. These are claims, not court findings.

Essity now owns Carefree in North America. An Essity spokesperson said, "We do not intentionally add PFAS chemicals to our products." The spokesperson also said, "Carefree stands behind its products." Essity plans to "vigorously respond," the report says.

What should I wear instead?

Quick answer: a liner with no PFAS in any layer, from a company that tests for it and will say so in writing.

  1. Ask before you buy. Ask the company two things. Do you test every layer for PFAS? Will you say so in writing? If the answer is no, skip it.
  2. Skip added scent. Pick a liner with no added scent or deodorant. Fewer extras means fewer things to ask about.
  3. Speak up about a reaction. If a liner gives you a rash or itching, stop using it and call your doctor.

One more thing. Do not take a liner being on the shelf as proof it was tested for PFAS. Ask the company.
