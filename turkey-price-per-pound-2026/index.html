How Much Does a Frozen Turkey Cost Per Pound in 2026?

The USDA store ad price, how it compares to last year, why the wholesale price is up, and where to find a price from a real person.

By Shannon Nicole, founder of Switch to America. Seven years helping women switch to a toxin-free home, and a health coach for over 20 years. Published November 6, 2026.

A turkey ad price is a sale number for one week. A farmer's price comes with a name and a face.

How much does a frozen turkey cost per pound right now?

Quick answer: about $1.26 a pound, on average, in store ads.

Every week, the USDA's Agricultural Marketing Service checks grocery store ads for turkey. Its report dated Friday, October 30, 2026, covers ads ending October 24 to November 5, 2026.

For a regular frozen whole turkey, it lists an average of $1.26 a pound across 3,037 stores. That is the same for birds under 16 pounds and over 16 pounds. The week before, it was $1.30 a pound across 2,465 stores.

The USDA says the report "reflects prices advertised by major grocery retailers to consumers through circulars and digital promotions." So it is an ad price, not a receipt. It can change every week. It is not what any one store charges.

The average is weighted by store count. A price that ran in more stores counts for more.

Source: USDA Agricultural Marketing Service, Weekly Grocery Store Turkey Feature Activity, dated October 30, 2026, national table and explanatory notes. This report is updated every week, so the numbers at that link may be newer than the ones here.

What did a turkey cost last Thanksgiving?

The American Farm Bureau Federation does its own count each year. Its 2025 survey, released November 19, 2025, says: "The average price for a 16-pound frozen turkey is $21.50. That is $1.34 per pound, down more than 16% from last year."

Its volunteer shoppers checked prices the first week of November 2025. They looked for the best prices without special coupons or combined purchase deals.

That is a different count from the USDA ad report. So do not line the two numbers up as if they came from the same place.

Source: American Farm Bureau Federation, Cost of Thanksgiving dinner declines, November 19, 2025.

Why do I keep hearing that turkey prices are up?

Quick answer: the wholesale price is up, and it is not the same as the store ad price.

The USDA's Economic Research Service tracks the wholesale price of frozen hen turkeys. In its October 16, 2026 outlook, it expects them to average 175.5 cents a pound in 2026. In 2025, the average was 135.8 cents.

That is not the tag at your store. Stores set their own prices.

The two can move apart. Last year, the Farm Bureau wrote: "While the wholesale price for fresh turkey is up from 2024, grocery stores are featuring Thanksgiving deals and attempting to draw consumer demand back to turkey, leading to lower retail prices for a holiday bird."

Is there a turkey shortage this year?

Quick answer: USDA's numbers do not point to one. It expects more turkey this year, not less.

Bird flu is back. USDA counted 11 cases in commercial turkey flocks in September, in Minnesota, Wisconsin, North Dakota and South Dakota. They affected 544,900 birds. August added 2 more cases and 119,300 birds.

Still, USDA raised its 2026 forecast to 5,134 million pounds of turkey. In 2025 it was 4,844 million pounds.

So why is the wholesale price higher? USDA raised its price forecast for the last three months of 2026. It said more trading going into fall buying season has "signaled ongoing demand strength."

Sources: USDA Economic Research Service, Livestock, Dairy, and Poultry Outlook: October 2026 (LDP-M-388), October 16, 2026, turkey section and red meat and poultry forecasts table; American Farm Bureau Federation, Cost of Thanksgiving dinner declines, November 19, 2025.

What can I do instead of chasing a turkey ad?

Quick answer: get your turkey from a farm near you, at a price the farmer tells you to your face.

  • Ask around. At your farmers market, ask who raises turkeys.
  • Call the farm. Ask how the birds live and what they eat.
  • Ask the price, and why. A farmer can tell you what goes into it.
  • No farm nearby? Ask the farmers you meet who they know.

I will not promise you a farm turkey costs less. Some cost more. What you get is a price from a person you can look in the eye.

Do one thing this week. Call one farm near you and ask about a turkey.
